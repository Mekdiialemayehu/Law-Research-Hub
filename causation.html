CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Causation?

Causation refers to the legal connection between a person's conduct and a prohibited result in criminal law.

It answers the question:

Did the accused's conduct cause the prohibited result?

Causation is particularly important in offences that require a specific consequence, such as death, injury, or property damage.

For these offences, proving that the accused performed an act may not be enough. The prosecution may also need to establish that the act legally caused the required result.

Two Parts of Causation

  • Factual causation.
  • Legal causation.

The precise rules differ between offences and legal systems.

CARD 2 · CRIMINAL LIABILITY

Causation as an Element of Criminal Liability

Not every criminal offence requires proof of causation.

A conduct-based offence may be established by proving that the prohibited conduct occurred.

However, where an offence requires a particular result, the prosecution generally must establish a sufficient connection between the accused's conduct and that result.

For a result-based offence, proving the act alone may not be enough. The prosecution must also establish the required causal connection.

For example, if a person is charged with an offence requiring proof that they caused another person's death, the prosecution must establish the necessary causal connection between the accused's conduct and the death.

CARD 3 · FACTUAL CAUSATION

Factual Causation

Factual causation asks whether the accused's conduct was a necessary cause of the result.

A common way of expressing the test is the "but for" test.

Would the result have occurred but for the accused's conduct?

If the result would not have occurred without the accused's conduct, factual causation may be established.

However, factual causation is usually only the first stage of the analysis.

CARD 4 · BUT FOR TEST

The "But For" Test

The "but for" test is commonly used to establish factual causation.

The court asks whether the prohibited result would have occurred but for the accused's conduct.

Example

A person pushes another individual, who falls and suffers an injury.

But for the push, would the injury have occurred?

If the answer is no, factual causation may be established.

The test is not appropriate for every possible factual situation, and courts may use different approaches where multiple causes are involved.

CARD 5 · LEGAL CAUSATION

Legal Causation

Legal causation asks whether the accused's conduct is sufficiently connected to the result for criminal responsibility to arise.

Even when factual causation is established, the law may not automatically treat the accused as legally responsible for every consequence that follows.

The court may therefore consider whether the connection between the conduct and result was legally sufficient.

Legal causation is sometimes described as proximate causation or the requirement that the accused's conduct remain an operating and substantial cause, depending on the jurisdiction and offence.
CARD 6 · COMPARISON

Factual and Legal Causation Compared

Factual Causation Legal Causation
Asks whether the accused's conduct actually contributed to the result. Asks whether the conduct is sufficiently connected to the result for legal responsibility.
Commonly uses the "but for" test. Considers whether the causal connection is legally sufficient.
Focuses on the factual connection. Focuses on the legal significance of that connection.
Usually comes first. Usually follows factual causation.
Both forms of causation may need to be established in result-based offences.
CARD 7 · MULTIPLE CAUSES

Multiple and Concurrent Causes

Sometimes a result has more than one cause.

For example, an injury or death may result from a combination of:

  • The accused's conduct.
  • Another person's actions.
  • A pre-existing condition.
  • Medical treatment.
  • Environmental circumstances.

The existence of other contributing factors does not automatically eliminate criminal responsibility.

Concurrent Causes

Concurrent causes occur when two or more events contribute to the same result.

The court may need to determine whether the accused's conduct was a substantial or legally significant cause of the result.

CARD 8 · INTERVENING ACTS

Intervening Acts

An intervening act is an event that occurs after the accused's conduct and may affect the causal connection between the conduct and the final result.

Intervening events may involve:

  • Another person's actions.
  • Medical treatment.
  • The victim's conduct.
  • Natural events.
  • Unexpected circumstances.

The court may need to decide whether the intervening event is sufficiently independent or significant to break the chain of legal causation.

Novus actus interveniens means a new intervening act that may affect the causal chain.
CARD 9 · BREAKING THE CHAIN

Breaking the Chain of Causation

The chain of causation may be broken where an intervening event is sufficiently independent and significant that the accused's conduct can no longer fairly be treated as the legal cause of the result.

However, not every intervening event breaks causation.

Courts consider the circumstances as a whole.

Acts of Third Parties

The conduct of another person may sometimes intervene between the accused's conduct and the final result.

The court may consider:

  • Whether the intervention was foreseeable.
  • Whether the original conduct remained an operating cause.
  • How independent the intervening act was.
  • Whether the intervening conduct was sufficiently significant to break the causal chain.
CARD 10 · MEDICAL TREATMENT

Medical Treatment

Medical treatment may sometimes occur between the accused's conduct and the final result.

If the accused causes an injury and the victim later receives medical treatment, the court may need to determine whether the accused's original conduct remains a legally sufficient cause of the eventual result.

The fact that medical treatment contributed to the outcome does not automatically break the chain of causation.

However, exceptionally independent or extraordinary treatment may raise different legal questions.

The precise test depends on the circumstances and applicable law.

CARD 11 · VICTIM & VULNERABILITY

Victim's Conduct and Pre-Existing Conditions

Victim's Conduct

The conduct of the victim can sometimes become relevant to causation.

The court may consider whether the victim's response was a reasonably foreseeable reaction to the accused's conduct or whether it was so extraordinary and independent that it breaks the causal chain.

Pre-Existing Conditions

A victim may have a pre-existing physical or medical condition that affects the outcome.

The accused may still be legally responsible where their conduct activates or contributes to the harmful result.

The existence of a pre-existing condition does not automatically eliminate causation.
CARD 12 · EGGSHELL SKULL RULE

The Eggshell Skull Rule

The eggshell skull rule reflects the principle that a person who unlawfully causes harm may be responsible for the consequences even if the victim was unusually vulnerable.

For example, if an apparently minor act causes unusually serious injury because the victim has a particular vulnerability, the accused may still be responsible for the resulting harm where the applicable legal requirements are satisfied.

The rule is not a universal answer to every causation question. Its application depends on the relevant legal system and offence.
CARD 13 · HOMICIDE

Causation in Homicide

Causation is especially important in homicide offences.

Where the prosecution alleges that the accused caused a person's death, it generally must establish:

  1. The accused performed the relevant conduct.
  2. The conduct was a factual cause of the death.
  3. The conduct remained a legally sufficient cause of the death.
  4. Any other required elements of the offence are established.

The specific requirements depend on whether the charge is murder, manslaughter, or another homicide offence.

CARD 14 · NON-FATAL OFFENCES

Causation in Non-Fatal Offences

Causation can also be important in offences involving injury or other prohibited consequences.

For example, where an offence requires proof that the accused caused bodily harm, the prosecution must establish the required connection between the accused's conduct and the harm.

The level and nature of harm required depend on the particular offence.

Example

If a person deliberately pushes another person and causes a serious injury, the prosecution may need to establish both that the push occurred and that the push legally caused the injury.

CARD 15 · ACTUS REUS & MENS REA

Causation, Actus Reus and Mens Rea

Causation and Actus Reus

Causation is closely connected to actus reus.

Actus reus = the relevant physical conduct and required result.

Causation = the legal connection between the conduct and result.

Therefore, causation may be necessary to establish the actus reus of a result-based offence.

Causation and Mens Rea

Causation and mens rea are separate concepts.

Causation concerns the connection between conduct and result, while mens rea concerns the mental state required by the offence.

In a result-based offence, the prosecution may need to establish both the causal connection and the required mental state.

CARD 16 · EVIDENCE

Causation and Evidence

Courts may rely on different forms of evidence when determining causation.

  • Witness testimony.
  • Medical evidence.
  • Forensic evidence.
  • Expert testimony.
  • Photographs and recordings.
  • Physical evidence.
  • Digital records.
  • Scientific evidence.

Experts may sometimes assist courts in understanding technical or medical questions concerning causation.

Ultimately, the court applies the relevant legal test to the evidence.

Burden of Proof

Where causation is an element of the offence, the prosecution generally must prove the necessary causal connection according to the applicable criminal standard.

CARD 17 · FORESEEABILITY

Causation and Foreseeability

Foreseeability may be relevant to legal causation in certain circumstances.

The court may consider whether the result or intervening event was sufficiently connected to the accused's conduct.

Foreseeability is not a universal test for causation.

Different offences and legal systems use different approaches to determine whether a causal connection remains legally sufficient.

Causation and Coincidence

Where a crime requires particular conduct and a particular result, the accused's conduct must be connected to that result according to the applicable causation rules.

CARD 18 · PRACTICAL EXAMPLE

Example of Causation

Imagine that a person deliberately pushes another person, causing them to fall and suffer a serious injury.

The prosecution may need to establish:

  1. The accused pushed the victim.
  2. The victim suffered the required injury.
  3. The injury would not have occurred in the same way without the push.
  4. The push remained a legally sufficient cause of the injury.
  5. The other elements of the offence are established.
If another event occurred after the push, the court might need to determine whether that event broke the chain of causation.
CARD 19 · DEFENCES & APPEALS

Causation on Defence and Appeal

Causation and Defences

Causation may become relevant when the accused argues that another event caused the prohibited result.

The defence may argue that:

  • The accused's conduct did not actually cause the result.
  • Another event was the true cause.
  • An intervening act broke the causal chain.
  • The prosecution failed to establish the required connection.

Causation on Appeal

An appellate court may consider whether a lower court correctly applied the law of causation.

An appeal may raise issues such as factual causation, legal causation, intervening acts, or whether the evidence was sufficient to support the causal finding.

CARD 20 · LEGAL SYSTEMS

Different Legal Systems

The concept of causation exists across many legal systems, although terminology and legal tests differ.

Common law systems often distinguish between:

  • Factual causation.
  • Legal causation.

Other legal systems may use concepts such as:

  • Causal connection.
  • Objective attribution.
  • Causation in fact.
  • Legal attribution of a result.
The precise rules must therefore be understood within the relevant legal system and offence.
CARD 21 · WHY IT MATTERS

Why Causation Matters

Causation helps:

  • Connect criminal conduct with prohibited results.
  • Prevent people from being held responsible for unrelated consequences.
  • Identify when an accused legally caused harm.
  • Establish responsibility in result-based offences.
  • Evaluate intervening events.
  • Distinguish factual connection from legal responsibility.
  • Provide a structured method for analyzing criminal liability.
Without causation rules, a person could potentially be held responsible for consequences that their conduct did not legally cause.
CARD 22 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

Causation is the legal connection between an accused person's conduct and a prohibited result.

A simple way to remember the concept is:

Causation = Did the accused's conduct legally cause the result?

Two Main Questions

Factual causation: Did the result occur because of the accused's conduct?

Legal causation: Is the connection between the conduct and result sufficient for criminal responsibility?

Causation may become complicated when several factors contribute to a result, when another person intervenes, when medical treatment is involved, or when the victim has a pre-existing condition.

Understanding causation is essential because criminal law must establish not only that a prohibited result occurred, but also that the accused's conduct was sufficiently responsible for that result under the law.
