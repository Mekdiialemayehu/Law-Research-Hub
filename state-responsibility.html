State Responsibility

Understanding when states are legally responsible under international law for conduct that breaches their international obligations.

What Is State Responsibility?

State responsibility is the legal principle that a state may be held responsible when its conduct breaches an obligation under international law.

In human-rights law, a state may incur responsibility when government authorities fail to respect, protect, or fulfil applicable human-rights obligations.

Basic Formula:

Attribution + Breach = Internationally Wrongful Act

The key question is: When can a state be legally responsible for conduct that violates international law or human rights?

State Responsibility and Human Rights

States are the primary duty-bearers under international human-rights law.

When a state accepts human-rights obligations through a treaty or another source of international law, it must comply with those obligations.

A state may be responsible for:

  • Direct violations by government officials.
  • Failure to protect individuals when protection is required.
  • Failure to investigate serious violations.
  • Failure to provide effective remedies.
  • Other attributable acts or omissions.
State responsibility can therefore involve both what the government does and what the government fails to do.
Card 2 of 15

Internationally Wrongful Acts

The central concept in state responsibility is the internationally wrongful act.

It generally exists when:

  1. The conduct is attributable to the state.
  2. The conduct breaches an international obligation binding on that state.

The conduct may involve either an act or an omission.

A state may therefore be responsible not only for what its authorities did, but also for what they unlawfully failed to do.
Card 3 of 15

Attribution

Attribution asks whether particular conduct is legally treated as conduct of the state.

States act through people and institutions. Conduct may therefore be attributable to the state when performed by:

  • Government ministries.
  • Police officers.
  • Military personnel.
  • Courts.
  • Legislatures.
  • Administrative officials.
  • Other state organs.
Attribution question: Is the conduct legally considered conduct of the state?
Card 4 of 15

State Organs and Unlawful Officials

Conduct of state organs is generally attributable to the state.

State organs can perform legislative, executive, judicial, or other governmental functions.

Examples include:

  • A police officer acting in an official capacity.
  • A ministry issuing an unlawful decision.
  • A court violating an international obligation.
  • A legislature adopting legislation inconsistent with a treaty.

State responsibility may remain even when an official exceeds authority or acts contrary to domestic instructions.

A state cannot necessarily avoid international responsibility simply by saying that an official disobeyed government instructions.
Card 5 of 15

Private Individuals and Governmental Authority

A state is not automatically responsible for every wrongful act committed by a private individual.

However, international law recognizes circumstances in which private conduct can engage state responsibility.

This may occur where:

  • The state directed the conduct.
  • The state controlled the conduct in the relevant circumstances.
  • The person or entity exercised governmental authority.
  • The state later adopted the conduct as its own.
  • The state separately breached an obligation by failing to protect individuals.
Mere private conduct is not automatically attributable to the state. The applicable legal test must be satisfied.
Card 6 of 15

Acts and Omissions

Act

State responsibility can arise from affirmative government conduct.

Example: A government official unlawfully arrests and detains a person.

Omission

Responsibility can also arise when authorities fail to take action required by international law.

Example: Authorities fail to take required protective or investigative measures despite having an applicable legal obligation.
State responsibility therefore covers both positive conduct and unlawful failures to act.
Card 7 of 15

Failure to Protect and Investigate

Human-rights law can impose positive obligations on states.

A state may therefore incur responsibility when it fails to take reasonable measures required to protect individuals from serious and foreseeable threats.

Positive obligations may also require:

  • Effective laws.
  • Preventive measures.
  • Investigations into serious violations.
  • Accountability mechanisms.
  • Effective remedies.
A state is not automatically responsible for every harmful act committed by a private person. The applicable legal threshold must be satisfied.
Card 8 of 15

Legislation and Judicial Decisions

Legislation

A state may incur responsibility through legislation that conflicts with an applicable international obligation.

A government generally cannot avoid international responsibility simply by pointing to its domestic law.

Judicial Decisions

National courts are part of the state for purposes of international responsibility.

A final judicial decision that breaches an applicable international obligation may therefore engage state responsibility.

Legislative, executive, and judicial actions can all potentially engage the international responsibility of the state.
Card 9 of 15

Breach, Timing, and Continuing Violations

Breach of an International Obligation

Attribution alone is not enough. The conduct must also breach an applicable international legal obligation.

Timing

The obligation must generally be binding on the state when the relevant conduct occurs.

Continuing Breach

Some violations continue over time.

Example: If a person is unlawfully detained, the violation may continue for as long as the unlawful detention continues.
Card 10 of 15

Treaties, Equality, and Human-Rights Obligations

States can accept international obligations through treaties and other recognized sources of international law.

Important human-rights treaties include:

  • International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.
  • International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights.
  • European Convention on Human Rights.
  • Convention on the Rights of the Child.
  • Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women.
  • Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.

State responsibility can also arise from breaches involving equality and non-discrimination obligations.

Discriminatory laws, decisions, unequal enforcement, or failures to protect against serious discrimination can potentially engage state responsibility.
Card 11 of 15

State Responsibility and Rights Restrictions

When a state restricts a qualified right, the restriction must comply with the applicable legal framework.

A court may examine:

  1. Was there a legal basis?
  2. Was there a legitimate aim?
  3. Was the measure necessary?
  4. Was it proportionate?

If the restriction breaches an applicable international obligation, state responsibility may arise.

Absolute Rights

Responsibility may arise particularly clearly when state authorities violate an absolute human-rights prohibition, such as the prohibition of torture.

Card 12 of 15

Emergencies and Circumstances Affecting Wrongfulness

States may sometimes exercise special powers during emergencies. However, emergency powers do not automatically eliminate international obligations.

International law recognizes limited doctrines such as:

  • Consent.
  • Self-defence.
  • Countermeasures under strict conditions.
  • Force majeure.
  • Distress.
  • Necessity.
These doctrines are narrowly defined and do not provide a general excuse for human-rights violations.

Where treaties permit derogation during qualifying emergencies, the state must still satisfy the treaty's specific requirements.

Card 13 of 15

Consequences of State Responsibility

When a state is internationally responsible for a wrongful act, international law may require consequences appropriate to the case.

Possible consequences include:

  • Cessation of continuing wrongful conduct.
  • Reparation.
  • Restitution.
  • Compensation.
  • Satisfaction.
  • Guarantees or assurances of non-repetition where appropriate.
The purpose of these consequences is to address the legal effects of the wrongful act and, where appropriate, repair the injury caused.
Card 14 of 15

Reparation, Remedies, and Individual Responsibility

Reparation

Reparation may take several forms depending on the circumstances.

  • Restitution: restoring the situation that existed before the wrongful act where possible and appropriate.
  • Compensation: financial reparation for legally recognized injury.
  • Satisfaction: appropriate non-monetary forms of redress.

Individual Responsibility

State responsibility should be distinguished from individual criminal responsibility.

A state may incur international responsibility for torture committed by government officials, while the individual officials may also face personal criminal responsibility under applicable law.

State Responsibility: The Complete Framework

What act or omission occurred?
2. Determine Attribution
Is the conduct legally attributable to the state?
3. Identify the Obligation
What international legal obligation applies?
4. Determine Breach
Did the attributed conduct violate that obligation?
5. Consider Special Circumstances
Do any applicable circumstances affect wrongfulness?
6. Determine Consequences
What remedies, cessation, or reparation may be required?
