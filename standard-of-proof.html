STANDARD OF PROOF

CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is the Standard of Proof?

The standard of proof refers to the level of certainty or strength of evidence that a party must satisfy in order to prove a claim or establish a particular fact in a legal proceeding.

It answers the question:

How convincing does the evidence need to be?

The standard of proof is different from the burden of proof.

The burden of proof concerns who is responsible for proving an issue, while the standard of proof concerns how much proof is required.

Different types of legal proceedings can use different standards of proof.

CARD 2 · CRIMINAL CASES

Standard of Proof in Criminal Cases

In many common law legal systems, the prosecution must prove the accused's guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

This is a high standard of proof and reflects the serious consequences that can result from a criminal conviction.

For example, if a person is charged with robbery, the prosecution must provide sufficient evidence to establish the elements of the offence to the required criminal standard.

If the evidence leaves a reasonable doubt about whether the accused committed the offence, the prosecution has not satisfied the standard of proof.
CARD 3 · BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT

Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

Beyond a reasonable doubt is a standard commonly associated with criminal proceedings.

It does not require absolute or mathematical certainty.

Instead, the evidence must be sufficiently convincing that the required level of reasonable doubt concerning guilt has been overcome.

The precise way this standard is explained and applied can differ between legal systems.

The important principle is that a person should not be convicted simply because they might have committed an offence or because guilt seems possible.

CARD 4 · CIVIL CASES

Standard of Proof in Civil Cases

Civil cases generally use a lower standard of proof than criminal cases.

In many common law systems, the usual civil standard is the balance of probabilities.

Under this standard, the court asks whether a particular fact is more likely than not to be true.

For example, a customer claiming that a company breached a contract generally needs to establish the facts supporting the claim on the balance of probabilities.

Balance of probabilities = The relevant fact is more likely than not to be true.
CARD 5 · BALANCE OF PROBABILITIES

Balance of Probabilities

The balance of probabilities is commonly used in civil proceedings.

It can be understood as requiring the court to decide whether the relevant fact is more probable than not.

For example, if a court concludes that an event was more likely than not to have occurred, the standard may be satisfied.

This is sometimes explained as requiring a probability greater than 50%, although courts generally consider the evidence as a whole rather than treating cases as mathematical calculations.

The court evaluates the evidence as a whole to determine whether the relevant fact is more likely than not to have occurred.
CARD 6 · CRIMINAL VS. CIVIL

Criminal and Civil Standards Compared

Criminal Cases

  • Commonly use "beyond a reasonable doubt."
  • Higher standard of proof.
  • Prosecution generally must prove guilt.
  • Reflects the serious consequences of criminal conviction.

Civil Cases

  • Commonly use "balance of probabilities."
  • Lower standard of proof.
  • Claimant generally must prove the claim.
  • Primarily concerns civil rights, obligations, or compensation.

The exact terminology and legal rules can vary between jurisdictions.

CARD 7 · WHY DIFFERENT STANDARDS?

Why Different Standards Exist

Criminal and civil proceedings have different purposes and consequences.

Criminal Proceedings

A criminal conviction can result in serious penalties imposed by the State.

Because of these consequences, criminal proceedings commonly require a higher standard of proof.

Civil Proceedings

Civil proceedings generally concern disputes involving matters such as contracts, property, compensation, or other private rights and obligations.

The difference in standards reflects the different purposes and consequences of criminal and civil proceedings.
CARD 8 · EXAMPLE

Standard of Proof in Practice

Imagine that two people are involved in a dispute.

Criminal Case

One person is accused of stealing another person's property.

The prosecution must prove the accused's guilt according to the applicable criminal standard.

Civil Case

The owner may separately bring a claim seeking compensation for the loss.

The civil court may determine whether the claimant has established the relevant facts according to the civil standard.

The same underlying event can therefore give rise to different legal proceedings using different standards of proof.
CARD 9 · BURDEN VS. STANDARD

Standard of Proof and Burden of Proof

Burden of Proof

Identifies who has the responsibility to prove an issue.

Standard of Proof

Identifies how convincing the evidence must be.

Burden of proof = Who must prove it?

Standard of proof = How strongly must it be proved?

A party may therefore have the burden of proving a claim but still fail because the evidence does not meet the required standard.

CARD 10 · EVIDENCE

Standard of Proof and Evidence

The standard of proof is closely connected to the law of evidence.

Evidence can include:

  • Witness testimony.
  • Documents.
  • Physical evidence.
  • Digital records.
  • Expert evidence.
  • Photographs and recordings.

The court evaluates the evidence according to the rules of the relevant legal system.

The quantity of evidence is not necessarily what matters most. A small amount of reliable evidence may be more persuasive than a large amount of weak or unreliable evidence.
CARD 11 · PRESUMPTION OF INNOCENCE

Standard of Proof and Presumption of Innocence

The standard of proof in criminal cases is closely connected to the presumption of innocence.

A person accused of a criminal offence is generally treated as innocent unless the prosecution establishes guilt according to the required legal standard.

This places an important responsibility on the prosecution and prevents criminal conviction from being based merely on accusation or suspicion.

Human Rights Protection

Article 11 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights recognises that a person charged with a penal offence should be presumed innocent until proved guilty according to law.

CARD 12 · DIFFERENT ISSUES & APPEALS

Standards for Different Issues and on Appeal

Different Legal Issues

Not every legal issue within a case necessarily operates in exactly the same way.

The law of a particular jurisdiction may establish different standards or burdens for specific issues, especially where particular defences, presumptions, or legal procedures are involved.

Standard of Proof on Appeal

An appellate court may consider whether the lower court applied the correct legal standard when making its decision.

For example, if a trial court applied the wrong standard of proof in a criminal case, that may constitute a significant legal error.

The precise approach taken by an appellate court depends on the legal system, the type of error, and the rules governing appeals.
CARD 13 · DIFFERENT SYSTEMS & IMPORTANCE

Different Legal Systems

The standards and terminology used in legal proceedings differ between countries.

Common law systems frequently use concepts such as:

  • Beyond a reasonable doubt.
  • Balance of probabilities.

Other legal systems may use different terminology or structures for evaluating proof.

The meaning and application of a particular standard must always be understood within the relevant legal system.

Why the Standard of Proof Matters

  • Establish how convincing evidence must be.
  • Protect individuals from decisions based on insufficient evidence.
  • Provide consistency in judicial decision-making.
  • Distinguish between suspicion and established facts.
  • Support fairness in criminal and civil proceedings.
  • Protect the presumption of innocence in criminal cases.
CARD 14 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

The standard of proof is the level of evidence or certainty required for a party to establish a claim or fact in a legal proceeding.

A simple way to remember the concept is:

Burden of proof = Who must prove it?

Standard of proof = How convincing must the proof be?

In many common law systems, criminal cases generally use the beyond a reasonable doubt standard, while civil cases generally use the balance of probabilities standard.

Understanding the standard of proof is essential because courts must determine not only who has the responsibility to prove a case, but also whether the evidence is strong enough to satisfy the law.
