Children's Right to Education

Understanding the legal protection of education for children through international, African, and Ethiopian human-rights law.

Core legal question: What legal duties do states have to ensure that every child can access, remain in, and benefit from education without discrimination?
01 · Definition

What is the Right to Education for Children?

The right to education is a human right held by every child. International human-rights law recognizes education not simply as a service provided by the state, but as a right connected to human dignity, development, equality, participation, and the full realization of other rights.

For children, the right to education includes more than the existence of schools. It concerns whether children can access education, attend regularly, remain in school, receive appropriate teaching, and develop their abilities in an environment that respects their dignity and rights.

The Convention on the Rights of the Child also makes clear that education should be directed toward the development of the child's personality, talents, mental and physical abilities, respect for human rights, cultural identity, equality, peace, and responsible participation in society.

The key idea is simple: every child has a right to education that is accessible, inclusive, protective, and directed toward the child's full development.
02 · Why It Matters

Why is Children's Education a Human-Rights Issue?

Education is closely connected to the development of a child's personality, abilities, confidence, participation, and understanding of human rights. The Committee on the Rights of the Child has emphasized that the aims of education are closely connected to the child's inherent dignity and equal rights.

The right is especially important because children depend on adults and public institutions to create conditions in which they can actually attend school. A child may have a legal right to education while poverty, discrimination, conflict, unsafe schools, disability, distance, child labour, or other barriers make that right difficult to exercise in practice.

Children's education is therefore not only an educational policy question. It can also involve equality, dignity, protection, freedom from discrimination, the best interests of the child, and the responsibilities of governments to make rights effective.

03 · International Legal Framework

International Protection

CRC · Articles 28–29

Convention on the Rights of the Child

Article 28 recognizes the child's right to education and requires States Parties to work toward compulsory and available free primary education, encourage different forms of secondary education, make higher education accessible on the basis of capacity, provide educational and vocational guidance, encourage regular attendance, and reduce school dropout.

Article 29 addresses the aims of education. It states that education should develop the child's personality, talents, and mental and physical abilities to their fullest potential and should promote respect for human rights, cultural identity, tolerance, equality, peace, and the natural environment.

CRC · Article 2

Non-Discrimination

Children's rights must be protected without discrimination on the grounds listed in the Convention. The right to education therefore applies to every child, including children who may face additional barriers because of disability, sex, language, social origin, nationality, economic circumstances, or other status.

The Committee on the Rights of the Child has emphasized that children must be able to participate in education without discrimination and that particular attention should be given to children who experience greater barriers to access.

ICESCR · Article 13

International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights

Article 13 recognizes the right of everyone to education. The Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights has explained that the right contains four essential and interrelated features: availability, accessibility, acceptability, and adaptability.

The Committee also explains that states have obligations to respect, protect, and fulfil these elements of the right to education, including taking positive measures to develop educational systems and protect children from barriers created by third parties.

Read the Covenant →

CRC · General Comment No. 1

The Aims of Education

General Comment No. 1 explains that education should be child-centred and connected to the child's dignity and development. Education is not simply about transferring information; it should help children develop their abilities, identity, respect for human rights, and capacity to participate responsibly in society.

04 · African Human-Rights Law

The African Legal Framework

African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child · Article 11

Right to Education

Article 11 provides that every child has the right to education. It requires States Parties to take appropriate measures to achieve the full realization of that right, including providing free and compulsory basic education, progressively making secondary education free and accessible, encouraging attendance, and reducing dropout rates.

The Charter also requires special measures for female, gifted, and disadvantaged children to ensure equal access to education.

Read Article 11 →

African Charter · Article 1

State Obligations

The African Children's Charter requires States Parties to recognize the rights contained in the Charter and to adopt legislative and other measures necessary to give effect to those rights.

This is important because the right to education is not fulfilled simply by recognizing it in law. States must also develop institutions, policies, and practical measures capable of making the right real.

African Children's Charter · Article 11

Protection for Girls and Disadvantaged Children

The African Children's Charter expressly requires special measures for female and disadvantaged children to ensure equal access to education. It also requires states to make it possible for children who become pregnant before completing their education to continue their education according to their individual ability.

Ethiopia · Regional obligations

Ethiopia and the African Children's Charter

Ethiopia ratified the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child in 2002 through Proclamation No. 283/2002. The Charter therefore forms part of the regional legal framework relevant to children's rights and education in Ethiopia.

05 · Understanding the Right

Four Dimensions of Education

Human-rights law provides a useful framework for asking whether education exists as a genuine right rather than merely as a formal promise.

Availability

Are there enough functioning schools, teachers, classrooms, learning materials, and other educational resources for children?

Accessibility

Can children actually reach and enter education without discrimination or unreasonable economic, physical, geographic, or social barriers?

Acceptability

Is the content and quality of education appropriate, relevant, culturally respectful, and consistent with children's dignity and human rights?

Adaptability

Can education respond to children's different needs and changing social circumstances, including the needs of disadvantaged and vulnerable children?

06 · Featured Case

Osman v. the United Kingdom

European Court of Human Rights · 1998

A landmark case on state responsibility and protection of a child in a school environment

Osman v. the United Kingdom concerned Ahmet Osman, a pupil at Homerton House School, and his family. School authorities became concerned about the conduct of a teacher who had developed an inappropriate attachment to Ahmet and communicated their concerns to the police and educational authorities.

The case arose under Article 2 of the European Convention on Human Rights, concerning the right to life. The applicants argued that the authorities had failed to take adequate steps in response to warning signs concerning the danger posed to Ahmet and his family.

The European Court examined the positive obligation of the state to take reasonable preventive measures where authorities knew, or ought reasonably to have known, of a real and immediate risk to the life of an identified individual. The Court ultimately found no violation of Article 2 on the particular facts of the case.

Why is it relevant?

Osman is not a case about the right to education itself. Its importance for children's education lies elsewhere: it illustrates that children's rights in school cannot be separated from the state's wider responsibilities to protect children and create safe conditions in which they can participate in school life.

The case therefore raises an important human-rights question. A child's education cannot be meaningful if the child is not adequately protected from serious risks within the institutions responsible for their schooling.

Osman also demonstrates an important principle of human-rights law: state responsibility can include positive duties to take reasonable protective measures when authorities know about serious risks.

07 · Understanding the Problem

Barriers to Children's Education

A legal right can exist while children continue to experience serious obstacles to using it. Human-rights research therefore asks what prevents children from entering school, remaining there, and receiving education on an equal basis.

Poverty
Poverty can make the indirect costs of schooling difficult for families to manage and can increase pressure on children to contribute to household income or work.
Child labour
Work can interfere with school attendance, study time, and completion of education, especially where children are expected to perform substantial economic or domestic responsibilities.
Conflict and displacement
Conflict can damage schools, interrupt education, displace families, and create conditions in which children cannot safely or consistently attend school.
Disability
Children with disabilities may face physical, communication, institutional, or discriminatory barriers when schools are not designed to provide inclusive education.
Distance and geography
Children living far from schools may face difficulties reaching education, particularly where transportation and nearby educational facilities are limited.
Discrimination
Children may experience unequal treatment based on sex, disability, language, social origin, nationality, economic circumstances, or other status.
Unsafe schools
Violence, abuse, harassment, degrading treatment, or unsafe physical conditions can undermine children's ability to learn and remain in school.
Dropout and exclusion
Children may leave school or be pushed out because of poverty, repeated disruption, discriminatory practices, family pressures, pregnancy, disability, conflict, or inadequate support.
08 · Ethiopia

Children's Right to Education in Ethiopia

Ethiopia's constitutional framework provides several protections relevant to children's education, including equality, protection from harmful practices, children's rights, and government responsibilities concerning access to education.

Article 25 · Right to Equality

The Constitution provides that all persons are equal before the law and are entitled to equal protection without discrimination, including discrimination on grounds of sex. This general equality guarantee is relevant to children's equal access to education.

Article 36 · Rights of Children

Article 36 recognizes specific rights of children. It protects children from exploitative practices and from work that may be hazardous or harmful to their education, health, or well-being.

The Constitution also provides that children have the right to be free from corporal punishment and cruel or inhuman treatment in schools and other institutions responsible for their care.

Article 90 · Social Objectives

Article 90 states that, to the extent the country's resources permit, policies shall aim to provide all Ethiopians access to public health and education. It further states that education shall be provided free from religious influence, political partisanship, or cultural prejudices.

Article 41 · Economic, Social and Cultural Rights

The Constitution also directs the state to allocate ever-increasing resources to provide public health, education, and other social services within the country's resources. This creates an important framework for examining the state's responsibility to progressively expand access to education.

Why the Ethiopian context matters

Ethiopia's legal framework demonstrates that children's education is connected to several different constitutional principles. The key research question is therefore not only whether the law recognizes education, but whether children across different circumstances can actually benefit from that right.

Ethiopia's regional commitment

Ethiopia ratified the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child in 2002. Article 11 of that Charter requires states to take measures toward the full realization of children's right to education, including free and compulsory basic education and special measures for female and disadvantaged children.

09 · Legal Analysis

Law on Paper vs. Education in Practice

Children's education provides a clear example of the difference between having a legal right and being able to exercise that right in everyday life.

Legal promise Practical question
Every child has a right to education. Are functioning educational institutions actually available to children?
Primary education should be free and compulsory. Can children attend without financial, geographic, or other barriers that make attendance unrealistic?
Education must be provided without discrimination. Do children with disabilities, girls, disadvantaged children, and minority groups receive equal access and support?
Children should be protected in schools. Are schools safe environments where children are protected from violence, abuse, degrading treatment, and other serious risks?
Education should develop the whole child. Does schooling promote dignity, abilities, human rights, tolerance, and meaningful participation rather than merely attendance?
States should reduce school dropout. What measures exist to help children remain in school when poverty, disability, conflict, family responsibilities, or other pressures interfere?
10 · Research Question

Think Like a Human-Rights Researcher

If every child has a right to education, what responsibility does a government have when poverty, discrimination, conflict, disability, or other barriers prevent children from actually enjoying that right?

A strong legal analysis should ask three things: What does the law promise? What prevents children from enjoying the right? What must the state do to make the right meaningful in practice?
