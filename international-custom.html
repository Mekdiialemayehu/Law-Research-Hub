INTERNATIONAL CUSTOM
● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ●
CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is International Custom?

International custom, or customary international law, is a source of international law that develops from a general practice of states that is accepted by them as legally required.

Unlike treaties, customary international law does not depend on a written agreement between states.

The identification of customary international law generally depends on two fundamental elements:

  • State practice.
  • Opinio juris.
State Practice + Opinio Juris → Customary International Law

The central question is: How can a legally binding international rule develop without a written treaty?

CARD 2 · TWO ELEMENTS

The Two Elements of Custom

For a customary rule to exist, both of the following elements generally need to be established.

Element Meaning
State Practice What states actually do through their governmental conduct.
Opinio Juris The belief that the practice is followed because international law requires or permits it.
Neither element should simply be assumed from the existence of the other. Practice and opinio juris are assessed separately, although evidence may sometimes be relevant to both.

A practice followed merely because it is convenient, traditional, or politically useful does not automatically become customary international law.

CARD 3 · STATE PRACTICE

What Is State Practice?

State practice refers to the actual conduct of states.

It can appear in many forms, including:

  • Government actions.
  • Diplomatic correspondence.
  • National legislation.
  • Executive decisions.
  • Military practices.
  • Statements by government officials.
  • Diplomatic protests.
  • National court decisions.
  • Conduct during international negotiations.
  • Positions taken before international organizations.
State practice must generally be assessed as a whole rather than by focusing on one isolated action.

Modern state practice is therefore much broader than traditional diplomatic behavior.

CARD 4 · GENERAL PRACTICE

General and Representative Practice

State practice must generally be sufficiently general to support the existence of a customary rule.

This does not necessarily mean that every state in the world must behave identically.

International courts consider factors such as:

  • The extent of the practice.
  • Its consistency.
  • Its significance.
  • Whether the practice is widespread and representative.
Widespread + Representative + Sufficiently Consistent Practice

The International Court of Justice has emphasized that customary international law requires sufficiently widespread and representative practice together with opinio juris.

CARD 5 · CONSISTENCY

Consistency of State Practice

State practice should generally demonstrate a sufficient degree of consistency.

If states repeatedly behave in completely contradictory ways, it may be difficult to establish the existence of a customary rule.

However, customary international law does not require perfect uniformity.

How Courts Assess It

Courts examine whether the practice, considered as a whole, is sufficiently consistent to support the claimed rule.

The existence of some contrary conduct does not necessarily destroy a customary rule. The overall pattern and legal significance of the practice matter.
CARD 6 · OPINIO JURIS

What Is Opinio Juris?

Opinio juris is the belief that a particular practice is followed because states consider themselves legally required, or legally entitled, to follow it.

This is the second fundamental element of customary international law.

The key distinction is:

State Practice = What States Do

Opinio Juris = Why They Do It as a Matter of Law

For example, states may regularly provide diplomatic privileges to foreign officials. The practice becomes relevant as customary international law when states follow it because they understand that international law requires or recognizes such treatment.

CARD 7 · PRACTICE ALONE

Practice Alone Is Not Enough

Repeated conduct does not automatically create customary international law.

States may follow a practice simply because it is:

  • Convenient.
  • Efficient.
  • Politically useful.
  • Based on courtesy or diplomatic tradition.
  • Morally or politically desirable.
The important question is not simply whether states behave in a particular way, but whether they regard that conduct as legally required or legally permitted under international law.

This is why opinio juris is essential to identifying customary international law.

CARD 8 · EVIDENCE

Evidence of Opinio Juris

Courts may examine many types of evidence when determining whether states believe a practice has legal significance.

  • Official government statements.
  • Diplomatic protests.
  • Legal opinions of governments.
  • Statements in international organizations.
  • National legislation.
  • National court decisions.
  • Treaty negotiations and conduct surrounding treaties.
  • Responses to alleged violations of a rule.
No single piece of evidence automatically establishes opinio juris. Courts generally assess the evidence as a whole.

Government Statements

Statements by government officials can help demonstrate a state's legal position, especially when supported by the state's conduct and other evidence.

CARD 9 · COURTS & INTERNATIONAL ORGANIZATIONS

Role of Courts and International Organizations

International courts help identify and apply customary international law.

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) frequently examines state practice and opinio juris when determining whether a customary rule exists.

The Court does not simply create a rule because it considers the rule desirable. It investigates whether the necessary elements of custom have been established.

International Organizations

International organizations can provide important evidence concerning state positions.

For example, state statements and voting patterns within the United Nations may provide evidence relevant to opinio juris.

A resolution adopted by an international organization does not automatically create customary international law. Its legal significance depends on the underlying evidence of practice and opinio juris.
CARD 10 · NORTH SEA CASES

North Sea Continental Shelf Cases

The North Sea Continental Shelf cases (1969) are among the most important authorities concerning customary international law.

The International Court of Justice considered whether a particular method of continental-shelf delimitation had become customary international law.

The Court concluded that the relevant rule had not been established as customary international law on the evidence before it.

Why the Cases Matter

The cases are particularly important because they demonstrate the need to examine both:

State Practice + Opinio Juris

They illustrate that a treaty provision or repeated conduct does not automatically establish a customary rule.

CARD 11 · TYPES OF CUSTOM

General, Particular & Regional Custom

General Custom

General customary international law consists of customary rules that apply broadly across the international community.

Particular Custom

International law can also recognize particular customary rules that apply among a more limited group of states.

Regional Custom

A customary rule can potentially develop among states within a particular region where the relevant states consistently follow a practice and regard it as legally required.

The existence of a particular or regional customary rule must be established through evidence of the relevant practice and opinio juris among the states concerned.
CARD 12 · TREATIES & CUSTOM

Relationship Between Treaties and Custom

Treaties and customary international law are separate sources of international law, but they can interact.

Treaty Custom
Created through international agreement and consent. Develops through state practice and opinio juris.
Generally binds states that become parties. May apply more broadly where the customary rule is established.
Usually contained in a written instrument. Does not require a written treaty.

Treaties Can Interact With Custom

  • A treaty may codify an existing customary rule.
  • A treaty may contribute to the development of custom.
  • A treaty provision may reflect a customary rule without automatically becoming customary itself.
CARD 13 · PERSISTENT OBJECTOR

The Persistent Objector Doctrine

A state that clearly and consistently objects to an emerging customary rule while that rule is developing may, under the applicable conditions, avoid being bound by that rule once it becomes established.

Requirements

The state generally must:

  1. Object while the rule is developing.
  2. Object clearly.
  3. Maintain its objection consistently.

A state cannot normally wait until a customary rule has already become established and then simply announce that it rejects the rule.

The persistent-objector doctrine is therefore narrowly applied.
CARD 14 · JUS COGENS

Customary Law and Jus Cogens

Jus cogens refers to peremptory norms of general international law from which no derogation is permitted.

Customary international law and jus cogens are closely connected, but they are not identical concepts.

Examples commonly recognized or strongly associated with jus cogens include prohibitions against:

  • Genocide.
  • Slavery.
  • Torture.
  • Aggression.

The persistent-objector doctrine does not provide a general escape from fundamental international legal rules having the status of peremptory norms.

Jus Cogens → No Derogation Permitted
CARD 15 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Why Customary International Law Matters

Customary international law is one of the central sources of international law because legally binding rules can develop without a written treaty.

It can play an important role in areas including:

  • Diplomatic relations.
  • The law of the sea.
  • International humanitarian law.
  • Human rights.
  • State responsibility.
  • International relations and sovereignty.
State Practice + Opinio Juris → Customary Rule → State Obligation → Possible State Responsibility

Key Principles

  • State practice: The actual conduct of states.
  • Opinio juris: The belief that conduct is legally required or permitted.
  • General practice: Practice should be sufficiently widespread and representative.
  • Consistency: The overall practice should be sufficiently consistent.
  • Evidence: Courts assess governmental conduct, statements, protests, legislation, decisions, and other relevant materials.
  • Persistent objector: A state may, under the applicable conditions, avoid a developing customary rule through clear and consistent objection.
  • Jus cogens: Peremptory norms cannot be rejected through ordinary persistent objection.
