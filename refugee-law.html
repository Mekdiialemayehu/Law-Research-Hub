CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Refugee Law?

Refugee law is the branch of international law concerned with protecting people who have fled their countries because they face persecution or other serious threats and cannot safely rely on the protection of their country of origin.

The central international framework is the 1951 Convention relating to the Status of Refugees and its 1967 Protocol.

Refugee law addresses questions such as:

  • Who qualifies as a refugee?
  • What protection must states provide?
  • Can a state return someone to a country where they face persecution or serious harm?
  • What rights do refugees have?
  • When can refugee protection be excluded or withdrawn?

CARD 2 · REFUGEE CONVENTION

The 1951 Refugee Convention

The 1951 Refugee Convention is the central international treaty concerning refugee protection.

It:

  • Defines who qualifies as a refugee.
  • Establishes important rights and protections.
  • Creates obligations for states parties.
  • Places limits on certain forms of expulsion.
  • Establishes the principle of non-refoulement.

The 1967 Protocol

The original Convention contained temporal and geographic limitations. The 1967 Protocol removed these limitations and expanded the refugee framework so that it could apply much more broadly.

Together, the 1951 Convention and 1967 Protocol form the foundation of the modern international refugee-protection system.
CARD 3 · REFUGEE DEFINITION

Who Is a Refugee?

Article 1A(2) of the 1951 Convention provides the central refugee definition.

In general terms, a refugee is a person who is outside their country and has a well-founded fear of persecution for a Convention reason, and who is unable or unwilling to obtain the protection of that country because of that fear.

The five traditional Convention grounds are:

  • Race.
  • Religion.
  • Nationality.
  • Membership of a particular social group.
  • Political opinion.
Outside Country + Well-Founded Fear + Persecution + Convention Ground + Lack of State Protection

For a person without a nationality, the relevant reference is generally their former habitual residence.

CARD 4 · KEY ELEMENTS

Elements of the Refugee Definition

A refugee claim can be analyzed through several connected questions.

Outside the Country ↓ Well-Founded Fear ↓ Persecution ↓ Convention Reason ↓ Unable or Unwilling to Obtain State Protection

Why Each Element Matters

  • The person generally must have crossed an international border.
  • The fear must be sufficiently supported by the circumstances.
  • The feared harm must amount to persecution or otherwise satisfy the applicable legal framework.
  • The persecution must generally be connected to a Convention ground.
  • The person must be unable or unwilling, because of the fear, to obtain protection from their country.
The precise interpretation of each element depends on international and national refugee law.
CARD 5 · IDPs & ASYLUM-SEEKERS

Refugees, IDPs & Asylum-Seekers

Refugee Internally Displaced Person
Has crossed an international border and requires international protection. Has been forced to leave home but remains within their own country.
May fall under international refugee law. Remains under the jurisdiction of their own state.

Asylum-Seeker

An asylum-seeker is a person who has sought international protection but whose claim to refugee status has not yet been finally determined.

Asylum-seeker = Person seeking protection.

Refugee = Person who meets the applicable refugee definition.

An asylum-seeker may ultimately be recognized as a refugee, receive another form of international protection, or have their claim rejected.

CARD 6 · WELL-FOUNDED FEAR

Well-Founded Fear & Persecution

Well-Founded Fear

The concept of well-founded fear generally contains both subjective and objective elements.

  • The person's genuine fear is relevant.
  • The surrounding circumstances must objectively support that fear.
  • Evidence may include threats, past persecution, country conditions, laws, policies, and violence directed at particular groups.

Persecution

Refugee protection is based on a fear of persecution, rather than simply any difficult or unpleasant circumstance.

Persecution generally involves sufficiently serious violations of fundamental rights or serious and sustained harm.

  • Severe physical abuse.
  • Arbitrary detention in appropriate circumstances.
  • Serious discriminatory measures.
  • Religious persecution.
  • Political persecution.
  • Other grave violations of fundamental rights.
Not every inconvenience, discrimination, or hardship amounts to persecution.
CARD 7 · CONVENTION GROUNDS

The Five Convention Grounds

The 1951 Convention identifies five principal grounds connecting persecution to refugee status.

  1. Race — persecution connected to racial or ethnic identity.
  2. Religion — persecution connected to religious belief, identity, practice, or lack of belief.
  3. Nationality — persecution connected to national, ethnic, linguistic, or related identity in appropriate circumstances.
  4. Particular Social Group — certain groups defined by characteristics fundamental to identity or that members cannot reasonably be expected to change, among approaches recognized in refugee law.
  5. Political Opinion — persecution connected to actual or sometimes attributed political opinions.

Political Opinion

Political persecution may involve opposition to government policies, political activism, participation in political organizations, or criticism of authorities.

A refugee claim generally requires a connection between the feared persecution and at least one Convention ground.
CARD 8 · STATE PROTECTION

State Protection & Non-State Actors

A central part of refugee law is whether the person can obtain effective protection from their country of origin.

Persecution does not always have to be committed directly by the government.

Non-State Persecution

Private individuals or organizations may be responsible for serious harm where the state is unable or unwilling to provide effective protection.

For example, a person may be targeted by a powerful private group because of their religion while the government is unable or unwilling to provide meaningful protection.

Internal Protection or Relocation

In some cases, decision-makers may consider whether the person could safely relocate to another part of their own country.

  • Does a safe area exist?
  • Can the person reasonably reach it?
  • Is effective protection available there?
  • Would relocation be reasonable in the circumstances?
The availability of state protection and internal relocation depends heavily on the individual case and applicable law.
CARD 9 · NON-REFOULEMENT

Non-Refoulement

Non-refoulement is the principle that protected persons should not be returned to a place where they would face prohibited persecution or other serious harm.

The principle is closely associated with Article 33 of the 1951 Refugee Convention.

It is one of the central principles of international refugee protection.

Human-Rights Law

Non-refoulement also interacts with broader international human-rights law.

Protection against return may apply where a person faces serious risks such as:

  • Torture.
  • Persecution.
  • Other serious or irreparable harm.
Protection → No Forced Return to Prohibited Danger

UNHCR recognizes non-refoulement as a core principle of refugee and human-rights protection and as part of customary international law.

CARD 10 · ASYLUM & EXPULSION

Asylum, Expulsion & Return

Asylum vs. Non-Refoulement

Asylum Non-Refoulement
Protection granted by a state to a person seeking safety. Protection against returning a person to prohibited serious danger.
Concerns the granting of protection. Concerns the prohibition against certain forms of return.

Expulsion

Article 32 of the Refugee Convention places limits on the expulsion of refugees.

Expulsion is permitted only under specified conditions and with important procedural protections.

Important Distinction

Expulsion Power ≠ Unlimited Power to Return a Person

A state may have authority to expel a person in certain circumstances, but international law can still prohibit returning that person to persecution, torture, or other serious harm.

CARD 11 · RIGHTS & DUTIES

Rights and Duties of Refugees

The Refugee Convention establishes important standards of treatment for refugees.

Rights

  • Non-discrimination.
  • Religious freedom.
  • Access to courts.
  • Education.
  • Employment.
  • Housing.
  • Freedom of movement.
  • Identity documents.
  • Travel documents.
  • Social protection.

Duties

Article 2 of the Refugee Convention provides that refugees must comply with the laws and regulations of the country in which they are present and with measures taken to maintain public order.

Refugee status provides international protection, but it does not remove the general obligation to respect the laws of the host state.
CARD 12 · RSD & EVIDENCE

Refugee Status Determination

Refugee Status Determination (RSD) is the process used to decide whether a person satisfies the applicable refugee definition.

A procedure may involve:

  1. Registration.
  2. Interviews.
  3. Evidence.
  4. Country-of-origin information.
  5. Legal analysis.
  6. A decision on eligibility.
  7. Review or appeal where available.

Evidence

Evidence can include:

  • Personal testimony.
  • Identity documents.
  • Police or court records.
  • Medical records.
  • Messages or correspondence.
  • Photographs.
  • Witness evidence.
  • Reliable country-of-origin information.
A lack of documentary evidence does not automatically mean that a refugee claim is false, particularly where people fleeing persecution may be unable to bring documents with them.
CARD 13 · EXCLUSION & CESSATION

Exclusion & Cessation

Exclusion

The Refugee Convention excludes certain people from refugee protection.

This can include persons for whom there are serious reasons to consider that they:

  • Committed a crime against peace, a war crime, or a crime against humanity.
  • Committed a serious non-political crime outside the country of refuge before admission as a refugee.
  • Are responsible for acts contrary to the purposes and principles of the United Nations.

Cessation

Refugee protection may end in circumstances specified by the Convention.

Examples include where a person:

  • Voluntarily re-avails themselves of their country's protection.
  • Voluntarily reacquires nationality.
  • Acquires a new nationality and enjoys its protection.
  • Voluntarily re-establishes themselves in the country they left.
  • No longer needs protection because circumstances fundamentally changed.
Exclusion concerns whether a person should receive Convention protection; cessation concerns circumstances in which existing refugee protection ends.
CARD 14 · AFRICAN REFUGEE LAW

Refugee Law in Africa

African refugee law has developed an important regional framework through the 1969 OAU Convention Governing the Specific Aspects of Refugee Problems in Africa.

The OAU Convention complements the 1951 Refugee Convention but adopts a broader refugee definition.

It includes people compelled to leave their usual residence because of circumstances such as:

  • External aggression.
  • Occupation.
  • Foreign domination.
  • Events seriously disturbing public order.

Why It Matters

The broader African definition recognizes that refugee movements can result not only from individualized persecution but also from armed conflict, foreign aggression, occupation, and serious disturbances of public order.

1951 Convention → Individualized Convention Grounds

1969 OAU Convention → Broader African Refugee Definition

This framework is particularly important in situations involving large-scale displacement and mass influx.

CARD 15 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Why Refugee Law Matters

Refugee law helps protect people who cannot safely rely on the protection of their own country.

It seeks to:

  • Protect people fleeing persecution.
  • Prevent forced return to serious danger.
  • Establish minimum standards of treatment.
  • Protect human dignity.
  • Provide legal status and rights.
  • Promote international cooperation.
  • Limit arbitrary expulsion.
  • Support access to education and employment.
  • Provide access to justice.
1951 Convention + 1967 Protocol + Regional Protection

Key Legal Questions

  • Refugee definition: Does the person satisfy the applicable legal definition?
  • Persecution: What caused the person to flee, and does the harm amount to persecution?
  • Convention ground: Is the persecution connected to race, religion, nationality, particular social group, or political opinion?
  • State protection: Can the person safely obtain effective protection from their country?
  • Exclusion: Does an exclusion clause apply?
  • Non-refoulement: Would return expose the person to prohibited persecution or serious harm?

Durable Solutions

The three traditional durable solutions are:

  1. Voluntary repatriation — returning home voluntarily when it is safe and dignified.
  2. Local integration — establishing a lasting life in the country of asylum.
  3. Resettlement — moving to another country that agrees to admit the refugee.
Key Takeaway: Refugee law is designed to ensure that people who cannot safely rely on the protection of their own country are not left without legal protection.
