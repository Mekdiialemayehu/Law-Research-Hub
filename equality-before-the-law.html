Equality Before the Law

Understanding equality, non-discrimination, equal protection, access to justice, and the relationship between equality and the rule of law.

What Is Equality Before the Law?

Equality before the law is a fundamental principle of human rights and the rule of law.

It means that people are entitled to equal treatment under the law and should not be subjected to arbitrary or unjustified differences in legal treatment.

Key Question:
Does the law treat people equally and fairly, without unjustified discrimination?

Equality does not always mean identical treatment. Different treatment may sometimes be lawful where there is an objective and reasonable justification.

Equality and the Rule of Law

Equality before the law is closely connected to the rule of law.

The rule of law requires public authorities to exercise power according to law rather than personal preference or arbitrary decisions.

Equality helps ensure that:

  • Government officials are accountable.
  • Courts apply legal rules consistently.
  • Individuals receive fair treatment.
  • Public authorities cannot act arbitrarily.
  • Legal rights are protected regardless of status.
If laws are applied differently to people without lawful justification, the rule of law is weakened.

Equality Does Not Always Mean Identical Treatment

Equality before the law does not necessarily require everyone to receive exactly the same treatment in every situation.

People may be in different circumstances, and the law may sometimes recognize those differences.

Examples of special protection may include:

  • Children.
  • Persons with disabilities.
  • Victims of discrimination.
  • People in vulnerable situations.
Key Principle:

Equality does not always mean treating everyone identically. It means avoiding unjustified or discriminatory differences in treatment.

Formal and Substantive Equality

Formal Equality

Formal equality focuses on equal treatment under legal rules. People in comparable circumstances should generally receive equal legal rights and obligations.

Substantive Equality

Substantive equality looks beyond whether rules appear equal on their face.

It considers the practical effects of laws and policies and whether they create unjustified disadvantage.

Two people may receive identical treatment but experience very different consequences. Substantive equality therefore asks whether people can genuinely enjoy equal protection and opportunity.

Non-Discrimination

Equality before the law is closely connected to non-discrimination.

Depending on the legal framework, prohibited grounds of discrimination may include:

  • Race.
  • Sex.
  • Religion.
  • National origin.
  • Disability.
  • Political opinion.
  • Social origin.
  • Other legally protected characteristics.
The exact protected grounds differ between legal systems. The applicable law determines which forms of discrimination are prohibited.
Card 5 of 15

Direct and Indirect Discrimination

Direct Discrimination

Direct discrimination occurs when a person is treated less favourably because of a protected characteristic.

Example: A law that expressly denies a legal benefit to people solely because of their sex could raise an issue of direct discrimination.

Indirect Discrimination

Indirect discrimination can occur when a rule appears neutral but has a particularly harmful effect on a protected group.

Courts may consider whether the measure has an objective, reasonable, and proportionate justification.

Equality Before the Law and Courts

Courts play an important role in ensuring equality before the law.

Judges should apply legal rules:

  • Independently.
  • Impartially.
  • Consistently.
  • Without unlawful discrimination.
  • According to the applicable legal framework.

A person should not receive better or worse treatment in court because of wealth, status, political influence, or another irrelevant characteristic.

Equality before the law supports confidence in the fairness and independence of the justice system.

Police, Government Officials, and Equality

Police and Law Enforcement

Police authorities should not arbitrarily target people because of protected characteristics.

  • Selective enforcement.
  • Discriminatory searches.
  • Unequal treatment of suspects.
  • Refusal to provide protection because of identity.

Government Officials

Public officials should not provide different legal treatment based on personal relationships, wealth, political influence, favouritism, or prejudice.

Public power should be exercised according to objective legal standards rather than personal preference.

Equality, Fair Trial, and Equality of Arms

Equality before the law is closely connected with the right to a fair trial.

Fair-trial protection may involve:

  • An independent and impartial tribunal.
  • Equal application of procedural rules.
  • The opportunity to present a case.
  • Protection against arbitrary treatment.
  • Judicial independence.

Equality of Arms

Equality of arms means that each party should have a reasonable opportunity to present their case without being placed at a substantial disadvantage compared with the opposing party.

Procedural rules should not unfairly favour one side in legal proceedings.

Access to Justice and Legal Aid

Equality before the law is meaningful only when people can actually access legal institutions.

Access to justice may require:

  • Accessible courts.
  • Fair legal procedures.
  • Legal assistance in appropriate circumstances.
  • Effective remedies.
  • Protection against discrimination.

Legal Aid

Legal aid can help reduce disadvantages caused by differences in financial resources.

Without meaningful access to justice, formal equality may provide little practical protection.

Equality and Vulnerable Groups

Human-rights law often pays particular attention to people who may face increased barriers to legal protection.

Examples include:

  • Children.
  • Persons with disabilities.
  • Migrants.
  • Minority groups.
  • Persons living in poverty.
  • Victims of serious abuse.

Special protective measures do not necessarily violate equality. In some circumstances, different treatment may be necessary to achieve genuine equality.

Example: Providing accessibility measures for a person with a disability may promote substantive equality rather than violate it.

Equality and Gender, Children, and Disability

Gender Equality

Legal rights should not be denied or restricted through unjustified gender discrimination.

Equality between women and men can be relevant to education, employment, political participation, family life, property, and access to justice.

Children

Children may receive special legal protections because of their age and circumstances, including special procedures, child-protection laws, and different criminal responsibility rules.

Disability

Genuine equality may sometimes require reasonable accommodation or accessibility measures so that people with disabilities can participate effectively in legal and public life.

Card 12 of 15

Equality and Positive Measures

Governments may sometimes take positive measures to address existing disadvantage.

Examples include:

  • Anti-discrimination legislation.
  • Accessibility requirements.
  • Special educational support.
  • Protective measures for vulnerable groups.
  • Programs designed to increase equal participation.

Depending on the legal system, these measures may be described as special measures or affirmative action.

Their legality depends on the applicable law, purpose, design, and proportionality of the measure.
Card 13 of 15

Equality and Proportionality

Equality before the law is closely connected to proportionality.

When a government treats two groups differently, a court may ask whether the distinction has sufficient justification.

The court may consider:

  1. What is the purpose of the distinction?
  2. Is the objective legitimate?
  3. Is there a reasonable connection between the distinction and the objective?
  4. Is the difference in treatment proportionate?
A difference in treatment is not automatically unlawful. The key issue is whether it has an objective and reasonable justification under the applicable legal framework.

Equality, Human Rights, and International Law

Universal Declaration of Human Rights

The UDHR recognizes equality before the law and the right to equal protection of the law.

International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights

The ICCPR contains several important equality protections, including provisions concerning non-discrimination, equality between men and women, equality before courts and tribunals, political participation, and equal protection of the law.

European Convention on Human Rights

Article 14 prohibits discrimination in the enjoyment of Convention rights. European human-rights jurisprudence also addresses equality and non-discrimination in many contexts.

The exact scope of equality protections depends on the applicable treaty, constitution, legislation, and case law.

Equality Before the Law: The Complete Framework

Equality Before the Law
People are entitled to equal legal protection and fair treatment without unjustified discrimination.
Formal Equality
Comparable people should generally receive equal treatment under the law.
Substantive Equality
The practical effects of laws and policies should not create unjustified disadvantage.
Non-Discrimination
Differences in treatment must not be based on prohibited or unjustified grounds.
Objective and Reasonable Justification
Different treatment may sometimes be lawful when it pursues a legitimate purpose and is appropriately justified.
Key Takeaway:

Equality Before the Law does not necessarily mean identical treatment. It means that the law must protect people fairly and must not treat comparable people differently without sufficient legal justification.

The key question in an equality case is therefore not simply:

"Were two people treated differently?"

It is: "Was the difference in treatment lawful, relevant, justified, and proportionate?"
