Positive vs. Negative Obligations

Understanding the duties of states to respect, protect, and fulfil human rights through restraint, protection, action, and effective remedies.

Human Rights and State Obligations

Human-rights law imposes obligations on states to respect, protect, and fulfil human rights.

These obligations can broadly be understood as negative obligations and positive obligations.

Key Question:
Must the government refrain from doing something, or must it take action to protect or facilitate a right?

The exact scope of positive and negative obligations depends on the particular right, legal system, and human-rights instrument involved.

What Are Positive and Negative Obligations?

A negative obligation generally requires the state to refrain from unjustifiably interfering with an individual's human rights.

A positive obligation generally requires the state to take reasonable and appropriate measures to protect rights or make their effective enjoyment possible.

Simple distinction:

Negative obligation = The state must NOT do something.

Positive obligation = The state may need to DO something.

The two obligations are not completely separate. A single human-rights case may involve both.

Negative Obligations

A negative obligation requires the state to avoid conduct that would unjustifiably interfere with a protected right.

These obligations are often described as duties to respect human rights.

Examples include:

  • Not subjecting a person to torture.
  • Not unlawfully imprisoning a person.
  • Not unlawfully interfering with private life.
  • Not arbitrarily restricting freedom of expression.
  • Not discriminating in circumstances prohibited by law.
The focus is on preventing the state itself from unjustifiably interfering with an individual's rights.
Card 3 of 15

Positive Obligations

A positive obligation requires the state to take reasonable action to protect or facilitate the effective enjoyment of human rights.

Possible measures include:

  • Creating appropriate laws.
  • Establishing effective institutions.
  • Protecting individuals from serious harm.
  • Investigating alleged violations.
  • Providing effective remedies.
  • Taking reasonable preventive measures.
  • Ensuring rights can be exercised in practice.
Positive obligations do not normally require governments to guarantee that every possible harm will be prevented. The required measures generally need to be reasonable and appropriate to the circumstances.

Why the Distinction Matters

The distinction helps determine what human-rights law requires from governments.

Negative obligation asks:

"What must the state refrain from doing?"

Positive obligation asks:

"What must the state do to protect or give practical effect to the right?"

For example, the right to private life may require the state not to interfere with privacy without lawful justification. At the same time, the state may need legal protections against serious invasions of privacy by private actors.

State Interference and State Protection

Negative obligations

Negative obligations become particularly important when the government itself interferes with a person's rights.

  • Arresting a person.
  • Searching a person's home.
  • Restricting speech.
  • Monitoring communications.
  • Interfering with family life.
  • Restricting peaceful assembly.

Positive obligations

Positive obligations become particularly important when individuals face serious threats from other people or private organizations.

The state may need to establish laws and institutions that provide reasonable protection against serious violence, exploitation, discrimination, or other rights violations.

Right to Life

The right to life illustrates the relationship between positive and negative obligations.

Negative obligation

The state must not arbitrarily or unlawfully take a person's life. This places a direct restriction on state authorities.

Positive obligation

The state may also have a duty to take reasonable measures to protect life.

  • Appropriate laws.
  • Effective law-enforcement systems.
  • Reasonable preventive measures.
  • Investigation of suspicious deaths.
  • Accountability for serious violations.
The exact scope of these obligations depends on the applicable human-rights framework.

Prohibition of Torture

The prohibition of torture creates a particularly strong negative obligation.

Negative obligation

State officials must not torture or subject individuals to prohibited ill-treatment.

Positive obligation

Effective protection against torture may also require systems designed to prevent and respond to abuse.

  • Criminal laws prohibiting torture.
  • Safeguards for people in detention.
  • Independent investigations.
  • Complaint procedures.
  • Accountability mechanisms.
  • Judicial remedies.
Effective human-rights protection can therefore involve both refraining from abuse and creating systems that prevent and respond to abuse.
Card 8 of 15

Freedom of Expression and Assembly

Freedom of Expression

Freedom of expression primarily protects individuals against unjustified government interference.

The government should not arbitrarily censor lawful expression simply because officials dislike the opinion being expressed.

In certain circumstances, positive obligations may also require legal protections that allow individuals, including journalists or other speakers, to exercise expression effectively.

Freedom of Assembly

The state should not unlawfully prohibit peaceful demonstrations. It may also sometimes need to take reasonable measures to allow peaceful assemblies to take place safely.

Card 9 of 15

Private and Family Life

The right to private and family life can create both negative and positive obligations.

Negative obligation

The state should not unjustifiably interfere with:

  • Privacy.
  • Family relationships.
  • The home.
  • Correspondence.
  • Personal autonomy.

Positive obligation

The state may also need to establish legal protections against serious interference by other individuals or organizations.

Human-rights law can therefore require both respect for privacy and protection of privacy.

Positive Obligations and Effective Protection

A right may exist formally but still be ineffective in practice.

For example, a constitution might recognize equality, but without effective laws and procedures for challenging serious discrimination, the right may be difficult to exercise meaningfully.

Human rights should receive practical and effective protection, rather than remaining merely theoretical.

Positive obligations can help transform rights from formal promises into practical protections.

This may require:

  • Effective laws.
  • Institutions.
  • Procedures.
  • Protection mechanisms.
  • Access to remedies.
Card 11 of 15

Reasonableness and Procedural Obligations

Reasonableness

Positive obligations do not normally require governments to take every conceivable action.

Courts may consider whether the state took reasonable and appropriate measures in the circumstances.

Relevant factors may include:

  • The seriousness of the risk.
  • Whether authorities knew or should have known about it.
  • The feasibility of preventive measures.
  • The importance of the right involved.
  • The interests of other individuals.

Procedural obligations

Some positive obligations concern procedures, such as investigating deaths, serious abuse, or other significant rights violations.

Investigations and Effective Remedies

Investigations

When serious human-rights concerns arise, the state may have an obligation to conduct an effective investigation.

Depending on the legal framework, an effective investigation may need to be sufficiently independent, thorough, and capable of establishing relevant facts and supporting accountability.

Effective Remedies

Human-rights protection also requires effective mechanisms through which individuals can challenge violations.

  • Judicial review.
  • Compensation.
  • Criminal proceedings.
  • Administrative procedures.
  • Court orders.
  • Investigations.

Positive and Negative Obligations Compared

Negative Obligations Positive Obligations
Require the state to refrain from unjustified interference. Require the state to take reasonable protective or supportive measures.
Focus on state restraint. Focus on state action or protection.
Often described as duties to respect rights. Often described as duties to protect or fulfil rights.
Example: government must not unlawfully censor speech. Example: government may need protections for lawful expression.

The two forms of obligation can operate at the same time.
Card 14 of 15

State Responsibility and Judicial Review

A government can sometimes incur human-rights responsibility because it failed to act, rather than because it directly caused the violation.

For negative obligations, courts may ask:

  1. Did the state interfere with the right?
  2. Was the interference lawful?
  3. Was a legitimate objective involved where required?
  4. Was the interference necessary?
  5. Was it proportionate?

For positive obligations, courts may ask:

  1. Was there a sufficiently serious rights issue?
  2. Did authorities know or should they have known about the risk?
  3. Were reasonable protective measures available?
  4. Did the authorities take appropriate action?
  5. Were effective procedures or remedies available?
States are not automatically responsible for every violation committed by a private individual. The applicable legal test must be satisfied.
Card 15 of 15

The Relationship Between Positive and Negative Obligations

The distinction between positive and negative obligations should not be treated as completely rigid.

A single human-rights case can involve both forms of obligation.

Example: Privacy

Negative obligation: The state must not unlawfully monitor a person.

Positive obligation: The state may need laws regulating private organizations that collect or misuse personal information.
Key Difference:

Negative = The state must NOT unjustifiably interfere.

Positive = The state may need to DO something reasonable to protect or make the right effective.

Positive obligations do not normally guarantee perfect outcomes. The central question is whether the state took the reasonable and appropriate measures required by the applicable human-rights framework.

Remember:

Negative = Respect by restraint.
Positive = Protect through action.
