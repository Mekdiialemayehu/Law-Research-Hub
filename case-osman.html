Osman v. United Kingdom (1998)

European Court of Human Rights | Human Rights Law

Case Overview

Case Name: Osman v. United Kingdom (1998)

Court: European Court of Human Rights (ECHR)

Area of Law: Human Rights Law

Key Articles: Article 2 and Article 6(1) of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR)

Key Principle: States have a positive obligation under Article 2 to take reasonable preventive measures when authorities knew or ought to have known of a real and immediate risk to the life of an identified individual from the criminal acts of a third party.

Facts of the Case

Ahmet Osman was a student at Homerton House School in London. His teacher, Paul Paget-Lewis, became unusually obsessed with him and began displaying disturbing behaviour.

The teacher harassed Ahmet and his family, followed them, damaged property, made threats, and caused serious concern. Several incidents were reported to the police, but they did not arrest or effectively intervene.

On 7 March 1988, Paget-Lewis carried out a shooting. He killed Ahmet's father, Ali Osman, and seriously injured Ahmet.

The Osman family argued that the police failed to protect them despite knowing, or should have known, about the danger.

Procedural History

The Osman family brought a negligence claim against the police in the UK. The domestic courts rejected the claim because police were protected from negligence claims relating to crime prevention.

After exhausting domestic remedies, the applicants brought the case before the European Court of Human Rights.

  • Violation of Article 2: Failure to protect life.
  • Violation of Article 6(1): Denial of access to court.

Key Legal Issue

Whether the United Kingdom violated Article 2 by failing to protect the Osman family from a known risk and whether Article 6(1) was violated by preventing their negligence claim against the police.

Arguments

Applicants (Osman Family)

The police knew or should have known that Paget-Lewis posed a real and immediate danger but failed to take reasonable preventive measures.

United Kingdom Government

The Government argued that the police could not have reasonably predicted the shooting and that police immunity was necessary to allow effective crime prevention.

Decision and Reasoning

Article 2 - Right to Life

The Court recognised that States have a positive obligation to protect individuals from real and immediate risks to life. However, it found that the evidence did not prove that authorities knew or should have known of a specific and immediate threat.

Therefore, there was no violation of Article 2.

Article 6(1) - Access to Court

The Court found that the blanket immunity protecting police from negligence claims was disproportionate and violated the applicants' right of access to a court.

Legal Impact

  • Established the "Osman test" for state responsibility.
  • Clarified positive obligations under Article 2.
  • Limited government responsibility to situations involving real and immediate risks.
  • Influenced later cases involving domestic violence and state protection, including Opuz v. Turkey.

Critical Evaluation

The judgment successfully balanced individual rights with the practical realities of policing. However, the requirement of a "real and immediate risk" can be difficult to prove and may limit protection for vulnerable individuals.

Although the Court created an important principle, its cautious application meant that victims may still struggle to hold authorities accountable.

Final Conclusion

Osman v. United Kingdom remains a significant human rights case because it defined when States can be responsible for failing to prevent harm. It also confirmed that access to justice must be practical and effective. The case continues to influence human rights law by balancing state responsibility with the realities of public protection.

Source

Personal Reflection

Osman v. United Kingdom helped me understand that access to a court is not just a technical legal right, but a real protection that must be available in practice. The case showed me that when people are denied the opportunity to bring their claims before a court, justice itself is weakened.

It also deepened my appreciation of Article 6 as a safeguard for fairness, dignity, and accountability in human rights law.
