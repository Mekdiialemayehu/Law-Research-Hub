CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Precedent?

Precedent refers to a previous judicial decision that may be used as authority when deciding a later case.

The principle of precedent is particularly important in legal systems based on common law, where courts may follow legal principles established by earlier decisions.

Precedent helps promote consistency, predictability, and stability in the law.

The basic idea is that when a court has already decided an important legal question, later courts may use that decision when dealing with similar facts or legal issues.

CARD 2 · STARE DECISIS

The Doctrine of Stare Decisis

The use of precedent is often connected to the principle of stare decisis, a Latin phrase meaning that courts should generally stand by decided matters.

Under this principle, courts generally follow relevant decisions from higher courts within the same legal system.

This does not mean that every previous case must always be followed. Whether a precedent is binding or persuasive depends on factors such as the court that made the original decision and the legal system involved.

The doctrine therefore balances consistency with the ability of courts to deal with cases that are meaningfully different.

CARD 3 · BINDING PRECEDENT

Binding Precedent

Binding precedent is a previous judicial decision that a court is required to follow.

In a hierarchical court system, lower courts are generally bound by relevant decisions of higher courts.

For example, if a country's highest court establishes a legal principle, lower courts may be required to apply that principle when deciding similar cases.

Binding precedent helps create consistency because similar legal issues are more likely to be decided according to the same legal rules.
CARD 4 · PERSUASIVE PRECEDENT

Persuasive Precedent

Persuasive precedent is a previous decision that a court is not required to follow but may consider when making its decision.

Persuasive authority can come from:

  • Decisions of lower courts.
  • Decisions from courts in other jurisdictions.
  • Obiter dicta from earlier cases.
  • Judicial decisions that are not binding on the court.

A judge may find a persuasive precedent convincing and use its reasoning when reaching a decision.

Binding precedent must generally be followed when applicable. Persuasive precedent may influence a court but does not require the court to follow it.
CARD 5 · RATIO DECIDENDI & OBITER DICTA

Ratio Decidendi and Obiter Dicta

Ratio Decidendi

The ratio decidendi is the legal principle or rule that was necessary for the court's decision.

It is the part of a judgment that provides the legal reasoning supporting the outcome and may form the basis of a binding precedent.

Obiter Dicta

Obiter dicta refers to statements made by a judge that are not essential to the decision in the case.

The term is Latin for "things said by the way."

Obiter dicta is generally not binding in the same way as the ratio decidendi, but it may still be persuasive.
CARD 6 · COURT HIERARCHY

Court Hierarchy and Precedent

The effect of precedent depends heavily on the hierarchy of courts.

Higher courts generally have greater authority than lower courts.

Supreme or Highest Court
↓
Appeal Court
↓
Trial or Lower Courts

A lower court will generally be required to follow relevant decisions from a higher court within the same legal system.

However, the precise rules differ between countries because each legal system has its own court structure and rules concerning precedent.

CARD 7 · EXAMPLE

Precedent in Practice

Imagine that a higher court decides that a particular type of government action is unlawful because it violates a legal requirement.

Later, a lower court hears a case involving very similar circumstances.

If the earlier decision is binding, the lower court will generally have to apply the legal principle established by the higher court.

This allows similar cases to be treated consistently and helps people understand how courts are likely to approach similar legal issues.
CARD 8 · FOLLOWING, DISTINGUISHING & OVERRULING

What Courts Can Do With Precedent

Following Precedent

A court follows precedent when it applies the legal principle from an earlier case to a later case involving sufficiently similar legal issues or facts.

Distinguishing Precedent

A court may distinguish an earlier case when the facts or legal circumstances are sufficiently different.

Distinguishing allows courts to respect existing precedent while recognising meaningful differences between cases.

Overruling Precedent

A higher court may overrule an earlier judicial decision when it determines that the earlier legal principle should no longer be treated as correct.

Overruling can allow the law to develop when previous decisions are considered outdated, incorrect, or inconsistent with later legal principles.

CARD 9 · LEGAL DEVELOPMENT

Precedent and the Development of Law

Precedent can contribute to the development of law.

Courts may interpret existing legal principles and apply them to new situations, especially when legislation does not provide a complete answer.

This can allow legal rules to develop gradually through judicial decisions.

Courts are generally expected to work within the authority given to them by the legal system. Major changes in the law may sometimes be more appropriately made by legislation.

Precedent and Statute Law

Statute law is created by the legislature, while precedent develops through judicial decisions.

Courts often interpret legislation when deciding cases. Their interpretations can then become important precedents for future cases.

CARD 10 · ADVANTAGES AND LIMITATIONS

Advantages and Limitations of Precedent

Advantages

  • Consistency: Similar cases can be treated similarly.
  • Predictability: People can better understand how courts may approach legal disputes.
  • Efficiency: Courts can rely on established legal reasoning.
  • Development of Law: Judicial decisions can clarify unclear areas and apply principles to new situations.

Limitations

  • Complexity: Large numbers of decisions can make relevant precedents difficult to identify.
  • Rigidity: Strict reliance on previous decisions can make the law less adaptable.
  • Conflicting Decisions: Different courts may sometimes produce apparently inconsistent decisions.
  • Lengthy Legal Reasoning: Judicial decisions can contain complex discussions of facts and legal principles.
CARD 11 · DIFFERENT LEGAL SYSTEMS

Precedent in Different Legal Systems

The role of precedent differs between legal systems.

Common Law Systems

In common law systems, judicial precedent generally plays a major role in the development and application of law.

Civil Law Systems

In civil law systems, legislation and legal codes generally play a more central role, although decisions of higher courts may still influence how laws are interpreted and applied.

The importance and binding effect of precedent therefore depend on the legal tradition and rules of the particular country.

Precedent and Judicial Independence

Precedent is also connected to judicial independence.

Judges must apply legal principles independently and impartially while respecting the rules governing precedent.

A system of precedent can limit arbitrary decision-making by requiring courts to consider existing legal authorities rather than deciding cases entirely according to personal preference.

CARD 12 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Why Precedent Matters

The doctrine of precedent helps determine:

  • How previous court decisions influence later cases.
  • When courts must follow earlier decisions.
  • When judges may consider earlier decisions only as persuasive authority.
  • How legal principles can develop over time.
  • How consistency and predictability are maintained within the legal system.
Precedent = Previous judicial decisions that guide future cases.

In systems that recognise binding precedent, lower courts generally follow relevant decisions of higher courts. Other decisions may be persuasive rather than binding.

Precedent therefore helps promote consistency and predictability while also allowing the law to develop through judicial reasoning.
