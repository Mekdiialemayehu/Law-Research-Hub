Understanding the legal responsibility of states to protect, respect, and fulfil women's human rights.

01 · Definition

What is State Responsibility?

State responsibility in women's rights refers to the legal responsibility governments may have when their actions or failures to act violate rights protected by national, regional, or international human-rights law.

A state may be responsible not only when government officials directly violate a woman's rights, but also when authorities fail to take reasonable measures to protect women from serious violations committed by private individuals.

State responsibility therefore involves both negative obligations—duties not to violate rights—and positive obligations—duties to take appropriate measures to protect and fulfil rights.

The key idea is simple: a state can have human-rights responsibilities even when the immediate harm is caused by a private individual.
02 · Why It Matters

Why is State Responsibility Important for Women's Rights?

Human-rights protections become meaningful only when states take steps to make those rights effective in practice. Laws on equality, dignity, security, and freedom from violence require functioning institutions capable of enforcing them.

This means that governments may have responsibilities to prevent foreseeable violations, protect people facing serious risks, investigate allegations, prosecute offenders where appropriate, and provide effective remedies.

State responsibility is particularly important in cases involving violence against women because the immediate perpetrator may be a private individual. Human-rights law can nevertheless examine whether government authorities knew, or should have known, about the risk and whether they responded appropriately.

03 · International Legal Framework

International Protection

CEDAW · Articles 1–2

Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women

CEDAW establishes a broad international framework for eliminating discrimination against women. Article 2 requires states to pursue policies aimed at eliminating discrimination and to take appropriate measures to ensure the effective protection of women's rights.

The CEDAW Committee has further explained that states can have responsibility for private acts when they fail to act with due diligence to prevent, investigate, punish, or provide redress for gender-based violence.

CEDAW · General Recommendation No. 35

Due Diligence and State Responsibility

General Recommendation No. 35 explains that gender-based violence against women can constitute discrimination under CEDAW. It emphasizes that states must respond to violence through appropriate legislative, administrative, judicial, and other measures.

The principle of due diligence is important because it asks whether the state took reasonable steps to prevent and respond to violations rather than focusing only on the identity of the immediate perpetrator.

International Human Rights Law

Positive Obligations

Human-rights obligations can require states to take active measures to make rights effective. These positive obligations may include establishing legal protections, creating effective institutions, investigating serious violations, and protecting individuals from foreseeable harm.

Due Diligence

Private Acts and State Responsibility

A state does not necessarily escape responsibility simply because a violation was committed by a private person. Where authorities knew or should have known about a serious risk and failed to take reasonable measures within their powers, the state's response can become a human-rights issue.

04 · State Duties

What Responsibilities Do States Have?

Respect

Respect Women's Rights

States must refrain from directly violating women's human rights through laws, policies, practices, or actions carried out by public authorities.

Protect

Protect Women from Violations

States may need to take reasonable measures to protect women from violations committed by private individuals when serious risks are known or reasonably foreseeable.

Fulfil

Fulfil Human Rights

States should create the legal, institutional, and practical conditions necessary for women to enjoy their rights effectively and equally.

Accountability

Investigate and Provide Remedies

Where serious violations occur, states may have duties to investigate effectively, hold perpetrators accountable where appropriate, and provide accessible and meaningful remedies.

05 · Featured Case

M.C. v. Bulgaria

European Court of Human Rights · 2003

A landmark case on state obligations and sexual violence

M.C. v. Bulgaria concerned an allegation of sexual violence against a young woman and the subsequent response of the Bulgarian authorities. The applicant argued that the investigation had failed to provide effective protection against serious violations of her rights.

The European Court of Human Rights examined Bulgaria's obligations under Article 3, which prohibits torture and inhuman or degrading treatment, and Article 8, which protects private life.

The Court emphasized that states have positive obligations to establish and effectively apply a system of criminal law capable of punishing non-consensual sexual acts. It found that the Bulgarian authorities' investigation had not adequately addressed the circumstances of the alleged sexual violence.

Why is it relevant?

M.C. is directly relevant to state responsibility because it demonstrates that human-rights law can impose positive obligations on states to protect individuals from serious violations committed by private persons.

The case also demonstrates that state responsibility is not limited to situations where government officials personally commit the violation. A state's failure to maintain an effective legal and investigative framework can itself raise questions under human-rights law.

M.C. therefore illustrates an important principle: protecting women's rights requires more than laws existing on paper; institutions must apply those laws effectively.

06 · Key Legal Principles

Key Principles of State Responsibility

Due Diligence

States should take reasonable measures to prevent, investigate, punish, and remedy serious human-rights violations.

Positive Obligations

Human-rights law can require states to take active measures to make women's rights effective in practice.

Effective Investigation

Serious allegations should be investigated through procedures capable of establishing the relevant facts and responsibility.

Non-discrimination

States must ensure that women receive equal protection and that discriminatory practices do not undermine their rights.

Access to Justice

Women should be able to access legal institutions and procedures capable of protecting their rights and providing remedies.

Effective Remedies

Where rights have been violated, states should provide meaningful legal and institutional remedies.

07 · Understanding the Problem

Why Can State Responsibility Be Difficult to Enforce?

Establishing state responsibility often requires examining what authorities knew, what they were legally required to do, and whether the measures they took were reasonable and effective.

Weak institutions
Courts, police, or other public institutions may lack the resources or capacity needed to protect rights effectively.
Failure to investigate
Inadequate investigations can prevent serious violations from being properly established and addressed.
Discriminatory attitudes
Gender stereotypes can influence how authorities respond to complaints and can weaken women's access to justice.
Lack of awareness
Women may not know what legal protections or remedies are available to them.
Limited access to justice
Cost, distance, social pressure, and institutional barriers can make legal remedies difficult to obtain.
Implementation gaps
Strong legal protections may exist formally while enforcement and practical protection remain limited.
08 · Ethiopia

State Responsibility in Ethiopia

Ethiopia's constitutional framework provides important protections that can be examined through the principle of state responsibility. These include equality, personal security, freedom from cruel or degrading treatment, and specific protections concerning women's rights.

Article 25 · Right to Equality

The Constitution provides that all persons are equal before the law and are entitled to equal protection without discrimination, including discrimination on grounds of sex.

Article 16 · Right to Security of Person

The Constitution protects every person from bodily harm and recognizes the right to liberty and security of person. These protections are relevant when examining whether the state has taken appropriate measures to protect women from violence.

Article 18 · Prohibition of Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment

The Constitution provides that everyone has the right not to be subjected to cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment or punishment. This protection is relevant when considering the state's responsibility to respond to serious violations of personal integrity.

Article 35 · Rights of Women

Article 35 recognizes women's equal enjoyment of rights and requires measures to address discrimination against women. It also recognizes women's right to protection from harmful customs and practices.

Why the Ethiopian context matters

State responsibility in Ethiopia is not only a question of whether legal protections exist. It also raises questions about implementation: whether women can access justice, whether authorities respond effectively to violations, whether investigations are adequate, and whether legal protections operate equally in practice.

09 · Legal Analysis

State Responsibility: Law vs. Implementation

The concept of state responsibility demonstrates why human-rights analysis must examine both formal legal protections and the actions of institutions responsible for enforcing them.

State obligation Practical question
Respect women's rights. Do government authorities themselves avoid actions that violate women's rights?
Protect women from violations. Does the state respond appropriately when authorities know that a woman faces a serious risk?
Investigate serious violations. Are allegations investigated effectively, independently, and without discriminatory assumptions?
Provide effective remedies. Can women realistically obtain protection, justice, compensation, or other appropriate remedies?
10 · Research Question

Think Like a Human-Rights Researcher

When does a state's failure to protect women's rights become a violation of its human-rights obligations?

A strong legal analysis should ask three things: What right has been affected? What legal duty does the state have? Did the state take reasonable and effective measures to fulfil that duty?
