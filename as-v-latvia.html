When Is an Investigation Enough? The European Court of Human Rights Rules on Forced Labour

A.S. v. Latvia and the difficult question of what states must do when allegations of exploitation arise

Published: September 2026 By: Mekdes Alemayehu

A recent judgment from the European Court of Human Rights has raised an important question about the responsibility of governments when allegations of forced labour and exploitation are made.

On 3 September 2026, the European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) delivered its judgment in A.S. v. Latvia, concerning a Latvian woman who alleged that she had been subjected to servitude or forced labour while working on a dairy farm. The Court ultimately found no violation of Article 4 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which prohibits slavery, servitude and forced or compulsory labour.

But the judgment raises a question that goes beyond the outcome of this particular case:

When someone alleges exploitation, what exactly must a state do to satisfy its human-rights obligations?

What Happened?

The applicant, identified by the Court as A.S., began working on a dairy farm in Latvia in December 2016. She had responded to a job advertisement and agreed to carry out two milking sessions a day.

According to the Court's account, there was no written employment contract. A.S. repeatedly asked about her salary but was told that payment would come later because the dairy-processing company had withheld the money.

She remained on the farm until May 2017. During that period, the farm operator provided her with various necessities, including food and other supplies, and gave her money for a medical visit.

After leaving the farm, A.S. complained to the Latvian State Labour Inspectorate about her unpaid wages. The complaint was subsequently referred to the police, and criminal proceedings were opened against the farm operator on human-trafficking charges.

The operator was initially convicted, but after further proceedings the conviction was overturned and he was ultimately acquitted. That acquittal became final in October 2022.

A.S. then brought her case to the European Court of Human Rights.

The Legal Question

A.S. relied on Article 4 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

Article 4 protects people from:

  • slavery;
  • servitude; and
  • forced or compulsory labour.

Her argument was not simply that she had not been paid. She argued that the circumstances surrounding her employment could amount to prohibited forced labour or servitude and that the Latvian authorities had failed to respond effectively.

The ECHR first found that her complaint was arguable and supported by prima facie evidence. The Court pointed to several circumstances, including the promise of remuneration, the alleged non-payment of wages, her living arrangements on the farm and her practical dependence on the farm operator for certain necessities.

The Court was not saying that the allegations were frivolous. Instead, it accepted that the allegations were serious enough to engage Article 4.

What Did the Court Decide?

Despite finding that Article 4 was applicable, the Court ultimately found no violation.

The Court examined whether Latvia had fulfilled its positive obligations under Article 4, including whether it had an adequate legal framework and whether the authorities had conducted an effective investigation.

The Court concluded that Latvia had a legal framework capable, in principle, of protecting people against the treatment alleged by A.S.

It also found that the investigation had been prompt and thorough.

The authorities had taken numerous investigative steps, gathered evidence from different sources, interviewed A.S. several times and examined the case through multiple levels of the domestic courts. A.S. had legal representation throughout the proceedings.

The Court also emphasized an important principle:

An acquittal does not automatically mean that a state has violated Article 4.

For the ECHR to find a violation based on the investigation, there would need to be significant shortcomings capable of undermining the proceedings' ability to establish what happened or identify those responsible.

The Court did not find such shortcomings in this case.

Therefore, the Court unanimously concluded that there had been no violation of Article 4.

My Analysis

I think this judgment is more complicated than simply saying that the Court "ruled in favour of Latvia."

On one hand, I understand the Court's reasoning.

A human-rights court cannot automatically conclude that a state violated Article 4 every time an allegation of forced labour ends in an acquittal. The purpose of the investigation is not necessarily to guarantee a conviction. States must investigate serious allegations properly, but courts still have to assess evidence and determine whether the legal requirements for criminal responsibility have been established.

In this case, the ECHR found that the Latvian authorities had taken the allegations seriously and had conducted a substantial investigation. From that perspective, finding no violation of Article 4 is understandable.

But I think the case also exposes a difficult weakness in the way human-rights protection can work in practice.

A person can make a serious allegation of exploitation, have the authorities investigate it, go through several levels of court, and still finish the process without a finding that the exploitation actually occurred.

That does not necessarily mean that the investigation was inadequate. But it raises an important question:

Is a thorough investigation enough if the person who reported the exploitation ultimately receives no judicial recognition that what happened to them amounted to forced labour or servitude?

I do not think the answer is simple.

Human-rights law must protect people from genuine exploitation, but it must also respect the principles of evidence and fair criminal proceedings. The challenge is finding the balance between these two responsibilities.

What I find particularly interesting about A.S. v. Latvia is that the Court did not dismiss the applicant's concerns as insignificant. It actually recognized that she had presented an arguable claim involving circumstances capable of engaging Article 4.

Yet the final legal conclusion was still that Latvia had not violated the Convention.

To me, this demonstrates an important distinction in human-rights law:

A court can recognize that a person's allegations are serious without ultimately finding that the state violated that person's human rights.

That distinction is easy to miss when reading only the headline of a judgment.

Why This Case Matters

A.S. v. Latvia shows that protecting human rights involves more than simply having laws that prohibit abuse.

States must also have mechanisms capable of responding when those rights may have been violated.

At the same time, an investigation does not have to produce a conviction to satisfy the European Convention. What matters is whether the authorities seriously, promptly and effectively investigated the allegations and whether significant flaws undermined the proceedings.

For me, the most important question left by this case is therefore not simply "Who won?"

What does justice require when an allegation of exploitation is investigated thoroughly, but the legal system ultimately finds that no criminal responsibility has been established?

That is a question that extends far beyond Latvia.

It is a question about what we expect human-rights law to accomplish when the experiences of an individual and the requirements of legal proof do not lead to the same conclusion.
