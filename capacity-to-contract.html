⚖️ Capacity to Contract

Contract Law · Interactive Legal Note

CAPACITY TO CONTRACT
● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ●
CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Capacity to Contract?

Capacity to contract refers to a person's legal ability to enter into a binding and enforceable contract.

Contract law does not always treat every person as having unlimited contractual capacity.

Certain individuals may receive special legal protection because they may be considered less able to understand contractual obligations or protect their own interests.

Capacity therefore asks the question:

Does this person have the legal ability to enter into this contract?

The exact rules concerning contractual capacity vary between legal systems.

CARD 2 · IMPORTANCE

Why Capacity Matters

Capacity rules help protect individuals who may not be fully able to understand or evaluate contractual commitments.

Without capacity rules, a person could potentially become legally bound by an agreement they did not properly understand.

Capacity Rules Balance:

  • Freedom of contract.
  • Protection of vulnerable parties.
  • Certainty in commercial transactions.
  • Fairness.
Capacity rules attempt to balance contractual freedom with protection against unfair exploitation.
CARD 3 · CATEGORIES

Categories of Capacity

Special rules may apply to contracts involving different categories of people and legal entities.

  • Minors.
  • People lacking sufficient mental capacity.
  • Persons affected by certain forms of intoxication.
  • Companies and other legal entities.
  • Agents acting on behalf of others.

The rules differ depending on the legal system and the type of contract involved.

CARD 4 · MINORS

Minors and Contractual Capacity

A minor is a person who has not reached the legal age of adulthood.

The age of majority varies between jurisdictions.

Because minors may have limited contractual capacity, the law often provides them with special protection.

However, a minor is not necessarily incapable of entering into every contract.

Certain contracts may be binding on a minor, while others may be unenforceable or subject to special rules.

Why Protection Exists

The purpose is to prevent minors from being unfairly bound by contractual commitments they may not have been able to properly evaluate.

CARD 5 · NECESSARIES

Contracts for Necessaries

A major exception concerning minors involves contracts for necessaries.

Necessaries are goods or services that are appropriate and reasonably necessary for the person's circumstances.

Examples

  • Food.
  • Clothing.
  • Basic accommodation.
  • Essential educational materials.
  • Necessary medical services.

The exact definition depends on the applicable law.

The purpose is to prevent minors from being unfairly deprived of essential goods and services simply because of their age.
CARD 6 · BENEFICIAL CONTRACTS

Beneficial Contracts of Service

Some legal systems recognize contracts of service that are genuinely beneficial to a minor.

For example, an agreement may involve:

  • Education.
  • Training.
  • Employment.
  • Professional development.

Such contracts may receive different treatment if the agreement is considered beneficial to the minor.

A court may examine the agreement as a whole rather than focusing on one isolated term.

The central question is whether the contract provides a genuine benefit to the minor under the applicable law.
CARD 7 · OTHER MINORS' CONTRACTS

Non-Necessaries and Ratification

Contracts for Non-Necessaries

Contracts involving unnecessary or disadvantageous purchases may receive greater protection for minors.

For example, a minor purchasing an expensive luxury item may not necessarily be treated in the same way as a minor purchasing essential clothing.

The legal consequences depend on the applicable jurisdiction.

Ratification After Adulthood

In some legal systems, a person may confirm or ratify certain agreements after reaching the age of majority.

Ratification means affirming an agreement that previously had limited or uncertain enforceability.

The precise rules concerning ratification depend on the jurisdiction.

CARD 8 · MENTAL CAPACITY

Mental Capacity

A person may have limited contractual capacity because they are unable to understand the nature or consequences of a transaction.

Mental capacity rules vary considerably between legal systems.

The Central Questions

  • Could the person understand the nature of the transaction?
  • Could the person understand its significant consequences?
  • Could the person make a rational decision concerning the agreement?
A person does not automatically lack contractual capacity merely because they have a mental impairment or condition.
CARD 9 · TIME AND KNOWLEDGE

Mental Capacity and the Other Party

The relevant question is generally whether the person had sufficient understanding at the time the contract was made.

Capacity is therefore usually assessed in relation to the particular transaction and circumstances.

Knowledge of the Other Party

In some legal systems, a contract made by a person lacking mental capacity may still have legal consequences if the other party had no reason to know about the lack of capacity.

Courts may consider:

  • The person's conduct.
  • The nature of the transaction.
  • Whether the other party knew or should have known about the incapacity.
  • Whether the contract involved necessaries.
  • Whether the transaction was fair.
CARD 10 · INTOXICATION

Intoxication and Capacity

Intoxication can sometimes affect contractual capacity.

A person may become so intoxicated that they are unable to understand the nature of a transaction.

However, being intoxicated does not automatically make a contract invalid.

Possible Requirements

Depending on the legal system, there may need to be evidence that:

  • The person was sufficiently intoxicated to lack understanding.
  • The other party knew or should have known about the person's condition.
The precise legal consequences of intoxication vary between legal systems.
CARD 11 · COMPANIES

Companies and Corporate Capacity

Capacity is not limited to individuals. Companies and other legal entities can also raise questions concerning contractual capacity.

A company acts through representatives such as:

  • Directors.
  • Officers.
  • Employees.
  • Agents.
  • Other authorized representatives.

Corporate Capacity

A company's constitutional documents and applicable company law may determine the powers it possesses.

Modern company legislation in many jurisdictions gives companies broad capacity to enter into contracts, although specific restrictions can still arise.

CARD 12 · AGENCY AND AUTHORITY

Agency and Authority

An agent is a person authorized to act on behalf of another person or organization.

For example, a company may authorize an employee to purchase office equipment on its behalf.

The legal effect may depend on whether the employee had actual or apparent authority.

Capacity = Legal ability to contract.

Authority = Legal power to contract on behalf of another person or entity.

A person may personally have full legal capacity but lack authority to enter into a particular contract on behalf of someone else.

CARD 13 · RELATED REQUIREMENTS

Capacity vs. Other Contract Requirements

Capacity vs. Consent

Capacity asks whether the person has the legal ability to enter into the contract.

Consent asks whether the person genuinely agreed to the contract.

A person may have full capacity but argue that their consent was affected by duress, undue influence, misrepresentation, or fraud.

Capacity vs. Consideration

Capacity concerns whether a person can legally contract.

Consideration concerns what was exchanged in support of the promise.

Capacity vs. Intention

Intention asks whether the parties intended their agreement to have legal consequences.

Capacity asks whether the parties were legally capable of creating those consequences.

CARD 14 · CONSEQUENCES

Consequences of Lack of Capacity

The legal consequences of lacking capacity can vary depending on the jurisdiction and the type of contract.

An agreement may be:

  • Binding.
  • Void.
  • Voidable.
  • Unenforceable.
  • Enforceable only to a limited extent.
  • Subject to restitution or another remedy.

Void

A void contract is treated as having no legal effect from the beginning.

Voidable

A voidable contract is generally valid unless and until the protected party takes legally recognized steps to avoid it.

Restitution

Where a contract is avoided or cannot be enforced because of capacity, questions may arise about whether money or property should be returned.

CARD 15 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

Capacity to contract refers to a person's legal ability to enter into a binding contract.

The law recognizes that certain people may require special protection, particularly:

  • Minors.
  • People lacking sufficient mental capacity.
  • People affected by certain forms of intoxication.

Capacity issues can also arise with companies and agents, although these involve different legal questions.

Important Distinctions

Capacity ≠ Consent

Capacity ≠ Consideration

Capacity ≠ Authority

A person may have the ability to contract but still have problems with consent. Similarly, an employee may have full personal capacity but lack authority to bind a company.

The key question in a contract case is therefore:

"Did the parties have the legal capacity and authority necessary to enter into this agreement?"

Once capacity is established, other requirements of contract formation must still be considered.
