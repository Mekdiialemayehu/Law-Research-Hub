PRESUMPTION OF INNOCENCE

CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is the Presumption of Innocence?

The presumption of innocence is a fundamental principle of criminal law stating that a person accused of a crime must be treated as innocent unless and until their guilt is established according to law.

It reflects the principle that an accusation is not the same as proof.

Who must prove that the accused is guilty?

In criminal proceedings, the prosecution generally bears the burden of proving the elements of the offence.

The accused does not normally have to prove that they are innocent.

The presumption of innocence is recognized in many legal systems and is closely connected to the right to a fair trial.

CARD 2 · PURPOSE

Purpose of the Presumption of Innocence

The presumption of innocence protects individuals from being treated as criminals merely because they have been accused.

Its Purposes Include

  • Protecting individuals against wrongful conviction.
  • Requiring the prosecution to prove criminal responsibility.
  • Preventing guilt from being assumed before trial.
  • Supporting impartial and fair criminal proceedings.
  • Limiting the power of the state to punish individuals without sufficient proof.
Criminal punishment should follow proof of guilt rather than simply an allegation.
CARD 3 · BURDEN OF PROOF

Presumption of Innocence and Burden of Proof

The presumption of innocence is closely connected to the burden of proof.

In most criminal proceedings, the prosecution carries the burden of proving the accused's guilt.

The prosecution must establish the elements of the offence according to the applicable standard of proof.

In many common law systems, the criminal standard is described as beyond reasonable doubt.

The accused generally does not have to prove that they did not commit the offence.

CARD 4 · STANDARD OF PROOF

Presumption of Innocence and Standard of Proof

The presumption of innocence means that a person should not be convicted unless the prosecution satisfies the required standard of proof.

In many common law jurisdictions, this standard is described as beyond reasonable doubt.

Presumption of innocence + prosecution's burden of proof + required standard of proof = protection against conviction without sufficient evidence.

The precise wording and application of the standard vary between legal systems.

CARD 5 · ACCUSATION

Accusation Is Not Proof

An accusation is not proof of guilt.

A person may be accused of an offence for many reasons, but the accusation itself does not establish that the offence was committed by that person.

Criminal courts must determine guilt based on admissible evidence and the applicable law.

The presumption of innocence prevents the criminal justice system from treating an allegation as an established fact.
CARD 6 · BEFORE TRIAL

Presumption of Innocence Before Trial

The presumption applies before a criminal trial as well as during the trial.

Before guilt is established, an accused person should not automatically be treated as a convicted criminal.

This principle may affect:

  • Public statements by authorities.
  • Treatment of the accused.
  • Judicial decisions.
  • Bail proceedings.
  • Pre-trial procedures.
  • The presentation of information about the case.
CARD 7 · DURING TRIAL

Presumption of Innocence During Trial

During trial, the presumption of innocence affects how the case is presented and decided.

The judge or jury should not begin with the assumption that the accused committed the offence.

Instead, the prosecution must establish the required elements through evidence.

The fact that a person has been charged does not itself satisfy the burden of proof.
CARD 8 · PROSECUTION

Presumption of Innocence and the Prosecution

The prosecution generally has the responsibility of proving the accused's guilt.

Depending on the offence, the elements may include:

  • Actus reus.
  • Mens rea.
  • Causation.
  • Particular factual circumstances.
If the prosecution fails to prove an essential element, the accused should not be convicted of that offence.
CARD 9 · THE ACCUSED

Presumption of Innocence and the Accused

The accused generally benefits from the presumption of innocence throughout the criminal process.

The accused may:

  • Challenge the prosecution's evidence.
  • Remain silent where the law provides a right to silence.
  • Present evidence.
  • Call witnesses.
  • Raise legal defences.
  • Challenge the admissibility or reliability of evidence.
The accused does not normally have to prove that they are innocent.
CARD 10 · RIGHT TO SILENCE

Presumption of Innocence and Right to Silence

The presumption of innocence is closely connected with the right to silence in many legal systems.

If the prosecution has the burden of proving guilt, an accused person should not ordinarily be required to provide a confession or prove their own innocence.

The right to silence protects the accused against being compelled to contribute to the prosecution's case, subject to the specific rules of the jurisdiction.

The relationship between the right to silence and the presumption of innocence varies between legal systems.

CARD 11 · CRIMINAL DEFENCES

Presumption of Innocence and Criminal Defences

The prosecution must first establish the elements of the offence according to the applicable rules.

Once a defence is properly raised, the legal system determines who bears the relevant evidential or persuasive burden.

Some defences operate by showing that an element of the offence has not been proved.

Other defences, such as self-defence or duress, may operate according to separate burden and proof rules.

The exact allocation depends on the legal system and the particular defence.
CARD 12 · MENS REA

Presumption of Innocence and Mens Rea

Mens rea is the mental element required for many criminal offences.

Where mens rea forms part of the offence, the prosecution generally must prove the required mental state.

For example, if an offence requires intention, the prosecution must establish the required intention according to the applicable legal test.

The accused should not simply be assumed to have possessed the required mental state. The prosecution must prove it where the law requires it.
CARD 13 · ACTUS REUS

Presumption of Innocence and Actus Reus

The actus reus is the physical or external element of a criminal offence.

The prosecution generally must establish that the accused committed the legally prohibited conduct or that the required circumstances existed.

The accused is not automatically assumed to have performed the prohibited act. The prosecution must establish the relevant physical elements through evidence.
CARD 14 · CIRCUMSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

Presumption of Innocence and Circumstantial Evidence

Criminal cases may sometimes rely heavily on circumstantial evidence.

This may include evidence concerning:

  • Location.
  • Communications.
  • Financial records.
  • Physical evidence.
  • Conduct before or after an event.

Circumstantial evidence may support an inference about what happened.

The court must assess such evidence carefully rather than assuming guilt because the circumstances appear suspicious.
CARD 15 · WITNESS EVIDENCE

Presumption of Innocence and Witness Evidence

Witness testimony may form an important part of a criminal case.

The court may need to assess:

  • The witness's reliability.
  • Consistency.
  • Opportunity to observe events.
  • Possible bias.
  • Accuracy of recollection.
  • Whether the testimony is supported by other evidence.
The existence of an accusation or witness statement does not automatically establish guilt.
CARD 16 · EVIDENCE

Presumption of Innocence and Evidence

The presumption of innocence does not prevent evidence from being presented against the accused.

The prosecution may present:

  • Witness testimony.
  • Documents.
  • Physical evidence.
  • Digital evidence.
  • Expert evidence.
  • Video or photographic material.
  • Statements made by the accused where legally admissible.
The court must determine whether the evidence is sufficient to establish guilt.
CARD 17 · ADMISSIBILITY

Admissibility of Evidence

Not every piece of information is necessarily admissible in a criminal trial.

Rules of evidence may restrict material that is:

  • Irrelevant.
  • Unreliable.
  • Unfairly prejudicial.
  • Obtained in violation of specific legal requirements.
  • Protected by a legal privilege.
The presumption of innocence reinforces the need for convictions to be based on legally usable evidence rather than mere suspicion.
CARD 18 · IMPARTIALITY

Judicial Impartiality

Judges must approach criminal cases impartially.

A judge should not assume that the accused is guilty simply because charges have been brought.

Judicial impartiality helps protect the presumption of innocence by ensuring that the case is decided according to evidence and law.

Judges may also be required to explain the burden and standard of proof to juries.

CARD 19 · JURY

Presumption of Innocence and the Jury

In jury trials, jurors must generally be directed to begin with the presumption that the accused is innocent.

They must then consider the evidence presented during the trial.

A jury should not convict because:

  • The accused appears suspicious.
  • The jury dislikes the accused.
  • The accused has failed to prove innocence.
  • A mere allegation exists.
The jury should convict only if the prosecution has proved the case according to the applicable standard.
CARD 20 · PRE-TRIAL DETENTION

Pre-Trial Detention

A person may sometimes be detained before trial.

Pre-trial detention does not necessarily mean that the person has been found guilty.

Courts may impose pre-trial detention for legally recognized reasons such as:

  • Risk of flight.
  • Risk of interference with witnesses.
  • Risk of further serious offending.
  • Other legally recognized grounds.
CARD 21 · BAIL

Presumption of Innocence and Bail

Bail is the temporary release of an accused person while criminal proceedings are ongoing, subject to conditions where required.

The existence of bail systems is closely connected to the principle that an accused person has not yet been convicted.

A decision to deny or grant bail is not necessarily a determination of guilt.

The applicable legal standards vary between jurisdictions.

CARD 22 · PUBLICITY

Pre-Trial Publicity

Public reporting can create challenges for the presumption of innocence.

Media reports may present allegations before a court has determined whether they are true.

Legal systems may respond through rules concerning:

  • Contempt of court.
  • Publication restrictions.
  • Reporting of ongoing proceedings.
  • Protection of juror impartiality.
  • Defamation.
  • Fair trial rights.
The balance between freedom of expression and fair trial rights varies between jurisdictions.
CARD 23 · PUBLIC STATEMENTS

Presumption of Innocence and Public Statements

Statements by police, prosecutors, government officials, or other public authorities may affect perceptions of guilt.

Authorities should generally distinguish between:

  • An allegation.
  • Evidence.
  • A charge.
  • A conviction.
Treating an accused person as though guilt has already been established can undermine the presumption of innocence.
CARD 24 · PREVIOUS CONVICTIONS

Previous Convictions and Character Evidence

Evidence of previous criminal convictions may sometimes be relevant in a later criminal case.

However, previous wrongdoing does not automatically prove that the accused committed the current offence.

Many legal systems therefore place restrictions on when previous convictions or prior misconduct may be presented.

Character Evidence

Character evidence concerns a person's general behavior, reputation, or previous conduct.

The presumption of innocence supports careful limits on reasoning that a person should be convicted because they appear to be a "bad person."
CARD 25 · BURDENS ON ACCUSED

Burdens on the Accused

Although the prosecution generally bears the burden of proving guilt, some legal systems permit limited burdens to be placed on the accused.

These may arise in relation to particular statutory defences or exceptions.

Human-rights law in some jurisdictions places limits on reverse burdens because they can affect the presumption of innocence.

The validity and scope of such burdens depend on the legal system.

CARD 26 · EVIDENTIAL & PERSUASIVE

Evidential and Persuasive Burdens

It is useful to distinguish between two different types of burden.

Evidential Burden

The responsibility of producing enough evidence to raise an issue.

Persuasive Burden

The responsibility of convincing the court of a particular proposition according to the required standard.

An accused person may have an evidential burden without having the final persuasive burden.
CARD 27 · REVERSE BURDENS

Presumption of Innocence and Reverse Burdens

A reverse burden occurs where the accused is required to establish or disprove a particular matter rather than leaving the entire burden on the prosecution.

Reverse burdens can raise concerns because they may appear to require the accused to prove innocence.

Courts in some jurisdictions therefore distinguish between:

  • Evidential burdens.
  • Persuasive burdens.
  • Provisions compatible with the presumption of innocence.
  • Provisions that place excessive pressure on the accused.
CARD 28 · HUMAN RIGHTS

Presumption of Innocence and Human Rights

The presumption of innocence is recognized as a fundamental human-rights principle.

Article 14(2) of the ICCPR provides that everyone charged with a criminal offence has the right to be presumed innocent until proved guilty according to law.

Article 6(2) of the ECHR contains a similar protection.

These provisions demonstrate that the presumption of innocence is not merely a procedural rule in many systems but also a recognized fundamental right.

CARD 29 · FAIR TRIAL

Presumption of Innocence and Fair Trial

The presumption of innocence forms part of the broader right to a fair trial.

A Fair Criminal Proceeding Generally Requires

  • An impartial decision-maker.
  • A meaningful opportunity to challenge the prosecution's case.
  • Adequate procedural safeguards.
  • Proper application of the burden and standard of proof.
  • Protection against arbitrary punishment.
The presumption of innocence helps ensure that criminal proceedings focus on proving guilt rather than requiring the accused to prove innocence.
CARD 30 · EQUALITY

Equality Before the Law

The principle applies regardless of the identity or status of the accused.

A person should not be presumed guilty because of:

  • Wealth or poverty.
  • Social position.
  • Reputation.
  • Occupation.
  • Nationality.
  • Previous allegations.
  • Public attention surrounding the case.
The prosecution must prove the case according to the same applicable legal standards.
CARD 31 · DISCRIMINATION

Presumption of Innocence and Discrimination

The presumption of innocence supports equal treatment in criminal proceedings.

A court should not assume that an accused person is guilty because of stereotypes or prejudice.

Evidence should be evaluated objectively and according to the applicable law.

Discriminatory assumptions may undermine both the presumption of innocence and the broader requirement of a fair trial.

CARD 32 · GUILTY PLEAS

Guilty Pleas and Confessions

Guilty Plea

A guilty plea is a formal admission by an accused person that they are guilty of the offence.

Legal systems establish procedural safeguards to ensure that guilty pleas are made properly and voluntarily.

Confessions

A confession may provide significant evidence in a criminal case.

The legal system may regulate:

  • How the confession was obtained.
  • Whether it was voluntary.
  • Whether the accused understood their rights.
  • Whether the confession is reliable.
CARD 33 · SILENCE

Presumption of Innocence and Silence at Trial

An accused person's decision not to testify does not automatically mean that they are guilty.

In many legal systems, the prosecution cannot simply rely on the accused's silence as proof that the charge is true.

The relationship between silence and the presumption of innocence depends on the applicable law.

Some jurisdictions permit limited legal consequences from silence in particular circumstances.

CARD 34 · STRICT LIABILITY

Presumption of Innocence and Strict Liability

Strict liability offences can raise particular questions concerning the presumption of innocence.

In a strict liability offence, the prosecution may not need to prove mens rea for one or more elements.

Strict liability does not mean that an accused person is automatically presumed guilty.

The prosecution must still prove the elements that the law requires.

Strict liability changes what the prosecution must prove by removing a particular fault requirement.

CARD 35 · APPEALS

Presumption of Innocence and Appeals

The presumption of innocence may remain relevant during appellate proceedings.

An appeal may challenge:

  • Whether the trial court correctly applied the burden of proof.
  • Whether the correct standard of proof was used.
  • Whether the judge properly directed the jury.
  • Whether evidence was improperly admitted.
  • Whether a legal rule unfairly affected the accused.
  • Whether the conviction was supported by sufficient evidence.
CARD 36 · WRONGFUL CONVICTIONS

Presumption of Innocence and Wrongful Convictions

The presumption of innocence plays an important role in reducing the risk of wrongful conviction.

Requiring the prosecution to prove guilt creates a safeguard against conviction based on:

  • Suspicion.
  • Rumor.
  • Prejudice.
  • Unsupported allegations.
  • Insufficient evidence.
No legal system can eliminate all possibility of wrongful conviction, but procedural protections aim to reduce that risk.
CARD 37 · REASONABLE DOUBT

Presumption of Innocence and Reasonable Doubt

Reasonable doubt is connected to the criminal standard of proof used in many legal systems.

Where the prosecution has failed to establish guilt to the required level, the accused should not be convicted.

The principle is not that every possible doubt requires acquittal. Instead, the court applies the legal standard established by the relevant jurisdiction.
CARD 38 · EXAMPLE

Example of the Presumption of Innocence

Imagine that a person is accused of stealing a valuable item.

The prosecution presents evidence suggesting that the accused was near the location where the item disappeared.

Simply being near the location does not automatically establish guilt.

The prosecution must prove the elements of the theft offence, including any required actus reus and mens rea.

If the evidence is insufficient to establish guilt according to the required criminal standard, the accused should not be convicted.

The presumption of innocence therefore requires the court to begin from the position that the accused is innocent and determine whether the prosecution has overcome that presumption through sufficient evidence.

CARD 39 · DIFFERENT SYSTEMS

Presumption of Innocence in Different Legal Systems

The presumption of innocence is recognized in many legal traditions, although its precise operation differs.

Common Law Systems

  • The prosecution's burden of proof.
  • The criminal standard of proof.
  • The right to silence.
  • Trial by an impartial court or jury.

Civil Law Systems

Civil law systems also recognize the principle, although procedural structures and terminology may differ.

International human-rights instruments provide additional protections that influence many national legal systems.
CARD 40 · STATUTORY INTERPRETATION

Presumption of Innocence and Statutory Interpretation

Courts may need to interpret legislation consistently with the presumption of innocence.

Questions may arise where legislation:

  • Places a burden on the accused.
  • Creates evidential presumptions.
  • Limits particular defences.
  • Establishes strict liability.
  • Alters ordinary criminal procedure.
Courts may interpret such provisions carefully to determine their effect on the accused's procedural rights.
CARD 41 · CRIMINAL PROCEDURE

Presumption of Innocence and Criminal Procedure

The principle influences many stages of criminal procedure, including:

  • Investigation.
  • Charging.
  • Bail.
  • Disclosure.
  • Trial.
  • Jury directions.
  • Sentencing.
  • Appeals.
At each stage, the legal system must distinguish between an allegation and established criminal guilt.

A person may be investigated, arrested, charged, or tried without having been convicted.

CARD 42 · SENTENCING

Presumption of Innocence and Sentencing

Sentencing normally occurs after a person has been convicted or has entered a valid guilty plea.

The presumption of innocence therefore continues to distinguish between:

  • Alleged conduct.
  • Proven criminal conduct.
  • Conduct relevant to sentencing under the applicable law.
The precise rules concerning unproved allegations and disputed conduct at sentencing vary between jurisdictions.
CARD 43 · CRIMINAL RECORDS

Presumption of Innocence and Criminal Records

A criminal record generally reflects convictions established through the criminal justice system.

An allegation, investigation, or charge is not necessarily equivalent to a conviction.

Treating unproven allegations as established criminal conduct can conflict with the principle underlying the presumption of innocence.

The legal treatment of arrest records, charges, and other information varies between jurisdictions.

CARD 44 · WHY IT MATTERS

Why the Presumption of Innocence Matters

The presumption of innocence helps:

  • Protect individuals from wrongful conviction.
  • Place the primary burden of proving guilt on the prosecution.
  • Support fair criminal trials.
  • Prevent accusations from being treated as established facts.
  • Protect the right to a fair hearing.
  • Reinforce the criminal standard of proof.
  • Limit arbitrary use of state power.
  • Support public confidence in the justice system.
It is therefore one of the most important principles in criminal procedure.
CARD 45 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

The presumption of innocence is the principle that a person accused of a crime must be treated as innocent unless and until their guilt is established according to law.

It places the primary burden of proving criminal responsibility on the prosecution.

Presumption of Innocence = An accused person is not guilty merely because they have been accused.

The Prosecution Generally Must Prove

  • The required actus reus.
  • The required mens rea.
  • Any other elements of the offence.
  • The case to the applicable criminal standard of proof.

The accused generally does not have to prove their innocence.

The presumption of innocence is closely connected to the burden of proof, standard of proof, right to a fair trial, right to silence, and criminal defences.

It is fundamental because criminal justice should punish only where guilt has been established through a lawful and fair process.
