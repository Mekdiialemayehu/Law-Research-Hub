Child Marriage

Understanding child marriage through international, African, and Ethiopian human-rights law.

Definition Why It Matters International Law African Framework Key Principles Barriers Ethiopia Legal Analysis Research Question Sources
Core legal question: What legal duties do states have to prevent child marriage, protect children at risk, and ensure that laws prohibiting child marriage are effective in practice?
01 · Definition

What is Child Marriage?

Child marriage generally refers to a formal marriage or informal union in which at least one of the parties is below the age of eighteen. International and regional human-rights bodies increasingly treat child marriage as a harmful practice that interferes with children's rights and disproportionately affects girls.

Child marriage is not only a question of age. It can also involve questions of consent, equality, education, health, personal development, protection from violence, and the child's ability to participate in decisions affecting their own life.

International human-rights standards emphasize that marriage should be based on free and full consent and that the minimum legal age of marriage should be eighteen for both girls and boys. The African Children's Charter expressly requires states to prohibit child marriage and to establish eighteen as the minimum age of marriage.

The central legal idea is simple: a child's rights do not disappear because a marriage has been arranged, celebrated, or socially accepted.
02 · Why It Matters

Why is Child Marriage a Human-Rights Issue?

Child marriage can interfere with a wide range of human rights, including rights to education, health, equality, dignity, development, protection from violence and exploitation, and participation in decisions affecting the child.

The Convention on the Rights of the Child requires states to make the best interests of the child a primary consideration, protect children from violence and exploitation, and take effective measures against harmful traditional practices. Child marriage is addressed by international bodies within this broader child-protection framework.

The issue is especially significant for girls because child marriage can reinforce unequal gender roles and limit educational, economic, and social opportunities. CEDAW therefore addresses both discrimination against women and the right to freely choose a spouse and enter marriage only with free and full consent.

The problem is also global. UNICEF estimates that around 640 million girls and women alive today were married in childhood, while millions of girls continue to enter child marriages each year.

03 · International Legal Framework

International Protection

Convention on the Rights of the Child · Articles 3, 12, 19, 24 and 28

Protection of the Child

The Convention on the Rights of the Child establishes a broad framework for children's rights. Its principles include the best interests of the child, the child's right to express views in matters affecting them, protection from violence and exploitation, the right to health, and the right to education. These guarantees are highly relevant when assessing the effects of child marriage.

Article 24 also requires states to take effective and appropriate measures to abolish traditional practices prejudicial to children's health.

Ethiopia ratified the Convention on the Rights of the Child in 1991.

CEDAW · Article 16

Equality and Free Consent in Marriage

Article 16 of CEDAW requires states to eliminate discrimination against women in marriage and family relations. It protects the equal right to enter into marriage, the right freely to choose a spouse, and the right to enter marriage only with free and full consent.

CEDAW's interpretation of marriage rights has also emphasized that the minimum age of marriage should be eighteen for both men and women and that child marriage should not be treated as an acceptable exception to equality and consent.

Read CEDAW →

CEDAW General Recommendation No. 31 · CRC General Comment No. 18

Child Marriage as a Harmful Practice

The joint interpretation by the CEDAW Committee and the Committee on the Rights of the Child identifies child marriage as a harmful practice and calls for legislative, institutional and educational measures to prevent it.

The approach goes beyond simply passing a law. States are expected to address the social, economic, cultural and institutional conditions that allow child marriage to continue.

Read the Joint General Recommendation / General Comment →

Universal Human Rights Principle

Free and Full Consent

Article 16(2) of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights states that marriage shall be entered into only with the free and full consent of the intending spouses.

This principle is important in child-marriage analysis because legal recognition of a marriage cannot substitute for the human-rights requirement that marriage be based on genuine consent.

04 · African Human-Rights Law

The African Legal Framework

African Children's Charter · Article 21

Express Prohibition of Child Marriage

Article 21 of the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child requires states to eliminate harmful social and cultural practices affecting children's welfare, dignity, growth and development.

More specifically, Article 21(2) requires child marriage and the betrothal of girls and boys to be prohibited and requires effective action, including legislation, to specify eighteen as the minimum age of marriage and to make registration of marriages compulsory.

Read the African Children's Charter →

Maputo Protocol · Article 6

Marriage, Consent, and Equality

Article 6 of the Protocol to the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights on the Rights of Women in Africa requires states to guarantee equality in marriage and to ensure that no marriage occurs without the free and full consent of both parties.

The Protocol also establishes eighteen as the minimum age of marriage for women and requires marriages to be recorded and registered in accordance with national law.

Ethiopia is a party to the Maputo Protocol, making its regional standards directly relevant to the legal analysis of women's and girls' rights in Ethiopia.

Read the Maputo Protocol →

African Human-Rights Interpretation

Ending Child Marriage in Africa

The African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights and the African Committee of Experts on the Rights and Welfare of the Child have emphasized that child marriage violates a range of rights, including rights to health, education, dignity, development, protection from abuse and equality.

Their Joint General Comment explains that states must use legislation, institutions, education, awareness programmes and other measures to prevent child marriage and protect children affected by it.

05 · Key Legal Principles

What Duties Can States Have?

Prohibition

States should establish a clear minimum age of marriage and prohibit child marriage in law, including informal arrangements that effectively create marital unions involving children.

Protection

Authorities should take reasonable measures to protect children who are known or likely to be at risk of being forced or placed into a child marriage.

Free and Full Consent

Marriage must be based on genuine, free and full consent rather than pressure, coercion, threats, or arrangements imposed by others.

Best Interests of the Child

In actions concerning children, public authorities must treat the child's best interests as a primary consideration.

Equality and Non-Discrimination

Girls must receive equal protection of the law, and legal systems should not preserve practices that discriminate against girls because of their sex.

Effective Remedies

Children affected by child marriage should have access to child-sensitive reporting mechanisms, legal protection, support services, and effective remedies.

06 · Understanding the Problem

Why Can Child Marriage Persist?

Child marriage can continue even where laws prohibit it. Human-rights analysis therefore requires looking at the social, economic, cultural and institutional conditions surrounding the law.

Social norms
Expectations about girls' roles, family honour, gender roles, and the preferred age for marriage can make child marriage appear socially normal even where it is legally prohibited.
Economic pressure
Poverty and economic insecurity can encourage families to view marriage as a way of reducing household costs or obtaining financial or social support.
Interrupted education
When girls have limited access to safe and affordable education, marriage may be presented as one of the few expected pathways available to them.
Weak registration systems
Where births and marriages are not consistently registered, determining a child's age and identifying unlawful marriages can become more difficult.
Institutional inaction
Laws can be ineffective when police, courts, local officials, community authorities or other institutions do not consistently enforce them.
Conflict and displacement
Conflict, displacement, drought and other emergencies can increase economic insecurity and disrupt education and child-protection systems, potentially increasing risks of child marriage.
07 · Ethiopia

Child Marriage in Ethiopia

Ethiopia has made important legal and policy progress in addressing child marriage, but the practice remains a significant human-rights issue. UNICEF's 2024 Ethiopia country profile reports that about 40 percent of women aged 20–24 had married before age eighteen, with substantial regional differences.

The same profile reports that about 19.9 million girls and women living in Ethiopia had been married before eighteen, illustrating the scale of the issue across generations.

More recent international monitoring also shows that the legal framework is not applied identically across all parts of the country. In 2026, the UN Committee on the Rights of the Child expressed concern that child marriage remained legal and prevalent in many rural areas and was particularly widespread in Afar and Somali Regional States.

Article 34 · Marital, Personal and Family Rights

Article 34 of the FDRE Constitution recognizes the right of men and women who have attained the legally defined marriageable age to marry and found a family. It also states that marriage shall be entered into only with the free and full consent of the intending spouses.

Article 35 · Rights of Women

Article 35 guarantees women equal rights and protections and requires the state to enforce women's right to eliminate the influence of harmful customs. It expressly prohibits laws, customs and practices that oppress women or cause bodily or mental harm.

Article 36 · Rights of Children

Article 36 provides children with rights including protection from exploitative practices and establishes the best interests of the child as a primary consideration in actions concerning children.

Article 9 · Supremacy of the Constitution and International Law

Article 9(4) states that international agreements ratified by Ethiopia are an integral part of the law of the land. Article 13 also requires the interpretation of constitutional rights in accordance with international human-rights instruments adopted by Ethiopia.

Revised Family Code · Article 7

The federal Revised Family Code establishes eighteen as the general minimum age of marriage for both men and women. However, Article 7(2) historically allowed the Minister of Justice, for serious cause, to grant a dispensation of up to two years.

This exception is legally important. It means that the federal framework has not always reflected the international human-rights standard of eighteen without exception. The UN Committee on the Rights of the Child reiterated in 2026 that Ethiopia should ensure that the minimum age of marriage is eighteen without exception and in all regions.

Criminal Code · Article 648

Ethiopia's Criminal Code contains a specific offence concerning early marriage. Article 648 provides criminal penalties for concluding marriage with a minor outside circumstances permitted by the relevant Family Code provisions.

Why the Ethiopian context matters

Ethiopia illustrates a central problem in human-rights law: the difference between a legal rule and its practical effect. A country may have a statutory minimum age, criminal penalties, constitutional protections, and international obligations, yet child marriage can continue where exceptions, regional differences, weak registration, poverty, social norms, or limited institutional enforcement remain.

The legal question is therefore not simply whether child marriage is prohibited in a statute. It is whether the entire legal and institutional system actually prevents children from being married before eighteen, protects children who are at risk, and provides effective remedies when violations occur.

08 · Legal Analysis

Law on Paper vs. Protection in Practice

Child marriage demonstrates why human-rights analysis must examine both formal legal rules and the way those rules operate in real life.

Legal promise Practical question
The minimum age of marriage should be eighteen. Is eighteen actually the minimum age in every region, without legal exceptions?
Marriage requires free and full consent. Does the child genuinely have the ability to refuse the marriage without pressure or coercion?
Children have a right to education. Can girls remain in school rather than being removed from education because of marriage?
Children are entitled to protection from exploitation and harmful practices. Do authorities intervene when they know that a child is at risk?
Girls are entitled to equality and non-discrimination. Are laws and social institutions challenging practices that place girls at greater risk?
Children should have access to justice and remedies. Can children safely report a proposed or completed child marriage and obtain effective protection?
A law can exist on paper and still fail to protect a child in practice. Human-rights law asks whether the protection is real, accessible, enforceable, and effective.
09 · Research Question

Think Like a Human-Rights Researcher

When a state has laws prohibiting child marriage, when does a failure to enforce those laws become a violation of human rights?

A strong legal analysis should ask three things: What rights are affected? What legal duties does the state have? Is the state's protection effective in practice?
