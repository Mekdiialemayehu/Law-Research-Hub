DUE PROCESS
● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ●
CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Due Process?

Due process refers to the legal requirement that the State and other public authorities must follow fair and lawful procedures when making decisions that affect a person's rights, liberty, property, or other protected interests.

It answers the question:

Was the person treated fairly and according to law?

Due process is a fundamental principle of the Rule of Law.

It means that government authorities should not be able to deprive a person of important rights or interests arbitrarily or without following established legal procedures.

The precise meaning and scope of due process varies between legal systems.

CARD 2 · PURPOSE

Purpose of Due Process

The main purpose of due process is to protect individuals from arbitrary or unfair government action.

It helps ensure that:

  • Laws and legal procedures are properly followed.
  • Individuals have an opportunity to be heard.
  • Decisions are made by appropriate authorities.
  • People are given notice of proceedings affecting them.
  • Individuals have an opportunity to respond to allegations or claims.
  • Courts and other decision-makers act fairly.
  • Government power is subject to legal limits.
Due process therefore provides an important safeguard against abuse of governmental authority.
CARD 3 · PROCEDURAL DUE PROCESS

Procedural Due Process

Procedural due process concerns how a legal decision is made.

It requires authorities to use fair procedures before taking action that significantly affects a person's rights or interests.

Depending on the circumstances, it may include:

  • Notice of the proceedings or proposed action.
  • An opportunity to present one's side of the case.
  • An opportunity to respond to evidence or allegations.
  • A hearing before an appropriate decision-maker.
  • An impartial decision-maker.
  • A decision based on relevant evidence.
  • Access to appropriate legal remedies or appeals.
CARD 4 · SUBSTANTIVE DUE PROCESS

Substantive Due Process

Substantive due process concerns what the government is permitted to do, rather than only the procedures it follows.

The concept is particularly associated with constitutional law in the United States.

Under a substantive due process approach, certain government actions may be unlawful because they improperly interfere with fundamental rights, even if the government has followed apparently proper procedures.

The meaning and scope of substantive due process varies considerably between legal systems.
CARD 5 · COMPARISON

Procedural and Substantive Due Process

Procedural Due Process

  • Focuses on how a decision is made.
  • Requires fair legal procedures.
  • May require notice and a hearing.
  • Is concerned with fairness of the process.

Substantive Due Process

  • Focuses on what government action is permitted.
  • Concerns protection of certain fundamental rights.
  • May examine whether interference with a right is constitutionally permissible.
  • Concerns the legality or constitutionality of the government action itself.

The distinction is particularly important in constitutional law.

CARD 6 · CRIMINAL PROCEEDINGS

Due Process in Criminal Proceedings

Due process is especially important in criminal cases because criminal proceedings can result in serious restrictions on a person's liberty.

Depending on the jurisdiction, due process protections may include:

  • Being informed of the accusation.
  • A fair and public hearing.
  • An impartial tribunal.
  • The opportunity to obtain legal assistance.
  • The opportunity to challenge evidence.
  • The opportunity to present a defence.
  • Protection against unlawful detention.
  • The right to appeal where provided by law.
These protections help prevent criminal punishment from being imposed through arbitrary or unfair procedures.
CARD 7 · RIGHT TO BE HEARD

Due Process and the Right to Be Heard

An important element of procedural fairness is the opportunity to be heard.

A person affected by a legal decision should generally have an opportunity to present relevant arguments, evidence, or explanations where the law requires such an opportunity.

A person should not normally be condemned without being given a meaningful opportunity to respond.

For example, if an authority intends to impose a legal penalty on a person, due process may require the person to receive notice of the proposed action and an opportunity to challenge it.

CARD 8 · NOTICE & IMPARTIALITY

Notice and an Impartial Decision-Maker

Due Process and Notice

Notice is an important part of fair procedure.

A person generally cannot meaningfully defend their rights if they do not know that legal proceedings or government action are taking place.

Notice may involve informing a person about:

  • The nature of the proceedings.
  • The allegations or claims against them.
  • A hearing date.
  • A proposed government decision.
  • The legal consequences of failing to respond.

Impartial Decision-Maker

Due process may require decisions to be made by an impartial and independent decision-maker.

CARD 9 · REPRESENTATION & EVIDENCE

Legal Representation and Evidence

Legal Representation

In certain legal proceedings, due process may include rights relating to legal representation.

Legal assistance is particularly significant when a person faces serious criminal consequences or when legal issues are sufficiently complex that effective participation would otherwise be difficult.

Evidence

Due process is closely connected to the fair treatment of evidence.

  • Knowing the evidence being relied upon.
  • Challenging opposing evidence.
  • Presenting relevant evidence.
  • Questioning witnesses where appropriate.
  • Making arguments about the meaning or reliability of evidence.
CARD 10 · PRESUMPTION & FAIR TRIAL

Due Process and Fair Trial Rights

Presumption of Innocence

Due process is closely connected to the presumption of innocence in criminal proceedings.

A person accused of a crime should not be treated as legally guilty merely because an accusation has been made.

The prosecution generally has the responsibility of proving guilt according to the applicable legal standard.

Fair Trial Protections

  • An independent and impartial tribunal.
  • A public hearing, subject to lawful exceptions.
  • Reasonable notice of the proceedings.
  • Adequate time and facilities to prepare a defence.
  • Legal assistance where applicable.
  • The opportunity to present and challenge evidence.
  • A decision based on law and evidence.
  • The ability to seek review or appeal where provided by law.
CARD 11 · CIVIL & ADMINISTRATIVE

Due Process Beyond Criminal Cases

Due Process in Civil Proceedings

Due process can also apply to civil proceedings involving matters such as:

  • Property.
  • Contracts.
  • Family matters.
  • Compensation.
  • Other legal rights and obligations.

For example, if a person is involved in a civil lawsuit, procedural fairness may require appropriate notice and an opportunity to respond to the opposing party's claim.

Administrative Decisions

Government agencies and administrative authorities may also be required to follow fair procedures.

Examples include decisions concerning licences, benefits, permits, professional qualifications, disciplinary measures, and government services.

CARD 12 · RULE OF LAW & JUDICIAL REVIEW

Due Process and Government Power

Rule of Law

The Rule of Law requires government power to be exercised according to established legal rules rather than personal preference or arbitrary authority.

Due process supports this principle by requiring legal procedures to be followed when government action affects individuals.

Judicial Review

Judicial review can provide a mechanism for challenging unlawful government action.

A court may examine whether a public authority:

  • Had legal authority to make the decision.
  • Followed required procedures.
  • Acted fairly.
  • Considered relevant matters.
  • Avoided acting arbitrarily.
  • Complied with constitutional or statutory requirements.
CARD 13 · HUMAN RIGHTS & EXAMPLE

Due Process and Human Rights

Due process is strongly connected to international human rights law.

Article 10 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights protects the right to a full and public hearing by an independent and impartial tribunal in the determination of rights and obligations and of criminal charges.

Article 11 also protects the presumption of innocence and requires criminal guilt to be established according to law.

Example

Imagine that a government authority intends to revoke a person's professional licence.

  1. Inform the person of the proposed action.
  2. Explain the legal basis for the decision.
  3. Give the person an opportunity to respond.
  4. Consider the relevant evidence and arguments.
  5. Have the matter decided by an appropriate authority.
  6. Provide reasons for the decision where required.
  7. Allow an appeal or judicial review where provided by law.
CARD 14 · REMEDIES & KEY TAKEAWAY

Why Due Process Matters

Due process helps:

  • Protect individual rights and liberties.
  • Prevent arbitrary government action.
  • Promote fair and lawful decision-making.
  • Ensure people have an opportunity to be heard.
  • Support judicial independence and impartiality.
  • Strengthen the Rule of Law.
  • Protect fair trial rights.
  • Provide mechanisms for challenging unlawful decisions.
  • Limit the misuse of government power.

Possible Remedies

Depending on the jurisdiction and circumstances, remedies may include:

  • Reconsideration of a decision.
  • A new hearing.
  • Judicial review.
  • An order requiring an authority to follow the correct procedure.
  • Reversal or cancellation of an unlawful decision.
  • Appeal to a higher court.
Due Process = Fair procedures + lawful decision-making.

Procedural due process focuses mainly on how a decision is made, while substantive due process, particularly in United States constitutional law, concerns whether government action itself improperly interferes with protected rights.

Due process is essential to the Rule of Law because it prevents government authorities from exercising power arbitrarily and helps ensure that legal decisions are made through fair, lawful, and accountable procedures.
