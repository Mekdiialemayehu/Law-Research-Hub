Opuz v. Turkey (2009)

European Court of Human Rights | Human Rights Law

Case Overview

Case Name: Opuz v. Turkey (2009)

Court: European Court of Human Rights (ECHR)

Area of Law: Human Rights Law

Key Articles: Article 2, Article 3 and Article 14 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

Key Principle: A State has a positive obligation to take reasonable preventive measures when authorities know, or ought to know, of a real and immediate risk of serious harm from domestic violence.

Facts of the Case

Nahide Opuz married H.O. in 1995 and soon became the victim of repeated domestic violence. Over several years, H.O. physically assaulted Opuz and her mother, including serious attacks, threats, and violent behaviour.

Although authorities received multiple complaints, effective protection was not provided. Many proceedings ended after complaints were withdrawn because Opuz and her mother feared retaliation.

In March 2002, H.O. killed Opuz's mother. He was convicted, but his sentence was reduced and he was later released pending appeal.

Opuz brought her case before the European Court of Human Rights, arguing that Turkey failed to protect her and her mother from domestic violence.

Procedural History

Before applying to the ECHR, Opuz repeatedly reported violence to Turkish authorities. Although investigations were opened, many proceedings ended after complaints were withdrawn.

After her mother's murder and the failure of domestic remedies, Opuz applied to the European Court of Human Rights. The Court delivered its judgment on 9 June 2009.

Key Legal Issue

Whether Turkey's failure to prevent repeated domestic violence and protect Nahide Opuz and her mother violated Articles 2, 3, and 14 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

Legal Arguments

Opuz's Arguments

  • Turkey violated Article 2 by failing to protect her mother from a known risk of violence.
  • Turkey violated Article 3 by failing to protect her from years of severe domestic abuse.
  • Turkey violated Article 14 because the ineffective response reflected gender discrimination.

Turkey's Argument

The Turkish government argued that Opuz had not exhausted domestic remedies and that authorities had taken steps based on the complaints received. They argued that prosecution was limited because complaints were withdrawn.

Legal Question Reformulated

The Court had to decide whether Turkey violated Articles 2, 3, and 14 of the European Convention on Human Rights by failing to protect Nahide Opuz and her mother from domestic violence despite being aware of the ongoing abuse.

Judgment and Reasoning

Decision

The European Court of Human Rights ruled in favour of Nahide Opuz and held that Turkey had violated the European Convention on Human Rights.

  • Article 2 (Right to Life): The Court found that Turkey failed to take adequate preventive measures despite authorities knowing, or should have known, of a real and immediate risk to Opuz's mother's life.
  • Article 3 (Prohibition of Inhuman or Degrading Treatment): The Court held that Turkey failed to protect Opuz from years of serious domestic violence.
  • Article 14 (Prohibition of Discrimination): The Court found that the failure of authorities to respond effectively to domestic violence amounted to gender-based discrimination.

Key Judicial Reasoning

The Court held that States have a positive obligation to protect individuals from domestic violence, even when the violence is committed by private individuals.

The authorities knew or should have known that Opuz and her mother faced a real and immediate risk of serious violence, but they failed to take effective protective measures.

The Court also found that the authorities' repeated failure to respond effectively reflected a broader pattern of gender-based discrimination against women.

Legal Impact

  • Established that domestic violence can create State responsibility under the European Convention on Human Rights.
  • Recognised domestic violence as a human rights violation rather than a private family matter.
  • Expanded the understanding of Article 14 by recognising that failure to protect women from domestic violence may amount to gender discrimination.
  • Strengthened legal protection for victims of domestic violence and influenced later human rights standards.
  • Influenced international discussions on violence against women and contributed to stronger legal frameworks addressing domestic violence.

Broader Social Impact

  • Raised awareness that domestic violence is a serious human rights issue affecting society as a whole.
  • Recognised victims' right to protection and justice from State authorities.
  • Encouraged stronger laws, prevention measures, and improved responses from police and courts.
  • Helped change social attitudes by challenging the idea that domestic violence is only a private family matter.

Critical Evaluation

Strengths

  • The judgment was a landmark decision because it clearly recognised domestic violence as a human rights violation.
  • It placed responsibility on governments when authorities fail to protect vulnerable individuals.
  • It strengthened the legal interpretation of non-discrimination by recognising gender-based failures in protection.

Criticisms

  • Implementation remains inconsistent because many countries still struggle to prevent domestic violence effectively.
  • The judgment provided limited guidance on exactly what preventive measures States must take.
  • Individual cases before international courts cannot provide recognition or justice to every victim facing similar situations.

Overall Assessment: Opuz v. Turkey was a major development in human rights law because it transformed domestic violence from a private issue into a matter of State responsibility. However, its effectiveness depends on governments implementing strong protection systems and addressing the causes of gender-based violence.

Final Conclusion

Opuz v. Turkey remains a landmark case in international human rights law because it established that States have a duty to protect individuals from domestic violence and can be held responsible when they fail to do so.

The judgment strengthened protections for women and changed the understanding of domestic violence by recognising it as a serious human rights issue. Although challenges remain in enforcement and prevention, the case continues to influence laws, policies, and attitudes toward gender-based violence worldwide.
