CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Consideration?

Consideration is something of legal value exchanged between parties in support of a contractual promise.

In traditional common-law contract law, a promise will generally not be enforceable as a simple contract unless it is supported by consideration, subject to important exceptions.

Consideration asks:

“What has each party given or promised in exchange for the other party's promise?”

A simple way to remember it is:

Consideration = the price paid for a promise.

Consideration does not necessarily mean money. It can include promises, services, acts, or agreements to refrain from exercising a legal right.

CARD 2 · CONTRACT FORMATION

Consideration and Contract Formation

A traditional common-law contract may involve several requirements.

  • Offer
  • Acceptance
  • Consideration
  • Intention to create legal relations
  • Capacity
  • Certainty
  • Legality
Offer + Acceptance = Agreement

Agreement + Consideration + Other Requirements = Potentially Enforceable Contract

Consideration helps distinguish an enforceable bargain from a purely voluntary promise.

For example, if A promises to sell a bicycle to B for $300 and B promises to pay $300, the promises provide consideration for one another.

CARD 3 · TYPES

Types of Consideration

Executory Consideration

Executory consideration consists of promises to perform obligations in the future.

For example, A promises to deliver a computer next month while B promises to pay $1,000 next month. Neither party has performed yet, but both have made legally relevant promises.

Executed Consideration

Executed consideration occurs when the required act has been performed in response to a promise.

This is particularly relevant to unilateral contracts.

Executory = promise to perform.

Executed = required act performed.
CARD 4 · PAST CONSIDERATION

Past Consideration

Past consideration is an act performed before the promise for which consideration is said to support.

The traditional common-law rule is:

Past consideration is generally not good consideration.

For example, B helps A move house without asking for payment. The next day, A promises to pay B $100 for the help.

B may have difficulty enforcing the promise because the assistance was already completed before A made the promise.

The act therefore occurred too early to form part of the bargain in the traditional analysis.

CARD 5 · EXCEPTION

Exception to Past Consideration

The rule against past consideration is subject to an important exception in some common-law systems.

A later promise may sometimes be enforceable where:

  • The act was performed at the promisor's request.
  • The parties understood that the act would be rewarded or paid for.
  • The subsequent promise would have been enforceable if it had been made before the act was performed.

The precise requirements depend on the applicable jurisdiction.

Past consideration is generally invalid, but a previous act can sometimes support a later promise when the circumstances satisfy an established exception.
CARD 6 · SUFFICIENCY

Sufficiency of Consideration

Consideration must generally have legal value.

However, it does not necessarily need to have substantial financial value.

Sufficient consideration = consideration that the law recognises as having legal value.

For example, a promise to refrain from exercising a legal right can potentially constitute sufficient consideration even if no money changes hands.

The key question is whether the law recognises the thing given or promised as having legal value.

CARD 7 · ADEQUACY

Adequacy of Consideration

Adequacy asks whether the economic value exchanged by the parties is roughly equal.

Traditional common-law courts generally do not require consideration to be economically adequate.

For example, A may sell a valuable painting to B for $10. The fact that the painting is worth much more does not automatically mean that there is no consideration.

Adequacy: Is the exchange economically equal?

Sufficiency: Does the thing given have legal value?

Other doctrines, such as fraud, duress, undue influence, or unconscionability, may become relevant in particular circumstances.

CARD 8 · FORBEARANCE

Forbearance as Consideration

Forbearance means refraining from doing something.

An agreement not to exercise a legal right can sometimes constitute consideration.

For example, A owes B $2,000. B agrees to delay bringing a genuine legal claim for six months if A follows a new payment schedule.

B's agreement to delay enforcement may constitute consideration where the applicable legal requirements are satisfied.

Forbearance can therefore have legal value even though it involves choosing not to take an action.

CARD 9 · EXISTING DUTY

Performance of an Existing Duty

A difficult consideration issue arises when a person promises to do something they are already legally required to do.

The traditional rule is that performance of an existing legal duty is generally not sufficient consideration for an additional promise.

A similar problem can occur where a person is already contractually required to perform certain work.

A party generally cannot automatically claim additional payment merely for doing exactly what they were already legally or contractually required to do.

However, important exceptions and modern developments exist.

CARD 10 · PRACTICAL BENEFIT

Practical Benefit

Some common-law cases recognise that performing an existing contractual obligation may, in certain circumstances, provide consideration where the other party receives a practical benefit.

Examples of Practical Benefits

  • Avoiding delay.
  • Avoiding additional expense.
  • Maintaining a commercial relationship.
  • Receiving commercially useful performance.

For example, a party may agree to pay more for an existing performance because doing so provides a practical commercial benefit.

The practical-benefit approach is subject to limitations and does not automatically make every contractual variation enforceable.
CARD 11 · THIRD PARTY DUTY

Existing Duty to a Third Party

The identity of the person to whom an existing duty is owed can matter.

A promise to perform an existing duty owed to a third party can sometimes constitute valid consideration for a new promise.

For example:

A is contractually required to perform a service for B. C promises A $500 if A performs that service.

A's performance of the existing duty owed to B may provide consideration for C's promise under traditional common-law principles.

This demonstrates that an existing duty owed to someone else may be treated differently from an existing duty owed directly to the promisor.

CARD 12 · UNILATERAL CONTRACTS

Consideration in Unilateral Contracts

Consideration operates differently in a unilateral contract.

The offeror promises something in return for the performance of a specified act.

Example:

“I will pay $500 to anyone who returns my lost phone.”

The person who returns the phone does not necessarily make a return promise.

Instead, the requested performance supplies the consideration.

In a unilateral contract, performance of the requested act can constitute executed consideration.
CARD 13 · ESTOPPEL

Promissory Estoppel

Promissory estoppel is an equitable doctrine that can sometimes operate where ordinary consideration is absent or where a party has relied on a promise.

It may become relevant where:

  • A clear promise was made.
  • The other party relied on the promise.
  • It would be inequitable or unfair to allow the promisor to act inconsistently with it.
Promissory estoppel should not simply be treated as a complete replacement for consideration. Its requirements and effects depend on the legal system.
CARD 14 · VARIATION & DEBT

Contract Variation and Part Payment of Debt

Contract Variation

Consideration can become important when parties attempt to change an existing contract.

For example, a builder agrees to complete work for $100,000 and later asks for another $10,000 to perform the same obligations.

The question is whether new consideration supports the additional payment.

Part Payment of Debt

Traditional common-law rules can also create difficulties where a creditor agrees to accept less than the full amount of an existing debt.

For example, A owes B $1,000 and B agrees to accept $700 in full settlement.

Modern legislation, case law, and equitable doctrines can modify traditional rules, so the precise legal position depends on the jurisdiction.
CARD 15 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

Consideration = Something of legal value exchanged for a promise.

Consideration can consist of:

  • Money
  • Goods
  • Services
  • Promises
  • Forbearance
  • Performance of an agreed act

Important Rules

  • Consideration must generally be sufficient, but it does not usually have to be economically adequate.
  • Past consideration is generally not good consideration.
  • Performance of an existing duty may not constitute fresh consideration, subject to exceptions.
  • Forbearance can constitute consideration.
  • A promise without consideration may be unenforceable as a simple contract, subject to exceptions.
The key question is not simply “Did someone make a promise?”

It is:

“What, if anything, was given or promised in exchange for that promise?”
