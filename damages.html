CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Are Damages?

Damages are a monetary remedy awarded by a court to compensate a person who has suffered legally recognized loss because of another person's wrongful conduct.

Damages can arise in several areas of private law, including contract law, tort law, negligence, and property law.

The purpose of damages is generally to provide compensation rather than punishment.

This principle answers the question:

What financial compensation should the law provide for a person's legally recognized loss?

The type and amount of damages depend on the legal claim, the nature of the loss, and the applicable legal system.

CARD 2 · CONTRACT & TORT

Damages in Contract and Tort

Contract Law

In contract law, damages generally aim to compensate the innocent party for loss caused by the breach of contract.

The central objective is often to place the claimant in approximately the position they would have been in if the contract had been properly performed.

For example, A agrees to deliver goods worth $5,000 to B but breaches the contract. B may potentially recover legally recognized losses caused by the breach.

Tort Law

In tort law, damages generally aim to compensate the claimant for harm caused by the defendant's wrongful conduct.

In negligence, damages may compensate for physical injury, property damage, medical expenses, lost income, and other legally recognized losses.

CARD 3 · COMPENSATORY DAMAGES

Compensatory Damages

Compensatory damages are damages intended to compensate for legally recognized loss.

They are the most common form of damages in civil law.

They may compensate for:

  • Financial losses.
  • Physical injury.
  • Property damage.
  • Certain non-financial harms.
The precise categories of compensable loss vary between jurisdictions.

Damages vs. Fines

Damages are generally awarded to compensate a claimant. Fines are generally imposed as a form of punishment by a public authority, often in criminal or regulatory proceedings.

For example, a negligent driver may be ordered to pay damages to an injured person while separately receiving a fine for violating traffic law.

CARD 4 · PERSONAL INJURY

Damages for Personal Injury

Where negligence causes physical injury, damages may compensate for various consequences of the injury.

  • Medical expenses.
  • Rehabilitation costs.
  • Lost earnings.
  • Reduced future earning capacity.
  • Pain and suffering.
  • Other legally recognized losses.

The claimant generally must establish that the losses were caused by the defendant's wrongful conduct.

Medical Expenses

Recoverable medical costs may include reasonable expenses relating to emergency treatment, hospital care, medication, rehabilitation, and necessary future treatment, depending on the applicable law.

Lost Income

An injury may prevent a person from working. Damages may therefore compensate for income lost because of the defendant's conduct.

CARD 5 · EARNING CAPACITY & PROPERTY

Loss of Earning Capacity & Property Damage

Loss of Earning Capacity

A person may suffer a reduction in their ability to earn income even if they have not completely stopped working.

For example, an injury may prevent someone from performing certain types of work. A court may therefore consider whether the person's future earning capacity has been reduced.

Property Damage

Damages can also compensate for damage to property.

For example, if a negligent driver crashes into another person's car, the owner may potentially recover the reasonable cost of repair or, in appropriate circumstances, the value of the property.

The precise measure of property damage depends on the circumstances and applicable law.
CARD 6 · NON-ECONOMIC LOSS

Non-Economic Loss

Not all losses are financial. Some legal systems recognize damages for non-economic harm, particularly in personal-injury cases.

Examples include:

  • Pain and suffering.
  • Loss of enjoyment of life.
  • Emotional distress.
  • Loss of function.
  • Psychological injury where legally recognized.

Pain and Suffering

Courts may consider the severity of the injury, duration of symptoms, medical treatment, recovery period, long-term effects, and impact on daily life.

Loss of Enjoyment of Life

Depending on the legal system, damages may recognize a reduced ability to participate in activities previously enjoyed, such as sports, hobbies, travel, or ordinary activities.

CARD 7 · SPECIAL CATEGORIES

Nominal, Aggravated & Punitive Damages

Nominal Damages

Nominal damages are a small amount awarded where a legal right has been violated but the claimant has not established substantial financial or other compensable loss.

They recognize that a legal wrong occurred and are more commonly associated with certain causes of action than with ordinary negligence claims.

Aggravated Damages

Some legal systems recognize aggravated damages where the defendant's conduct or the manner in which the wrongdoing was committed causes additional distress, humiliation, or injury to the claimant.

These damages remain compensatory rather than primarily punitive.

Punitive or Exemplary Damages

Punitive damages, sometimes called exemplary damages, are intended partly to punish exceptionally wrongful conduct and deter similar conduct.

They are generally exceptional and subject to specific legal requirements.

CARD 8 · CONTRACT DAMAGES

Expectation, Reliance & Consequential Damages

Expectation Damages

Expectation damages aim to give the claimant the benefit of the bargain. They generally seek to place the claimant in approximately the position they would have occupied if the contract had been performed.

For example, A agrees to sell B goods for $1,000. A breaches, and B must purchase equivalent goods for $1,300. The $300 difference may potentially represent expectation loss.

Reliance Damages

Reliance damages compensate expenses or losses incurred because a person relied on the contract.

For example, B spends $2,000 preparing to perform a contract, but A later breaches. Depending on the circumstances, B may seek recovery of legally recognized reliance losses.

Consequential Damages

A breach or tort can sometimes cause losses beyond the immediate harm. Recovery may depend on causation, foreseeability, remoteness, and mitigation.

CARD 9 · CAUSATION

Causation and Damages

A claimant generally cannot recover damages for losses that were not caused by the defendant's wrongful conduct.

Did the wrongful act cause the claimed loss?

This is an important connection between causation and damages.

For example, if an accident causes a broken arm, medical treatment associated with that injury may be connected to the accident.

A completely unrelated expense would generally not be recoverable merely because it occurred after the accident.

Why Causation Matters

Causation prevents a defendant from being held responsible for losses that are unrelated to their wrongful conduct.

A legally recognized loss normally needs to be sufficiently connected to the wrongful conduct before compensation can be awarded.
CARD 10 · REMOTENESS & FORESEEABILITY

Remoteness and Foreseeability

Remoteness

Even if a loss was caused by the defendant, the law may restrict recovery where the loss is too remote.

The court may ask whether the type of loss was sufficiently foreseeable or legally connected to the wrongful conduct.

Foreseeability

Foreseeability can be important in determining whether certain losses are legally recoverable.

A type of harm that could reasonably have been anticipated may be more likely to fall within the defendant's legal responsibility.

Highly unusual consequences may be considered too remote under the applicable legal rules.

Remoteness prevents liability from extending indefinitely to every consequence eventually connected to an event.
CARD 11 · MITIGATION

Mitigation of Loss

A claimant generally has a duty to take reasonable steps to mitigate their losses.

Mitigation means reducing or limiting the harm caused by the defendant's conduct.

For example, following an accident, a claimant may be expected to obtain appropriate medical treatment where reasonable.

The claimant does not normally have to take unreasonable or excessively burdensome measures.

Mitigation in Contract Law

A person whose contract has been breached should generally take reasonable steps to reduce their losses.

For example, if a supplier fails to deliver goods, B may need to make reasonable efforts to obtain replacement goods rather than allowing losses to increase unnecessarily.

Mitigation requires reasonable conduct, not perfection.
CARD 12 · LIMITS ON RECOVERY

Remoteness, Mitigation & Certainty

Remoteness vs. Mitigation

Principle Question
Remoteness Which losses are sufficiently connected to be legally recoverable?
Mitigation Which avoidable losses should not be recovered?

Certainty of Loss

A claimant generally needs to provide sufficient evidence that a loss actually occurred or is reasonably measurable.

Courts may be reluctant to award damages based entirely on speculation.

Future Loss

Courts may recognize future losses where they can be established with sufficient legal certainty.

Examples include future medical treatment, future loss of earnings, long-term disability, and continuing care costs.

CARD 13 · EVIDENCE & LIABILITY

Evidence, Burden of Proof & Multiple Defendants

Evidence of Damages

Evidence is important in proving the amount of damages.

  • Medical records.
  • Receipts.
  • Employment records.
  • Repair estimates.
  • Expert testimony.
  • Financial documents.
  • Photographs.
  • Contracts and invoices.

Burden of Proof

In civil cases, the claimant generally bears the burden of establishing the facts necessary for the claim.

In a negligence case, this may include establishing duty of care, breach, causation, and damage.

In many common-law systems, the standard of proof in civil cases is the balance of probabilities.

Multiple Defendants

Where more than one person contributes to the claimant's loss, the legal system may determine whether both are liable, how responsibility is allocated, and whether one defendant can seek contribution from another.

CARD 14 · OTHER REMEDIES

Damages and Other Legal Remedies

Damages are one of several remedies available in civil law.

  • Specific performance.
  • Injunctions.
  • Restitution.
  • Declaratory relief.

Restitution vs. Damages

Damages = Compensation for loss.

Restitution = Restoration or reversal of an unjust benefit.

Specific Performance

Specific performance requires a party to perform a contractual obligation. Courts may consider it where damages are inadequate and the legal requirements for equitable relief are satisfied.

Injunctions

An injunction is a court order requiring a person to do something or refrain from doing something.

Damages may compensate for a completed loss, while an injunction may prevent continuing or future wrongful conduct.

CARD 15 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

Damages are a monetary remedy designed primarily to compensate for legally recognized loss.

In contract law, damages generally aim to protect the benefit of the bargain.

In tort law, damages generally aim to compensate for harm caused by wrongful conduct.

Remember

  • Causation = The loss must be connected to the wrongful conduct.
  • Remoteness = The loss must be sufficiently connected to be legally recoverable.
  • Mitigation = The claimant should take reasonable steps to limit avoidable loss.

Common Categories

  • Compensatory damages.
  • Expectation damages.
  • Reliance damages.
  • Nominal damages.
  • Consequential damages.
  • Non-economic damages.
  • Punitive or exemplary damages in exceptional cases.
Damages = Money awarded to provide a legal remedy for compensable loss.

The key question is not simply whether there was a legal wrong. It is also:

What loss did the wrong cause, and what compensation does the law permit the claimant to recover?
