Women's Access to Justice

Understanding women's right to access courts, legal services, remedies, and equal protection through international, African, and Ethiopian law.

Definition Why It Matters International Law African Framework Case Study Key Principles Barriers Ethiopia Research Question Sources
Core legal question: What legal and practical protections must exist so that women can obtain justice and effective remedies on an equal basis?
01 · Definition

What is Access to Justice?

Access to justice is the ability of people to seek recognition and protection of their rights through competent courts, tribunals, administrative bodies, legal services, and other appropriate mechanisms, and to obtain an effective remedy when their rights are violated.

For women, access to justice requires more than the formal existence of courts. Women must be able to use justice systems in practice, understand their rights, obtain legal assistance where necessary, participate meaningfully in proceedings, and receive decisions and remedies without discrimination or gender bias.

The key idea is simple: a legal right is difficult to exercise if a person cannot realistically reach the system that protects it.
02 · Why It Matters

Why is Access to Justice a Human-Rights Issue?

Human rights are meaningful only when people have practical ways to challenge violations and obtain remedies. Without effective access to justice, protections against discrimination, violence, unlawful treatment, and other rights violations may remain only promises on paper.

Women can face additional obstacles because of discrimination, poverty, lack of legal information, social stigma, geographic isolation, inaccessible procedures, or gender stereotypes within justice institutions.

Human-rights law therefore asks whether women can reach a competent and independent body, understand and participate in proceedings, receive fair treatment, and obtain an effective remedy when their rights have been violated.

03 · International Legal Framework

International Protection

CEDAW · Article 2

State Duties to Protect Women's Rights

CEDAW requires States Parties to pursue a policy of eliminating discrimination against women. Article 2 includes the duty to establish legal protection of women's rights on an equal basis with men and to ensure effective protection of women against discrimination through competent national tribunals and other public institutions.

Read CEDAW →

CEDAW · Article 15

Equality Before Courts and Tribunals

Article 15 requires States Parties to accord women equality with men before the law and, in civil matters, the same legal capacity and opportunities to exercise that capacity. It also requires women and men to be treated equally in all stages of procedure in courts and tribunals.

Read Article 15 →

CEDAW · General Recommendation No. 33

Women’s Access to Justice

General Recommendation No. 33 provides a detailed framework for women's access to justice. It addresses the justiciability, availability, accessibility, good quality, provision of remedies, and accountability of justice systems, as well as discriminatory laws, procedures, gender stereotyping, and other barriers.

UDHR · Articles 7–8–10

Equality, Effective Remedy, and Fair Hearing

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights recognizes equality before the law and equal protection without discrimination. Article 8 recognizes the right to an effective remedy before competent national tribunals, while Article 10 recognizes the right to a full and equal fair and public hearing by an independent and impartial tribunal.

Read the UDHR →

ICCPR · Articles 2–3–14–26

Equal Access and Fair Proceedings

The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights requires states to respect and ensure recognized rights without discrimination, guarantees equality of rights for men and women, provides for fair hearings before competent and impartial tribunals, and establishes equality before the law and equal protection of the law.

04 · African Human-Rights Law

The African Legal Framework

African Charter · Article 7

Right to Have a Cause Heard

Article 7 of the African Charter recognizes the right of every individual to have their cause heard. It includes access to competent national organs, the right to defence, and a hearing within a reasonable time by an impartial court or tribunal.

Read the African Charter →

Maputo Protocol · Article 8

Access to Justice and Equal Protection

Article 8 specifically addresses women's access to justice. It provides for equality before the law and equal protection and benefit of the law and requires effective access by women to judicial and legal services, including legal aid. It also calls for legal education, effective gender equality enforcement, equal representation of women in the judiciary and law enforcement, and reform of discriminatory laws and practices.

Read Article 8 →

Maputo Protocol · Article 25

Remedies

The Maputo Protocol also requires States Parties to provide appropriate remedies to any woman whose rights or freedoms have been violated and to ensure that such remedies are determined by a competent judicial or legislative authority.

Read the Maputo Protocol →

Ethiopia · Regional obligations

Ethiopia and the Maputo Protocol

Ethiopia ratified the Maputo Protocol on 18 July 2018. Its provisions on access to justice, legal services, legal aid, equal protection, and remedies therefore form part of the regional human-rights framework relevant to Ethiopia.

05 · Featured Case

Airey v. Ireland

European Court of Human Rights · 1979

A landmark case on effective access to court

Airey v. Ireland concerned Johanna Airey, who sought a judicial separation from her husband but could not effectively bring the proceedings because of the complexity of the procedure and the absence of available legal assistance for her circumstances.

The European Court of Human Rights held that Ireland had violated Article 6 §1 of the European Convention because the applicant did not have an effective right of access to the High Court. The Court recognized that rights guaranteed by the Convention must be practical and effective rather than merely theoretical or illusory.

Why is it relevant?

Airey is especially relevant to women's access to justice because it demonstrates that formal access to a court is not always enough. Where legal procedures are too complex for a person to use effectively without assistance, the absence of practical support can prevent genuine access to justice.

The case therefore illustrates a central principle of this topic: justice must be realistically accessible, not merely legally available.

06 · Key Legal Principles

What Does Effective Access to Justice Require?

Availability

Courts, tribunals, legal services, and other appropriate justice mechanisms must exist and be capable of functioning.

Accessibility

Justice should be physically, economically, legally, and practically accessible without discrimination.

Legal Aid

Where lack of financial resources prevents effective participation, legal aid can be essential to meaningful access.

Fair Hearing

Women are entitled to fair proceedings before competent, independent, and impartial bodies.

Gender Equality

Justice systems should protect women equally and address discriminatory laws, procedures, stereotypes, and practices.

Effective Remedies

A justice system should be capable of providing an appropriate remedy when a right has been violated.

07 · Understanding the Problem

Barriers to Women's Access to Justice

Women's access to justice can be limited by barriers within the legal system as well as by social and economic circumstances outside it.

Cost
Court fees, legal representation, transport, and other expenses can make legal proceedings inaccessible to women without sufficient resources.
Legal complexity
Complicated laws, procedures, forms, or evidence requirements can make it difficult for a person to pursue a claim without assistance.
Geography
Long distances to courts and legal services can create significant barriers, especially for people living in rural or remote areas.
Legal awareness
A woman cannot easily claim a right she does not know she has or does not know how to enforce.
Gender stereotypes
Bias or stereotypes among justice officials can affect how women's evidence, complaints, or claims are received.
Social stigma
Fear of stigma, retaliation, or social pressure can discourage women from seeking legal protection or reporting violations.
Language and disability
Procedures may become less accessible where interpretation, communication support, or reasonable accommodation is unavailable.
08 · Ethiopia

Women's Access to Justice in Ethiopia

Ethiopia's Constitution expressly recognizes a right of access to justice and establishes equality before the law, while also providing specific protections for women.

Article 37 · Right of Access to Justice

Article 37 provides that everyone has the right to bring a justiciable matter to, and obtain a decision or judgment by, a court of law or another competent body with judicial power. This is the constitutional foundation for access to justice in Ethiopia.

Article 25 · Right to Equality

The Constitution provides that all persons are equal before the law and are entitled to equal protection without discrimination, including discrimination based on sex.

Article 35 · Rights of Women

Article 35 guarantees women equal rights with men in the enjoyment of constitutional rights and protections and requires measures to prevent and eliminate discrimination against women.

Article 9 · International Agreements

The Constitution provides that international agreements ratified by Ethiopia form an integral part of the law of the land. Ethiopia's treaty commitments concerning women's equality and access to justice are therefore relevant to the national legal framework.

Why the Ethiopian context matters

A complete analysis must distinguish between the formal right to bring a case and the practical ability to use the justice system. Cost, distance, legal information, language, legal assistance, procedural complexity, and the treatment of women within justice institutions can all affect whether access is genuinely effective.

09 · Legal Analysis

Law on Paper vs. Access in Practice

Access to justice shows why a constitutional or treaty guarantee must be examined alongside the conditions that determine whether a person can actually use it.

Legal promise Practical question
Everyone has a right to access justice. Can a woman realistically reach the relevant court or legal institution?
Women are equal before the law. Are women treated equally throughout legal and judicial proceedings?
Women should have effective access to legal services. Can women obtain legal information, representation, interpretation, or legal aid when needed?
Rights require effective remedies. Can the justice system provide a meaningful remedy when a woman's rights have been violated?
10 · Research Question

Think Like a Human-Rights Researcher

When does a legal right to seek justice become meaningless because the justice system is technically available but practically inaccessible?

A strong legal analysis should ask three things: Is a legal remedy available? Can the person realistically access it? Will the system provide an effective and equal remedy?
