Can an Acquittal Be Reversed Without Hearing the Key Witness? The ECHR on Fair Trial Rights

Brunell and McArdle v. the Netherlands and the difficult question of how courts should assess decisive evidence on appeal

Published: October 2026 By: Mekdes Alemayehu

A recent judgment from the European Court of Human Rights has highlighted an important safeguard in criminal proceedings: when a court overturns an acquittal, can it rely on a witness whose credibility is central to the case without hearing that witness directly?

On 1 September 2026, the European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) delivered its judgment in Brunell and McArdle v. the Netherlands. The case concerned two men who had initially been acquitted of manslaughter by a trial court, but were later convicted on appeal. The appeal court relied heavily on testimony from a witness whose reliability had been seriously questioned, even though it did not hear him in person.

The ECHR unanimously found a violation of Article 6 § 1 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which protects the right to a fair trial.

The judgment raises a question that goes beyond this particular criminal case:

If a witness's account is decisive in determining guilt, should an appeal court be able to reject an acquittal without assessing that witness's credibility for itself?

What Happened?

The applicants, Kenneth Brunell and Barry McArdle, are Irish nationals. The proceedings arose from the death of a man in the Netherlands in 2009. During the investigation, a witness identified as X eventually made statements accusing the two applicants of involvement in the death and in the subsequent disposal of the body.

X had also been connected to the events under investigation. He initially remained silent, but later gave an account after gaining access to the case file and the investigative material. His statements became a central part of the prosecution's case.

Brunell and McArdle denied involvement in the killing. They acknowledged that they had stayed in the same accommodation as X, but maintained that they had not been present when the man died. The courts therefore had to consider not only what the witness said, but also how reliable his account was.

On 15 May 2017, the Amsterdam Regional Court found the two men guilty of unlawfully disposing of a body, but acquitted them of murder or manslaughter. The court considered X's statements insufficiently reliable to establish their involvement in the death beyond reasonable doubt. It also noted the circumstances in which X had changed from remaining silent to accusing the applicants.

The men received sentences of two years' imprisonment for the separate offence. The public prosecutor appealed the acquittals for manslaughter.

On 17 May 2018, the Amsterdam Court of Appeal overturned the acquittals and convicted both men of manslaughter as well as unlawfully disposing of the body. It sentenced each of them to 13 years' imprisonment.

The appeal court considered X's account alongside other material, including telephone data and forensic evidence. However, it did not hear X directly, despite the importance of his credibility to the question of who had been responsible for the death. The Dutch Supreme Court later upheld the convictions.

Brunell and McArdle then brought their complaints to the European Court of Human Rights, arguing that the proceedings had not met the standards required for a fair trial.

The Legal Question

The applicants relied on Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which protects the right to a fair hearing in the determination of a criminal charge.

A fair trial involves more than allowing a defendant to attend court or appoint a lawyer. The way evidence is examined can also matter, especially when a court is deciding whether someone is criminally responsible for a serious offence.

The key legal issue was not simply whether an appeal court is allowed to reach a different conclusion from a trial court. Appeals exist partly to review decisions and correct errors. The question was whether the Court of Appeal could reverse the acquittals while relying on decisive testimony from a witness it had not heard directly, even though the reliability of that testimony was a central dispute.

The Court considered the principle of immediacy. In this context, that principle concerns the importance of a court directly examining the evidence on which its decision depends, rather than relying only on a record of what someone said earlier. A judge who hears a witness directly has an opportunity to assess the testimony first-hand and consider questions about its reliability.

The issue was not whether witness testimony can ever be used on appeal. It was whether the appeal court could make a fresh decision about guilt while relying on a decisive witness whose credibility had already been seriously challenged, without hearing him itself.

What Did the Court Decide?

On 1 September 2026, the ECHR unanimously held that there had been a violation of Article 6 § 1. It did not decide the applicants' guilt or innocence under Dutch criminal law. Instead, it examined whether the process that led to their convictions had been fair under the Convention.

The Court acknowledged that the appeal court had considered other material, including telephone tracking information and forensic evidence. But, in the ECHR's assessment, that material did not independently explain exactly what had happened inside the accommodation or establish the individual roles of the people involved.

As a result, the assessment of guilt depended heavily on whether X's account could be trusted. His statements were therefore decisive to the outcome, rather than merely one unimportant detail in a larger body of clear evidence.

The ECHR noted that the first-instance court had raised serious doubts about X's reliability. When the appeal court overturned the acquittals, it reassessed the facts and the applicants' guilt while relying on his decisive testimony. Yet it had not heard X in person and therefore lacked the opportunity to assess his reliability directly.

The Court found that this was incompatible with the principle of immediacy and undermined the fairness of the proceedings as a whole. The later review by the Dutch Supreme Court did not correct the problem, because the case was not sent back for the witness to be examined afresh by the appeal court.

A court's power to reassess a case on appeal does not remove its duty to respect fair-trial safeguards when deciding that a person should be convicted.

The ECHR also indicated that reopening the proceedings would be the most appropriate form of redress. The applicants had not sought financial compensation, and the Court considered that finding a violation was itself sufficient just satisfaction in the case.

The judgment is a Chamber judgment. At the time of writing, the three-month period in which a party may request referral to the Grand Chamber had not yet expired, so the judgment was not yet final.

My Analysis

I think this judgment is an important reminder that the fairness of a criminal trial depends not only on the final decision, but also on how that decision is reached.

On one hand, appeal courts must have the ability to reconsider decisions made by lower courts. If they could never disagree with a trial court, the appeal process would lose much of its purpose. Different judges can evaluate the same record differently, and a conviction may sometimes be justified even after an earlier acquittal.

But appellate review becomes more difficult when a case turns on the credibility of a particular witness. If that person's account is the main evidence connecting the accused to a crime, the court cannot treat credibility as a minor detail. It is part of the foundation on which the outcome rests.

What concerns me most is the difference between reading a witness's earlier statement and having a meaningful opportunity to assess the witness directly when that statement determines the outcome of the case.

Written records can show the words a witness used, but they may not resolve every question about the reliability of the account. A court may need to consider how the testimony fits with other evidence, how inconsistencies are explained and whether questions from the parties expose important uncertainties. Direct examination is not a guarantee that a court will discover the truth, but it can be an essential safeguard when credibility is decisive.

At the same time, I do not think the judgment means that every appeal must repeat an entire trial or call every witness again. That would make legal proceedings unnecessarily slow and complex. The central concern here was more specific: the appeal court reconsidered guilt on the basis of a witness whose credibility was central to the case, without hearing that witness directly.

This is why I find the ECHR's reasoning convincing. The Court did not say that the applicants had to remain acquitted simply because a lower court had acquitted them. It focused instead on whether the procedure used to overturn those acquittals was fair.

If a person's guilt depends heavily on whether a witness is believable, can a court fairly decide that question without hearing the witness itself?

I think the answer depends on the circumstances, but the safeguard should be taken seriously. Fair-trial rights are particularly important when the consequences of a decision are severe. A legal system must be able to correct mistakes, but it must do so through procedures that give the people affected a fair opportunity to challenge the evidence used against them.

The case also shows why a court's reasoning matters. It is not enough to say that one court reached a different conclusion from another. We need to understand what evidence changed the outcome and whether the court deciding the case had a fair basis for relying on that evidence.

To me, the central lesson is this:

Fairness is not only about whether a court reaches the right answer. It is also about whether the process gives the court a reliable and fair way to reach that answer.

Why This Case Matters

Brunell and McArdle v. the Netherlands matters because it illustrates the role of procedural rights in protecting people facing criminal charges. Article 6 does not guarantee that a defendant will be acquitted, nor does it prevent a higher court from changing a lower court's decision. It requires the proceedings to respect the standards of fairness protected by the Convention.

The judgment is also a reminder that evidence must be assessed in context. Telephone records or forensic findings may corroborate parts of a witness's account without independently resolving every question about what happened or who was responsible. Courts must consider the actual role that each piece of evidence plays in the reasoning that leads to a conviction.

The case does not establish that a witness must be heard in person in every appeal. Instead, it highlights the importance of direct assessment where an appellate court is reconsidering guilt and a witness's credibility is decisive, particularly when that credibility was seriously disputed at first instance.

For me, the most important question is therefore not simply "Can an appeal court overturn an acquittal?" It is whether the process used to do so respects the rights of the people whose liberty is at stake.

How can courts correct possible mistakes without weakening the safeguards that make a criminal trial fair?

That question extends beyond the Netherlands. It is a challenge for any legal system in which courts must balance effective appeals, reliable evidence and the fundamental right to a fair hearing.
