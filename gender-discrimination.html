Gender Discrimination

Understanding discrimination against women and girls through international, African, and Ethiopian human-rights law.

Core legal question: What does the law require when women and girls are treated differently, excluded, or disadvantaged because of their sex?
01 · Definition

What is Gender Discrimination?

Gender discrimination is unequal treatment, exclusion, restriction, or disadvantage based on sex or gender that interferes with a person's equal enjoyment of rights and freedoms.

In women's-rights law, discrimination is broader than an openly stated rule that says women are inferior. A law, policy, practice, or institutional arrangement may also be discriminatory when its purpose or effect is to restrict women's rights or place women at a disadvantage because of sex.

The key idea is simple: equality requires more than treating people differently; it requires that women and men can enjoy their rights on an equal basis.
02 · Why It Matters

Why is Gender Discrimination a Human-Rights Issue?

Non-discrimination is a foundational principle of human rights. When women are denied opportunities, protections, services, or legal rights because of sex, the issue can affect their ability to enjoy other rights on an equal basis.

Discrimination can be direct, such as an explicit rule excluding women from a right or opportunity. It can also be indirect, where a formally neutral rule or practice has a disproportionate and unjustified negative effect on women.

Human-rights law therefore asks not only whether discrimination is written into a law, but also whether institutions, policies, social practices, and their effects continue to produce inequality.

03 · International Legal Framework

International Protection

CEDAW · Article 1

Defining Discrimination Against Women

CEDAW defines discrimination against women as any distinction, exclusion, or restriction made on the basis of sex that has the effect or purpose of impairing or nullifying the recognition, enjoyment, or exercise by women of human rights and fundamental freedoms in political, economic, social, cultural, civil, or any other field.

Read CEDAW →

CEDAW · Article 2

State Duties to Eliminate Discrimination

Article 2 requires States Parties to condemn discrimination against women and pursue a policy of eliminating it. The obligations include adopting appropriate legislative and other measures, establishing legal protection of women's rights on an equal basis with men, and taking measures to modify or abolish discriminatory laws, regulations, customs, and practices.

Read Article 2 →

UDHR · Articles 1–2–7

Equality and Equal Protection

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights establishes the principle that all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights. Article 2 prohibits distinctions including those based on sex, while Article 7 provides that all are equal before the law and entitled to equal protection without discrimination.

Read the UDHR →

CEDAW · General Recommendation No. 28

Substantive Equality

General Recommendation No. 28 explains the core obligations of states under Article 2 of CEDAW and emphasizes that states must address discrimination in both law and practice. The Committee's approach focuses on the actual enjoyment of rights, including situations where formally neutral rules can produce unequal effects.

ICCPR · Article 26

Equality Before the Law

Article 26 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights establishes equality before the law and equal protection of the law without discrimination. It is an important general non-discrimination guarantee that complements the women-specific protection found in CEDAW.

04 · African Human-Rights Law

The African Legal Framework

African Charter · Article 2

Non-Discrimination

Article 2 of the African Charter guarantees the enjoyment of the rights and freedoms recognized in the Charter without distinction, including distinction based on sex. Article 3 further guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the law.

Read the African Charter →

Maputo Protocol · Article 2

Elimination of Discrimination Against Women

Article 2 requires States Parties to combat all forms of discrimination against women through legislative, institutional, and other measures. It also calls for effective implementation of equality, corrective action where discrimination continues in law or fact, and steps to change social and cultural patterns based on ideas of the inferiority or superiority of either sex.

Read the Maputo Protocol →

Maputo Protocol · Article 3

Dignity and Development of Personality

Article 3 recognizes every woman's right to dignity, respect as a person, and free development of her personality. States are required to adopt measures protecting women from exploitation and degradation and to protect women's dignity.

Read Article 3 →

Ethiopia · Regional obligations

Ethiopia and the Maputo Protocol

Ethiopia ratified the Maputo Protocol on 18 July 2018. Its regional obligations therefore form an important part of the legal framework for examining discrimination against women in Ethiopia.

05 · Featured Case

Opuz v. Turkey

European Court of Human Rights · 2009

A case linking gender discrimination with unequal protection

Opuz v. Turkey concerned repeated domestic violence and threats experienced by the applicant, together with serious violence against her mother. The applicant argued that the authorities had failed to provide effective protection and that this failure reflected discrimination against women.

The European Court of Human Rights found violations of Article 2 concerning the applicant's mother's right to life, Article 3 concerning the applicant's protection from ill-treatment, and Article 14 taken together with Articles 2 and 3. The Court found that the authorities' general lack of response to domestic violence created a discriminatory effect on women.

Why is it relevant?

Opuz is relevant because it demonstrates that gender discrimination does not have to appear as an openly discriminatory written rule. A system can also raise discrimination concerns when institutions fail to give women effective protection from serious rights violations and that failure disproportionately affects women.

The case therefore helps illustrate the difference between formal equality and substantive equality: having laws that appear neutral is not enough when their operation leaves women without equal protection in practice.

06 · Key Legal Principles

How Does Equality Law Work?

Non-Discrimination

Women and men should be able to enjoy human rights without discrimination based on sex.

Formal Equality

The law should not expressly deny a right or protection to women simply because they are women.

Substantive Equality

Equality also asks whether women can actually enjoy rights on an equal basis in practice.

Direct Discrimination

An explicit distinction based on sex that disadvantages women can constitute direct discrimination.

Indirect Discrimination

A formally neutral rule or practice may still be discriminatory when its effects disproportionately disadvantage women without adequate justification.

Positive Measures

Human-rights law can permit or require measures designed to accelerate genuine equality where inequality persists.

07 · Understanding the Problem

Where Can Gender Discrimination Appear?

Gender discrimination can occur in law, institutions, workplaces, education systems, families, and social practices. Some forms are explicit; others can be harder to recognize because they appear neutral on their face.

Education
Different rules, expectations, access, or opportunities for girls and boys can produce unequal educational outcomes.
Employment
Discrimination can affect recruitment, promotion, pay, working conditions, or access to particular occupations.
Marriage and family
Rules or practices that give women fewer rights in marriage, divorce, child-related decisions, or property can create legal inequality.
Access to justice
Discriminatory procedures, stereotypes, or institutional practices can make it harder for women to obtain equal protection or remedies.
Political participation
Formal or practical barriers can limit women's equal participation in public and political life.
Social and cultural practices
Customs and stereotypes based on assumptions about women's or men's roles can reinforce unequal treatment.
08 · Ethiopia

Gender Discrimination in Ethiopia

Ethiopia's Constitution establishes equality before the law, prohibits discrimination including on grounds of sex, and specifically guarantees women equal rights with men in the enjoyment of constitutional rights and protections.

Article 25 · Right to Equality

The Constitution provides that all persons are equal before the law and are entitled to equal protection of the law without discrimination. Sex is expressly included among the prohibited grounds of distinction.

Article 35 · Rights of Women

Article 35 states that women have equal rights with men in the enjoyment of the rights and protections provided by the Constitution. It also recognizes women's equal rights in marriage, employment, property, and education and requires measures aimed at preventing and eliminating discrimination against women.

Article 9 · Supremacy of the Constitution and International Agreements

The Constitution provides that international agreements ratified by Ethiopia form an integral part of the law of the land. This makes Ethiopia's treaty commitments an important part of the legal framework used to examine discrimination against women.

Employment and non-discrimination

Ethiopia has also ratified International Labour Organization Convention No. 111 on discrimination in employment and occupation. The Convention addresses distinctions based on sex that impair equality of opportunity or treatment in employment and occupation.

Why the Ethiopian context matters

The legal question is therefore not simply whether equality is recognized in Ethiopian law. A human-rights analysis also asks whether women experience equal treatment in practice and whether discriminatory laws, policies, institutional practices, or social patterns are effectively identified and addressed.

09 · Legal Analysis

Law on Paper vs. Equality in Practice

Gender discrimination demonstrates why human-rights law distinguishes between a right existing formally and a right being enjoyed effectively.

Legal promise Practical question
Women are equal before the law. Do laws and institutions actually provide women with equal protection?
Discrimination based on sex is prohibited. Are discriminatory practices identified even when they are not openly described as unequal?
Women should enjoy rights equally with men. Do women have the same real opportunities to exercise those rights?
States should eliminate discrimination. Are laws, policies, institutions, and social practices being changed where inequality persists?
10 · Research Question

Think Like a Human-Rights Researcher

When can a rule or practice that appears neutral on its face still amount to gender discrimination?

A strong legal analysis should ask three things: Who is treated differently or disadvantaged? Is the difference connected to sex or gender? Does the law or practice prevent women from enjoying rights equally in reality?
