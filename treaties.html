CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is a Treaty?

A treaty is a formal international agreement concluded between states or, where legally permitted, between states and international organizations, and governed by international law.

Treaties are one of the most important sources of international legal obligations.

They allow states to create formal commitments concerning matters such as human rights, trade, peace and security, diplomatic relations, environmental protection, international cooperation, borders, criminal justice, and international organizations.

Treaty → Consent → Legal Obligation → Performance

The central question of treaty law is: How do states create binding legal obligations between themselves under international law?

CARD 2 · TREATY LAW

Sources of Treaty Law

The principal international framework governing treaties between states is the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) 1969.

The Convention establishes rules concerning:

  • Treaty formation.
  • Consent to be bound.
  • Reservations.
  • Treaty interpretation.
  • Amendment and modification.
  • Invalidity.
  • Termination.
  • Withdrawal.

Many rules contained in the VCLT also reflect customary international law.

Treaty law is therefore based on both treaty rules and relevant rules of customary international law.
CARD 3 · TYPES OF AGREEMENTS

Treaty vs. Ordinary Agreement

Not every international document is necessarily a treaty.

Treaty Ordinary / Non-Binding Agreement
Intended to create legal obligations governed by international law. May express political intentions or cooperation without creating the same type of international legal obligations.
Governed by international law. May include political declarations, joint statements, or certain memoranda of understanding.

The legal effect of an international document depends on the intention of the parties and the applicable international law.

The label given to an agreement is not necessarily decisive. Its substance and legal intention matter.
CARD 4 · PARTIES

Who Can Become a Party?

States are the principal participants in treaty-making.

International organizations may also conclude treaties where they possess the necessary legal capacity.

State ↔ State

State ↔ International Organization

The legal rules applicable to treaties involving international organizations can differ from those governing treaties exclusively between states.

International Organizations

International organizations can participate in treaty-making, facilitate negotiations, monitor implementation, or perform administrative functions such as acting as depositaries.

CARD 5 · FORMATION

Treaty-Making Process

Treaty-making can involve several stages. The exact procedure depends on the treaty and the constitutional rules of the participating states.

  1. Negotiation.
  2. Adoption of the text.
  3. Authentication of the text.
  4. Signature.
  5. Ratification or another form of consent.
  6. Entry into force.
  7. Implementation and performance.
Not every treaty follows exactly the same sequence. The treaty itself and applicable international law determine the precise requirements.
CARD 6 · CONSENT

Consent to Be Bound

A central requirement of treaty law is the state's consent to be bound.

A state does not ordinarily become a treaty party simply because another state signs the treaty.

Consent may be expressed through:

  • Signature.
  • Exchange of instruments.
  • Ratification.
  • Acceptance.
  • Approval.
  • Accession.
  • Another agreed method.

The treaty and applicable international law determine which method is required.

Consent is fundamental because treaties generally create international obligations through the agreement of the parties.
CARD 7 · SIGNATURE & RATIFICATION

Signature, Ratification & Accession

Signature

Signature may indicate participation in the treaty-making process and can have certain legal effects. However, signature does not always mean that the state is fully bound by the treaty.

Ratification

Ratification is the formal international act by which a state confirms its consent to be bound after signature where ratification is required.

Accession

Accession allows a state that did not sign the treaty during the original signing period to become a party where the treaty permits it.

The precise legal effect of signature, ratification, and accession depends on the treaty and applicable international law.
CARD 8 · ENTRY INTO FORCE

Entry Into Force

A treaty becomes legally operative according to its entry-into-force provisions.

A treaty may specify:

  • A particular date.
  • A required number of ratifications.
  • Specific conditions that must be satisfied.
  • Another agreed mechanism.

Before entry into force, the treaty may not yet impose its full substantive obligations on the parties.

Consent + Required Conditions → Entry Into Force → Treaty Obligations
CARD 9 · PACTA SUNT SERVANDA

Agreements Must Be Kept

Pacta sunt servanda means that agreements must be kept.

Article 26 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties provides that every treaty in force is binding upon its parties and must be performed by them in good faith.

Good Faith

States should not deliberately manipulate treaty obligations or use bad-faith conduct to undermine their commitments.

Good faith supports:

  • Trust between states.
  • Stability of international relations.
  • Consistent treaty performance.
  • Respect for international obligations.
CARD 10 · RESERVATIONS

Reservations

A reservation is a unilateral statement made by a state when expressing consent to be bound by a treaty that seeks to exclude or modify the legal effect of certain treaty provisions in their application to that state.

Reservations are particularly important in multilateral treaties.

When Can a Reservation Be Invalid?

A reservation may be impermissible when:

  • The treaty prohibits reservations.
  • The treaty permits only specified reservations and the proposed reservation falls outside them.
  • The reservation is incompatible with the object and purpose of the treaty.

Other states may also object to reservations, which can affect the legal relationship between the states.

CARD 11 · INTERPRETATION

Treaty Interpretation

Treaties must be interpreted to determine the meaning of their provisions.

The Vienna Convention establishes important general rules of interpretation.

Ordinary Meaning

Terms are generally interpreted according to their ordinary meaning unless a special meaning was intended.

Context

Interpretation considers the context of the provision, including the treaty's text, preamble, annexes, and certain related agreements and instruments.

Object and Purpose

Treaties are interpreted in light of their object and purpose.

Treaty interpretation is therefore more than examining one isolated word. Text, context, and object and purpose must be considered together.
CARD 12 · INTERPRETATION

Further Interpretive Materials

Where appropriate, interpreters may consider supplementary means of interpretation.

Preparatory Work

The preparatory work of the treaty can help confirm a meaning or resolve ambiguity.

Circumstances of Conclusion

The circumstances in which the treaty was concluded may also provide relevant information.

Subsequent Agreement and Practice

Treaty interpretation can also take account of:

  • Subsequent agreements between the parties.
  • Subsequent practice establishing agreement concerning interpretation.
  • Relevant rules of international law applicable between the parties.
Text + Context + Object & Purpose + Relevant Subsequent Practice
CARD 13 · AMENDMENT & TERMINATION

Changing or Ending Treaties

Amendment

A treaty may be amended according to procedures established by the treaty or applicable international law.

Modification

Modification may allow some parties to alter the operation of a treaty among themselves where international law permits it.

Withdrawal

Some treaties allow a state to withdraw according to specified procedures, such as notice requirements or waiting periods.

Termination

A treaty may terminate in circumstances recognized by the treaty or international law, including agreement of the parties, expiration, or certain serious breaches.

CARD 14 · INVALIDITY & JUS COGENS

Limits on Treaty-Making

Treaty-making power is not unlimited.

Invalidity

A treaty may be invalid in limited circumstances, including:

  • Error.
  • Fraud.
  • Corruption of a state representative.
  • Coercion of a representative.
  • Coercion of a state by threat or use of force.
  • Conflict with a peremptory norm of general international law.

Jus Cogens

Jus cogens refers to peremptory norms of general international law from which no derogation is permitted.

Examples commonly associated with jus cogens include prohibitions against genocide, slavery, torture, and aggression.

A treaty that conflicts with an existing peremptory norm is invalid according to the applicable rules of international law.

CARD 15 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Why Treaties Matter

Treaties provide one of the central mechanisms through which states create formal international legal obligations.

They can regulate:

  • Human rights.
  • Trade.
  • Peace and security.
  • Environmental protection.
  • Diplomatic relations.
  • International cooperation.
  • International criminal justice.
  • Refugee protection.
Negotiation → Adoption → Signature → Consent → Entry Into Force → Performance

Key Principles

  • Consent: States must validly consent to be bound.
  • Pacta sunt servanda: Treaties in force are binding and must be performed in good faith.
  • Interpretation: Treaties are interpreted according to international legal rules.
  • Reservations: Reservations are subject to international-law limits.
  • State responsibility: Breach of a treaty obligation can give rise to international responsibility.
  • Jus cogens: Treaty obligations cannot override applicable peremptory norms.
