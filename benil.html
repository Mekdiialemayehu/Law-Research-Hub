When Can Security Justify Surveillance? The European Court of Human Rights Rules on Prison Privacy

Benli v. Türkiye and the difficult question of how far governments can go when security concerns meet the right to privacy

Published: September 2026 By: Mekdes Alemayehu

A recent judgment from the European Court of Human Rights has raised an important question about the limits of state surveillance in prisons, particularly when detainees communicate with their lawyers and families.

On 15 September 2026, the European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) delivered its judgment in Benli v. Türkiye, concerning a Turkish man who had been detained following the attempted coup d’état of 15 July 2016.

During his detention, his meetings with his lawyer were monitored by a prison officer, while his correspondence with his family was electronically recorded and stored on the National Judicial Network System, known as UYAP.

The Court ultimately found a violation of Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which protects the right to respect for private and family life.

But the judgment raises a question that goes beyond the circumstances of this particular case:

When a state claims that surveillance is necessary for national security, what limits must still apply to the government's interference with a person's private life?

What Happened?

The applicant, identified by the Court as Salih Benli, was placed in detention on 21 July 2016 at Diyarbakır D-Type High-Security Prison on suspicion of membership of an organisation that the Turkish authorities considered responsible for the attempted coup.

Following the attempted coup, Türkiye was operating under a state of emergency and special measures had been introduced concerning detainees suspected of involvement with the organisation.

On 23 July 2016, the Diyarbakır chief public prosecutor's office issued a notice concerning investigations into suspected members of the organisation.

The notice stated that meetings between detainees and their lawyers could potentially threaten national and prison security. It therefore instructed prison authorities to have an officer present during meetings between detainees and their lawyers and to monitor those meetings.

On 17 August 2016, Benli was transferred to Şanlıurfa T-Type High-Security Prison.

According to the Government, he had fifteen meetings with his lawyer between 1 September 2016 and 23 March 2018. All of those meetings were monitored by an officer present in the meeting room.

There was another form of surveillance as well.

The Şanlıurfa prison administration recorded both incoming and outgoing correspondence on the UYAP system, a computer system used by the Turkish judiciary.

Benli's private correspondence with his wife was therefore electronically recorded and stored.

He challenged both measures.

On 4 January 2017, he complained to the Şanlıurfa enforcement judge, asking for the monitoring of his lawyer meetings and the electronic recording of his correspondence to stop.

The enforcement judge rejected the complaint, finding that the measures were permitted under the emergency legislation and other domestic rules.

Benli then appealed, but the Şanlıurfa Assize Court dismissed his objection.

He subsequently applied to the Turkish Constitutional Court.

In March 2020, the Constitutional Court declared his application inadmissible.

Benli then brought his case to the European Court of Human Rights.

The Legal Question

Benli relied on Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

Article 8 protects a person's right to respect for:

  • private life;
  • family life;
  • home; and
  • correspondence.

The right is not absolute.

Under Article 8 §2, an interference with private life can be lawful in certain circumstances, including when it is based on law and pursues a legitimate aim such as national security or public safety.

This creates a difficult balance.

Governments may have legitimate reasons for monitoring communications in prisons, particularly during serious security emergencies.

But the Convention also requires legal safeguards capable of protecting individuals against arbitrary interference.

The central question was therefore not simply whether Türkiye had a security concern.

The question was whether the measures imposed on Benli were sufficiently defined and safeguarded by law to satisfy Article 8.

The ECHR also had to consider whether Benli had properly exhausted domestic remedies before bringing his complaint to Strasbourg.

What Did the Court Decide?

The Court first rejected the Government's argument that Benli had failed to exhaust domestic remedies.

The Court noted that Benli had expressly relied on Article 8 before the Turkish Constitutional Court and had referred to the Court's relevant Article 8 case-law.

His complaint was therefore properly raised at the national level.

The ECHR then examined the monitoring of his meetings with his lawyer.

The Court found that the monitoring interfered with his right to respect for private life.

It also examined whether that interference had been "in accordance with the law."

This became the central issue.

The Court relied on principles it had already established in its earlier case-law concerning detainees' confidential communications with lawyers.

It found that the relevant emergency legislation gave prosecutors broad discretion to impose restrictions, without sufficiently defining the scope of that discretion or setting out adequate conditions and safeguards.

In the Court's view, the lack of clear limits meant that the law could be interpreted and applied in an arbitrary way.

The fact that the measures had been introduced during a state of emergency did not change that conclusion.

The Court specifically considered Türkiye's reliance on Article 15 of the Convention, which allows states, in exceptional circumstances, to take measures derogating from certain Convention obligations.

But the ECHR held that the absence of safeguards against arbitrariness and abuse could not be justified simply by the existence of the emergency situation.

The Court therefore found a violation of Article 8 in relation to the monitoring of Benli's meetings with his lawyer.

The Court then examined the recording and storage of his private correspondence.

Again, the issue was not simply whether prison authorities could inspect correspondence in some circumstances.

The problem was the legal basis for electronically recording and storing the correspondence on UYAP.

The Court referred to its earlier judgment in Nuh Uzun and Others v. Türkiye, where it had already examined similar measures.

In that case, the Court had found that official letters used as the basis for recording prisoners' correspondence had not been communicated to the prisoners and therefore did not satisfy the "quality of law" requirement under Article 8.

They did not provide sufficient legal certainty or adequate protection against arbitrary interference.

The Court found that the same reasoning applied to Benli's case.

Therefore, the ECHR unanimously concluded that there had been a violation of Article 8 concerning both the monitoring of his meetings with his lawyer and the recording and storage of his correspondence.

The Court awarded Benli €2,000 in respect of non-pecuniary damage and €500 for costs and expenses.

My Analysis

I think this judgment is more complicated than simply saying that the Court "ruled against Türkiye."

The interesting part of Benli v. Türkiye is that the Court did not say that national security concerns are never sufficient to justify restrictions on prisoners' communications.

Instead, the judgment focuses on something more fundamental:

What safeguards exist when the government interferes with a person's private life?

On one hand, I understand the security concerns raised by the Turkish authorities.

The case arose shortly after the attempted coup of July 2016, during a period in which the authorities were investigating suspected members of an organisation they considered responsible for the coup attempt.

The Government argued that communications between detainees and lawyers could potentially be used to transmit information or instructions connected to the organisation.

From the state's perspective, monitoring communication could therefore be presented as a security measure rather than simply an intrusion into privacy.

But the Court's reasoning shows why justification alone is not enough.

A government may have a legitimate objective, but the measures used to achieve that objective still have to operate within a legal framework that protects individuals from arbitrary decisions.

This is particularly important when the communication being monitored is between a detainee and a lawyer.

Confidential communication with a lawyer is closely connected to the ability of a person to obtain legal assistance and prepare a defence.

If the authorities can monitor those conversations without clearly defined limits, the relationship between a lawyer and client can lose its confidentiality.

That creates a difficult tension between security and individual rights.

I think the case also demonstrates why the phrase "in accordance with the law" in Article 8 is more important than it might initially appear.

It does not simply mean that the government can point to a law, regulation or emergency decree.

The Court's approach requires the legal framework itself to contain sufficient safeguards.

People must be able to know the circumstances in which their rights can be restricted, and authorities must not be given unlimited or undefined discretion.

The same issue appears in relation to correspondence.

Recording private letters is not exactly the same as reading a letter once.

Electronic recording and storage can create a lasting record of private communications.

That makes questions about who can access the information, how long it can be kept and what legal rules govern its use particularly important.

The Court's reasoning therefore goes beyond Benli's individual circumstances.

It illustrates a broader principle:

A state may have a legitimate security objective, but the existence of that objective does not automatically remove the need for legal safeguards.

What I find particularly interesting about Benli is that the Court did not have to decide whether Türkiye's security concerns were completely unjustified.

Instead, it found that the legal framework did not provide sufficient protection against arbitrary interference.

That distinction matters.

The judgment does not say that governments must choose between security and privacy.

Instead, it emphasizes that security measures themselves must operate within a framework that respects human rights.

Why This Case Matters

Benli v. Türkiye shows that the right to privacy does not disappear when a person enters prison.

It also demonstrates that emergency situations do not automatically suspend every requirement of the European Convention on Human Rights.

States may face genuine security threats, but restrictions on fundamental rights still require a sufficiently clear legal basis and safeguards against arbitrary interference.

The case is especially important for confidential lawyer-client communication.

A detainee may depend on private communication with a lawyer to understand legal proceedings, prepare a defence and challenge the actions of the authorities.

For me, the most important question raised by Benli v. Türkiye is therefore not simply "Was the surveillance justified?"

How can governments protect national security without creating a system in which private communication becomes subject to broad and unpredictable state monitoring?

That is a question that extends far beyond Türkiye.

It is a question about how human-rights law should operate during times of crisis, when governments may face legitimate security concerns but individuals still depend on legal safeguards to protect their privacy.
