SEPARATION OF POWERS
● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ●
CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Separation of Powers?

Separation of Powers is the principle that the powers of government should be divided between different institutions so that no single institution has unlimited authority.

The exact structure differs between countries, but the basic purpose is to prevent excessive concentration of government power.

Government functions are generally divided between three main branches: the legislature, the executive, and the judiciary.

CARD 2 · THE THREE BRANCHES

The Three Branches of Government

The three main branches of government are generally:

  • The Legislature — makes and changes laws.
  • The Executive — carries out and administers laws.
  • The Judiciary — interprets and applies the law and resolves legal disputes.
The purpose of dividing these functions is to reduce the risk that too much government power will be concentrated in one institution.
CARD 3 · THE LEGISLATURE

The Legislature

The legislature is responsible for creating and changing laws.

In many countries, the legislature is a parliament or congress. Legislators debate proposed laws and vote on legislation.

Main Functions

  • Creating and changing laws.
  • Debating proposed legislation.
  • Approving taxation and public spending.
  • Representing the public.
  • Scrutinising the actions of the executive.

The precise powers of a legislature depend on the constitutional system of the country.

CARD 4 · THE EXECUTIVE

The Executive

The executive is responsible for implementing and administering laws.

It commonly includes the head of government, ministers, government departments, and public authorities.

Main Functions

  • Implementing laws.
  • Developing government policies.
  • Administering public services.
  • Making decisions within powers given by law.
  • Managing the day-to-day functions of government.
The executive must generally exercise its powers within the legal limits established by the constitution and other laws.
CARD 5 · THE JUDICIARY

The Judiciary

The judiciary consists of courts and judges.

Its role includes interpreting and applying the law, resolving disputes, and protecting legal rights.

Why Judicial Independence Matters

Courts must be able to make decisions independently and impartially.

If the executive or legislature could directly control judicial decisions, individuals would have less protection against unlawful government action.

An independent judiciary is therefore an important safeguard against the misuse of government power.
CARD 6 · CHECKS AND BALANCES

Checks and Balances

Separation of powers does not necessarily mean that the three branches operate completely independently.

Modern constitutional systems often use checks and balances, where each branch has certain powers that can limit, supervise, or hold another branch accountable.

Examples

  • A legislature may scrutinise the actions of the executive.
  • Courts may review whether government decisions are lawful.
  • The executive may have powers relating to the implementation of legislation.
  • Courts or other institutions may review legislation for compliance with higher legal rules.

The overall purpose is to prevent too much power from being concentrated in one institution.

CARD 7 · EXAMPLE

Separation of Powers in Practice

Imagine that a government department makes a decision that seriously affects an individual's legal rights.

Under a system based on separation of powers, the individual may be able to challenge the legality of that decision before an independent court.

The court does not simply accept the government's decision because the government has political authority.

Instead, the court examines whether the decision was made within the powers given to the government by law.

This creates an important check on executive power and helps protect individuals from unlawful government action.

CARD 8 · DIFFERENT SYSTEMS

Separation of Powers in Different Countries

The separation of powers is not organised identically everywhere.

Some countries have a stronger institutional separation between the branches, while others have greater overlap between government institutions.

For example, in some parliamentary systems, members of the executive are also members of the legislature.

This does not necessarily mean that separation of powers is absent. Accountability can operate through different constitutional mechanisms.

Separation of powers should therefore be understood as a principle of limiting and controlling government power rather than requiring every branch to be completely isolated from the others.
CARD 9 · RULE OF LAW

Relationship with the Rule of Law

Separation of powers is closely connected to the Rule of Law.

The Rule of Law requires public authorities to act according to law and helps prevent arbitrary uses of government power.

Separating government functions between different institutions creates opportunities for oversight and accountability.

Why Separation of Powers Matters

  • Prevents abuse of government power.
  • Protects individual rights.
  • Supports judicial independence.
  • Creates accountability between government institutions.
  • Reduces the risk of arbitrary government decisions.
  • Strengthens the Rule of Law.
CARD 10 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

Separation of Powers is the principle that government authority should be distributed between different branches, usually the legislature, executive, and judiciary.

Its purpose is not simply to divide government into three parts.

It also creates checks, accountability, and limits on power, helping prevent abuse of authority and protect the Rule of Law.
