ACTUS REUS

CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Actus Reus?

Actus reus is a Latin term meaning "guilty act."

In criminal law, actus reus refers to the physical or external element of a criminal offence.

It concerns the conduct, action, omission, or other external element that the law prohibits or requires.

It answers the question:

What did the accused physically do, or fail to do?

For many criminal offences, the prosecution must establish both the required physical element and the required mental element before a person can be convicted.

The precise requirements of actus reus depend on the definition of the particular offence and the applicable legal system.

CARD 2 · ELEMENT OF A CRIME

Actus Reus as an Element of a Crime

A criminal offence generally consists of particular elements that the prosecution must prove.

Actus reus is concerned with the physical or external element of the offence.

For example, an offence may require the prosecution to establish that the accused:

  • Took property belonging to another person.
  • Caused physical injury.
  • Entered a building without permission.
  • Possessed a prohibited item.
  • Drove a vehicle in a prohibited manner.
  • Failed to perform a legally required duty.

The prosecution must establish the relevant physical elements according to the applicable standard of proof.

CARD 3 · CONDUCT

Actus Reus and Conduct

The most straightforward form of actus reus is a voluntary physical act.

For example, if a person intentionally strikes another individual and causes an injury, the physical act of striking may form part of the actus reus of an applicable offence.

The actus reus of an offence is not always simply "doing something." The definition of the particular offence determines exactly what physical elements must be established.

Voluntary Conduct

Criminal liability generally requires conduct that is voluntary. A person is not normally criminally responsible for a physical movement that occurs completely outside their control.

Possible examples of involuntary conduct include movements resulting from:

  • Certain medical conditions.
  • Reflex movements.
  • External physical forces.
  • Unconscious physical movements.
CARD 4 · OMISSIONS

Actus Reus and Omissions

Actus reus can sometimes consist of a failure to act, known as an omission.

Criminal law does not generally impose criminal liability simply because a person failed to help another person.

However, an omission may amount to actus reus where the law recognizes that the person had a legal duty to act.

Possible Legal Duties

  • A duty imposed by legislation.
  • A contractual duty.
  • A duty arising from a particular relationship.
  • A duty voluntarily assumed by a person.
  • A duty arising after a person creates a dangerous situation.
Simply being aware that someone needs assistance does not automatically create criminal liability for failing to help.
CARD 5 · LEGAL DUTIES

Actus Reus and Legal Duties

Where criminal liability is based on an omission, the prosecution generally needs to establish that:

  1. The accused failed to perform the required act.
  2. A legally recognized duty to act existed.
  3. The circumstances required the accused to perform that duty.
  4. The relevant requirements of the offence were satisfied.

The exact circumstances that create a legal duty vary between legal systems.

An omission becomes legally significant when the law recognizes a duty requiring the person to act.
CARD 6 · CIRCUMSTANCES

Actus Reus and Circumstances

Some offences require particular circumstances to exist.

In such cases, the actus reus may include not only the accused's conduct but also specific surrounding facts.

For example, an offence may require:

  • Property to belong to another person.
  • A person to be in a particular protected category.
  • The accused to be in a particular location.
  • A particular legal status to exist.
  • The conduct to occur without lawful authority.

These circumstances form part of the physical or external elements that must be established for the offence.

CARD 7 · CONSEQUENCES

Actus Reus and Consequences

Some offences require a particular result or consequence.

For example, an offence may require proof that the accused's conduct:

  • Caused death.
  • Caused injury.
  • Caused property damage.
  • Caused a particular form of loss.

In such offences, the prosecution must establish the required consequence as part of the actus reus.

Proving that the accused performed the relevant act may not be sufficient by itself when the offence requires proof of a particular result.
CARD 8 · CAUSATION

Actus Reus and Causation

Where an offence requires a particular result, the prosecution may need to establish causation.

Causation asks whether the accused's conduct legally caused the prohibited result.

Factual Causation

Factual causation examines whether the accused's conduct was a necessary cause of the result.

Would the result have occurred without the accused's conduct?

Legal Causation

Legal causation considers whether the accused's conduct is sufficiently connected to the prohibited result to justify criminal liability.

The exact tests for causation vary between legal systems and offences.

CARD 9 · POSSESSION & OFFENCE TYPES

Possession and Types of Offences

Possession

Possession offences are another important area of actus reus.

Possession does not necessarily mean physically holding something at a particular moment.

Depending on the legal system, possession may involve:

  • Physical control.
  • Knowledge or awareness of the item.
  • The ability to exercise control over it.

Result Crimes

Result crimes require proof of a particular consequence, such as death, physical injury, or property damage.

Conduct Crimes

Conduct crimes focus primarily on whether the prohibited conduct occurred. The prosecution may not need to prove a separate harmful result.

CARD 10 · MENS REA & LIABILITY

Actus Reus and Criminal Liability

Actus Reus and Mens Rea

Actus reus = the physical element of the offence.

Mens rea = the required mental element of the offence.

A criminal offence may require the prosecution to establish both.

Coincidence

In offences requiring both physical and mental elements, the required mental element generally must exist at the relevant time in relation to the actus reus.

This is sometimes called the principle of concurrence or coincidence.

Strict Liability

In certain strict liability offences, the prosecution may not need to prove a particular mental element for one or more elements of the offence.

However, actus reus remains important because the prosecution must still establish the physical elements required by the offence.

CARD 11 · CRIMINAL RESPONSIBILITY

Actus Reus and Criminal Responsibility

The existence of a physical act does not automatically mean that a person is criminally liable.

The prosecution must prove the elements required by the relevant offence.

These may include:

  • The prohibited conduct.
  • Required circumstances.
  • A prohibited consequence.
  • Causation, where necessary.
  • A legally relevant omission, where applicable.

Other legal issues, such as available defences, may also affect criminal responsibility.

Example

Imagine that a person is charged with an offence requiring proof that they caused another person physical injury.

  1. The accused performed the relevant physical act.
  2. The victim suffered the required injury.
  3. The accused's conduct caused the injury.
  4. Any required circumstances of the offence existed.

These physical elements together form the relevant actus reus.

CARD 12 · PROOF & EVIDENCE

Actus Reus and Evidence

In criminal proceedings, the prosecution generally carries the burden of proving the elements of the offence, including the relevant actus reus.

Evidence used to establish physical elements may include:

  • Witness testimony.
  • Documents.
  • Photographs.
  • Video recordings.
  • Physical objects.
  • Medical evidence.
  • Digital records.
  • Forensic evidence.

If the prosecution fails to prove an essential element of actus reus, the offence has not been established.

CARD 13 · DEFENCES & APPEALS

Actus Reus, Defences and Appeals

Defences

Even where the prosecution establishes the physical elements of an offence, a defendant may rely on a legally recognized defence where its requirements are satisfied.

Depending on the legal system, these may include:

  • Self-defence.
  • Duress.
  • Necessity.
  • Insanity or mental disorder doctrines.
  • Automatism or involuntary conduct.

Appeals

An appellate court may consider whether the trial court correctly identified and applied the physical elements of the offence.

An appeal may argue that the prosecution failed to prove an essential physical element, that causation was incorrectly applied, or that an omission was treated as criminal despite the absence of a legal duty.

CARD 14 · LEGAL SYSTEMS

Different Legal Systems

The concept of actus reus is particularly associated with common law criminal law.

Different legal systems may use different terminology or organize the elements of criminal offences differently.

Some legal systems may describe criminal liability in terms of:

  • Material elements.
  • Objective elements.
  • Physical elements.
  • Conduct and consequences.

Although terminology differs, many criminal law systems distinguish between the external or objective elements of an offence and the required mental state.

The exact rules concerning actus reus must therefore be understood within the relevant jurisdiction.
CARD 15 · WHY IT MATTERS

Why Actus Reus Matters

Actus reus helps:

  • Identify the physical elements of a criminal offence.
  • Prevent punishment based solely on thoughts or intentions.
  • Establish what conduct the prosecution must prove.
  • Clarify when omissions may create criminal liability.
  • Identify when causation must be established.
  • Provide structure for analyzing criminal responsibility.
  • Protect individuals from criminal conviction without proof of the required conduct.
Actus reus is fundamental because criminal liability generally requires more than simply having a thought or intention. The law must identify and prove the physical elements that make up the particular offence.
CARD 16 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

Actus reus is the physical or external element of a criminal offence.

It may involve:

  • A voluntary act.
  • An omission where a legal duty to act exists.
  • Particular circumstances.
  • A prohibited consequence.
  • A causal connection between conduct and result.

A simple way to remember the concept is:

Actus Reus = What was physically done or legally omitted?

For offences that require both physical and mental elements, the prosecution generally must establish the required actus reus as well as the applicable mental element.
