CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Sentencing?

Sentencing is the process by which a court determines the legal punishment or other consequences imposed on a person who has been convicted of a criminal offence.

It takes place after guilt has been established, either following a trial or after a valid guilty plea.

What legal consequence should follow from this criminal conviction?

Sentencing is not simply about imposing punishment. Courts may consider punishment, deterrence, public protection, rehabilitation, reparation, and denunciation.

The precise purposes and sentencing powers differ between legal systems.

CARD 2 · CONVICTION

Sentencing and Conviction

Conviction = Has the accused been proved guilty?

Sentencing = What legal consequence should follow from that guilt?

A conviction establishes that the accused has been found legally responsible for the offence.

Sentencing is the next stage.

The court considers the offence, the offender, the circumstances of the case, and the applicable sentencing law before determining the appropriate sentence.

CARD 3 · PURPOSES

Purpose of Sentencing

Sentencing serves several possible purposes.

  • Retribution — proportionate punishment for wrongdoing.
  • Deterrence — discouraging future offending.
  • Rehabilitation — helping offenders change their behaviour.
  • Incapacitation — restricting the offender's ability to cause further harm.
  • Reparation — addressing harm caused to victims or communities.
  • Denunciation — expressing society's condemnation of criminal conduct.
Not every sentencing system gives these purposes the same weight.
CARD 4 · RETRIBUTION

Retribution

Retribution refers to the principle that criminal conduct should result in a proportionate punishment.

It is based on the seriousness of the offence and the offender's level of responsibility.

Retribution does not necessarily mean revenge. It involves the idea that punishment should reflect the wrongfulness of the conduct.

A more serious offence may therefore justify a more severe sentence.

CARD 5 · DETERRENCE

Deterrence

Deterrence aims to reduce future offending by making criminal conduct less attractive.

Specific Deterrence

Focuses on preventing the particular offender from committing further offences.

General Deterrence

Focuses on discouraging other people from committing similar offences.

A substantial sentence for a serious offence may communicate that similar conduct will result in significant legal consequences.

The effectiveness of deterrence can vary depending on the offence and circumstances.

CARD 6 · REHABILITATION

Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation aims to address causes or circumstances that contribute to offending and help the offender return to lawful behaviour.

Possible Measures

  • Education.
  • Counseling.
  • Treatment programs where legally appropriate.
  • Employment support.
  • Structured supervision.
  • Skills development.
Rehabilitation is particularly important in sentencing systems that seek to reduce reoffending over the long term.
CARD 7 · INCAPACITATION

Incapacitation

Incapacitation seeks to protect the public by restricting an offender's ability to commit further offences.

Imprisonment is the most obvious example.

Other restrictions, such as probation conditions or restrictions on particular activities, may also serve protective purposes.

Incapacitation focuses on preventing further offending by limiting an offender's ability to commit certain offences.
CARD 8 · REPARATION

Reparation

Reparation refers to steps taken by an offender to address harm caused by their conduct.

Depending on the legal system, this may include:

  • Compensation.
  • Restitution.
  • Returning property.
  • Paying for damage.
  • Other forms of restorative action.
Reparation recognizes that criminal offences may cause direct harm to victims or communities.
CARD 9 · DENUNCIATION

Denunciation

Denunciation involves the court expressing society's condemnation of particular criminal conduct.

A sentence may communicate that certain conduct is especially serious or unacceptable.

Denunciation is particularly connected to the symbolic function of criminal punishment.

The principle may be especially relevant to serious offences involving violence, exploitation, corruption, or serious dishonesty.

CARD 10 · PROPORTIONALITY

Proportionality

Proportionality is a central principle in sentencing.

It generally requires the seriousness of the sentence to correspond appropriately to the seriousness of the offence and the offender's responsibility.

Courts may consider:

  • The harm caused.
  • The offender's culpability.
  • The circumstances of the offence.
  • Aggravating factors.
  • Mitigating factors.
  • The applicable sentencing framework.
A sentence should not normally be excessively severe compared with the offence.
CARD 11 · AGGRAVATING

Aggravating Factors

Aggravating factors are circumstances that may justify a more severe sentence.

  • Serious physical or psychological harm.
  • Use of violence or weapons.
  • Targeting a vulnerable victim.
  • Abuse of a position of trust.
  • Planning or premeditation.
  • Previous relevant convictions.
  • A particularly high level of culpability.
  • Persistent or repeated offending.
The court must apply only aggravating factors recognized by the applicable law.
CARD 12 · MITIGATING

Mitigating Factors

Mitigating factors are circumstances that may support a less severe sentence.

  • Genuine remorse.
  • Cooperation with authorities.
  • An appropriate guilty plea.
  • Limited role in the offence.
  • Lack of previous convictions.
  • Young age, where legally relevant.
  • Significant personal circumstances.
  • Evidence of rehabilitation.
Mitigation does not erase guilt. It may influence the appropriate punishment.
CARD 13 · GUILTY PLEA

Guilty Plea

A guilty plea occurs when the accused formally admits the offence.

In many legal systems, an early guilty plea may reduce the sentence because it can:

  • Save court time.
  • Avoid the need for a full trial.
  • Reduce stress for witnesses and victims.
  • Demonstrate acceptance of responsibility.
The amount of any reduction depends on the jurisdiction and the stage at which the plea is entered.
CARD 14 · PREVIOUS CONVICTIONS

Previous Convictions

Previous convictions may influence sentencing where the law permits them to be considered.

The court may consider:

  • The number of previous convictions.
  • Their seriousness.
  • Their similarity to the current offence.
  • How recently they occurred.
  • Evidence of rehabilitation since the earlier offences.
Previous convictions do not automatically justify any particular punishment.
CARD 15 · CHARACTER

Character and Sentencing

The offender's character may sometimes be relevant to sentencing.

Evidence may concern:

  • Reputation.
  • Previous conduct.
  • Community contributions.
  • Employment or education.
  • Personal circumstances.
  • Rehabilitation efforts.
Courts must apply the rules of the applicable legal system and avoid punishing an offender for conduct that is not legally relevant.
CARD 16 · GUIDELINES

Sentencing Guidelines

Some legal systems use sentencing guidelines to promote consistency.

Guidelines may identify:

  • The starting point for a sentence.
  • Relevant aggravating factors.
  • Relevant mitigating factors.
  • Appropriate sentence ranges.
  • Adjustments for guilty pleas.
  • Principles concerning different types of punishment.
The legal force of sentencing guidelines differs between jurisdictions.
CARD 17 · MANDATORY SENTENCES

Mandatory Sentences

A mandatory sentence is a punishment or minimum punishment required by law for a particular offence or circumstance.

Mandatory sentencing can limit judicial discretion.

Arguments in Support

  • Consistency.
  • Predictability.
  • Deterrence.

Arguments Against

  • Possible disproportionate results.
  • Reduced judicial flexibility.
  • Less ability to consider individual circumstances.
CARD 18 · MINIMUM & MAXIMUM

Minimum and Maximum Sentences

Legislation may establish a minimum or maximum penalty.

Maximum sentence = the highest punishment the court is legally permitted to impose.

Minimum sentence = the lowest sentence required where the law makes a minimum mandatory.

Where discretion exists between the minimum and maximum, the court determines the appropriate sentence according to the applicable principles.

CARD 19 · JUDICIAL DISCRETION

Judicial Discretion

Judicial discretion refers to the authority of a judge to choose among legally permitted sentencing options.

A judge must generally:

  • Follow the law.
  • Apply relevant sentencing principles.
  • Consider mandatory factors.
  • Avoid irrelevant considerations.
  • Impose a lawful sentence.
Judicial discretion allows individual circumstances to be considered, but it is not unlimited.
CARD 20 · TYPES OF SENTENCES

Types of Sentences

Criminal justice systems recognize different forms of sentence.

  • Imprisonment.
  • Suspended sentences.
  • Probation or community supervision.
  • Community service.
  • Fines.
  • Restitution or compensation.
  • Discharges.
  • Treatment or rehabilitation orders where legally available.
  • Other restrictions imposed by law.
Not every legal system uses all of these forms of punishment.
CARD 21 · IMPRISONMENT

Imprisonment

Imprisonment involves placing an offender in lawful custody for a specified period.

It is generally used for serious offences or circumstances where other sanctions are considered inadequate.

Courts may consider:

  • The seriousness of the offence.
  • Risk of reoffending.
  • Protection of the public.
  • Statutory requirements.
  • Aggravating and mitigating factors.
CARD 22 · SUSPENDED SENTENCE

Suspended Sentence

A suspended sentence is a sentence that may not be immediately served in custody, subject to legally defined conditions.

Depending on the legal system, the sentence may become enforceable if the offender commits another offence or breaches specified conditions.

Suspension can provide an alternative to immediate imprisonment in some circumstances.

The terminology and legal effect differ across jurisdictions.

CARD 23 · PROBATION

Probation

Probation generally involves allowing an offender to remain in the community subject to supervision and conditions.

Conditions may include:

  • Reporting to a probation officer.
  • Attending rehabilitation programs.
  • Avoiding particular people or places.
  • Complying with treatment requirements where lawfully ordered.
  • Remaining in education or employment where appropriate.
A breach of probation conditions may result in additional legal consequences.
CARD 24 · COMMUNITY SERVICE

Community Service

Community service requires an offender to perform unpaid work for a specified number of hours.

Possible Purposes

  • Punishment.
  • Reparation to the community.
  • Rehabilitation.
  • Avoiding imprisonment for suitable offenders.
Its availability and conditions vary between legal systems.
CARD 25 · FINES

Fines

A fine is a monetary penalty imposed by the court.

The amount may depend on:

  • The seriousness of the offence.
  • Statutory limits.
  • The offender's financial circumstances.
  • The purpose of the sentence.
In some legal systems, unpaid fines can result in further legal consequences.
CARD 26 · RESTITUTION

Restitution and Compensation

Restitution generally involves restoring property or value that was unlawfully taken or damaged.

Compensation may require an offender to pay money to a victim for legally recognized losses.

These forms of sentence emphasize the consequences suffered by victims.

Their availability depends on the offence and applicable legislation.

CARD 27 · DISCHARGES

Absolute and Conditional Discharges

Absolute Discharge

An absolute discharge generally means that no additional punishment is imposed after conviction.

Conditional Discharge

A conditional discharge may require the offender to comply with specified conditions for a period of time.

The legal consequences differ between jurisdictions.
CARD 28 · CONCURRENT & CONSECUTIVE

Concurrent and Consecutive Sentences

Concurrent Consecutive
Sentences are served at the same time. Sentences are served one after another.
Multiple sentences may overlap in time. The periods are added sequentially, subject to applicable law.

These concepts commonly arise when an offender is convicted of multiple offences.

CARD 29 · TOTALITY

Totality Principle

The totality principle concerns the overall effect of sentences imposed for multiple offences.

The court may need to ensure that the combined sentence is proportionate to the offender's total criminality.

This prevents a series of individual sentences from producing an overall punishment that is excessively severe.

The precise operation of the totality principle varies between legal systems.

CARD 30 · VICTIMS

Sentencing and Victims

Victims may have an important role in sentencing proceedings.

Depending on the jurisdiction, victims may be able to provide a victim impact statement explaining how the offence affected them.

Sentencing remains governed by legal principles, and punishment must remain proportionate and lawful.
CARD 31 · VICTIM IMPACT

Victim Impact Statements

A victim impact statement generally describes the physical, emotional, financial, or other consequences of an offence.

It may assist the court in understanding the harm suffered by the victim.

A victim impact statement does not generally determine the sentence by itself.

The court must consider the statement alongside the applicable sentencing law and other evidence.

CARD 32 · OFFENDER CIRCUMSTANCES

Sentencing and the Offender's Circumstances

Courts may consider personal circumstances where they are legally relevant.

  • Age.
  • Family responsibilities.
  • Health or disability where legally relevant.
  • Employment.
  • Education.
  • Financial circumstances.
  • Evidence of rehabilitation.
Personal circumstances do not necessarily excuse criminal conduct. They may help determine an appropriate and proportionate sentence.
CARD 33 · YOUTH

Youth Sentencing

Young offenders may be sentenced differently from adults.

This reflects recognition that children and adolescents may have:

  • Greater capacity for rehabilitation.
  • Different levels of maturity.
  • Less developed judgment.
  • Greater vulnerability to harmful environments.
Youth justice systems may therefore prioritize rehabilitation and education more strongly than adult sentencing systems.
CARD 34 · EQUALITY & CONSISTENCY

Sentencing and Equality

Sentencing should generally be consistent and non-discriminatory.

Consistency promotes:

  • Equality before the law.
  • Predictability.
  • Public confidence.
  • Fairness.
Consistency does not mean every offender must receive exactly the same sentence. Individual circumstances may justify differences.
CARD 35 · PROPORTIONALITY & EQUALITY

Proportionality Compared with Equality

Proportionality Consistency
Is the sentence appropriate for the seriousness of the offence and the offender's responsibility? Is the sentence reasonably consistent with comparable cases?
Focuses on the relationship between offence and punishment. Focuses on comparable treatment between similar cases.
A sentence should be individualized while still remaining within the framework established by law.
CARD 36 · HUMAN RIGHTS

Sentencing and Human Rights

Sentencing is subject to important legal and human-rights limits in many jurisdictions.

These may restrict:

  • Arbitrary punishment.
  • Disproportionately severe penalties.
  • Torture or cruel, inhuman, or degrading punishment.
  • Retroactive criminal punishment.
  • Discriminatory sentencing.
Article 7 of the ICCPR prohibits torture and cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment or punishment.
CARD 37 · LEGALITY

Sentencing and the Principle of Legality

The principle of legality requires criminal punishment to have a lawful basis.

A person should not be punished under a criminal rule that did not exist, in the relevant form, when the conduct occurred.

A court generally cannot impose a sentence that exceeds the punishment authorized by law.

The principle therefore limits arbitrary sentencing power.

CARD 38 · RETROACTIVE PUNISHMENT

Retrospective Punishment

Criminal law generally prohibits imposing a heavier punishment than was legally available when the offence was committed.

Article 15 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights protects against retroactive criminal punishment while recognizing limited rules concerning later, more lenient penalties.

The exact application depends on the jurisdiction.

CARD 39 · REHABILITATION PROGRAMMES

Rehabilitation Programmes

Some sentences include programs designed to reduce the likelihood of future offending.

  • Educational courses.
  • Behavioral programs.
  • Counseling.
  • Vocational training.
  • Supervision.
  • Community-based rehabilitation.
The availability of programs depends on the correctional and sentencing systems of the jurisdiction.
CARD 40 · ELECTRONIC MONITORING

Electronic Monitoring

Some legal systems allow courts to impose electronic monitoring as part of community supervision or another sentence.

Electronic monitoring can help enforce conditions such as:

  • Curfews.
  • Location restrictions.
  • Residence requirements.
Its use is governed by the applicable law and procedural safeguards.
CARD 41 · IMMIGRATION CONSEQUENCES

Sentencing and Immigration Consequences

A criminal conviction may sometimes have consequences beyond the sentence imposed by the criminal court.

For non-citizens, these may include immigration consequences under separate legal rules.

  • Immigration detention.
  • Loss of immigration status.
  • Removal proceedings.
  • Restrictions on future entry.
Such consequences are often governed by immigration law rather than by the criminal sentence itself.
CARD 42 · FINANCIAL CIRCUMSTANCES

Sentencing and Financial Circumstances

A person's ability to pay may be relevant when the court considers certain financial penalties.

For example, a fine that is reasonable for one offender may be disproportionately burdensome for another.

Some legal systems therefore permit courts to consider financial circumstances when determining fines.

The precise approach depends on the applicable legislation.

CARD 43 · PLEA BARGAINING

Sentencing and Plea Bargaining

Plea bargaining refers to negotiations in which an accused person may agree to plead guilty in exchange for a prosecutorial concession, such as the withdrawal or reduction of charges.

Plea bargaining systems differ significantly around the world.

Where legally permitted, an agreed outcome may influence the eventual sentence, but a court generally remains subject to its legal sentencing powers.
CARD 44 · SENTENCING HEARING

Sentencing Hearings

A sentencing hearing is the stage at which the court receives information relevant to the appropriate sentence.

The court may consider:

  • The facts of the offence.
  • The offender's criminal history.
  • Aggravating factors.
  • Mitigating factors.
  • Victim impact.
  • Guilty plea.
  • Relevant sentencing guidelines.
  • Applicable statutory requirements.
CARD 45 · EVIDENCE & REASONS

Sentencing Evidence and Judicial Reasons

The evidence considered at sentencing may differ from the evidence required to establish guilt at trial.

The court may receive information relevant to:

  • The seriousness of the offence.
  • Harm to victims.
  • The offender's background.
  • Risk of reoffending.
  • Rehabilitation.
  • Financial circumstances.

Judicial Reasons

Judges may be required to provide reasons for the sentence imposed.

Reasons promote transparency and allow appellate courts to review whether the sentence was lawful.
CARD 46 · APPEALS

Sentencing and Appeals

A sentence may sometimes be appealed.

An appeal may argue that the sentence was:

  • Excessive.
  • Unduly lenient where the law permits such an appeal.
  • Based on an error of law.
  • Based on irrelevant considerations.
  • Based on failure to consider a mandatory factor.
  • Inconsistent with sentencing guidelines or statutory requirements.
CARD 47 · SENTENCING ERRORS

Sentencing Errors

A sentencing error may occur where a court:

  • Misinterprets the law.
  • Applies the wrong sentencing framework.
  • Fails to consider a mandatory factor.
  • Considers an irrelevant factor.
  • Miscalculates the applicable sentencing range.
  • Imposes an unlawful sentence.
An appellate court may correct the sentence according to its statutory powers.
CARD 48 · CRIMINAL RECORD

Sentencing and Criminal Records

A sentence usually becomes part of the offender's criminal record after conviction.

The existence of a criminal record may have future legal consequences involving:

  • Future sentencing.
  • Employment in regulated professions.
  • Licensing.
  • Immigration matters.
  • Eligibility for certain programs.
The extent and duration of these consequences vary between legal systems.
CARD 49 · RELEASE & PAROLE

Rehabilitation After Release and Parole

Supervision After Release

Some offenders remain subject to supervision after release from prison.

  • Probation.
  • Parole.
  • Reporting requirements.
  • Restrictions on activities.
  • Rehabilitation programs.

Parole

Parole is the conditional release of a prisoner before the full custodial period has ended, subject to legal rules and supervision.

A person released on parole may have to comply with conditions, and violations may result in further consequences.
CARD 50 · RESTORATIVE JUSTICE

Sentencing and Restorative Justice

Restorative justice focuses on repairing harm caused by crime and involving affected individuals or communities where appropriate.

It may involve:

  • Victim-offender dialogue.
  • Mediation.
  • Community conferences.
  • Agreements to repair harm.
  • Other restorative processes.
Restorative justice does not replace conventional sentencing in every legal system.
CARD 51 · POLICY

Sentencing and Criminal Justice Policy

Sentencing reflects broader decisions about how a legal system responds to crime.

Different systems may emphasize:

  • Punishment.
  • Deterrence.
  • Rehabilitation.
  • Public protection.
  • Restorative justice.
Changes in sentencing policy can affect imprisonment rates, rehabilitation programs, and the use of community-based penalties.
CARD 52 · DIFFERENT SYSTEMS

Sentencing in Different Legal Systems

Sentencing differs considerably between jurisdictions.

Differences may concern:

  • Available punishments.
  • Mandatory minimums.
  • Maximum penalties.
  • Sentencing guidelines.
  • Judicial discretion.
  • Youth sentencing.
  • Parole.
  • Fines.
  • Restitution.
  • Appeals.
Sentencing principles must always be understood within the applicable legal system.
CARD 53 · EXAMPLE

Example of Sentencing

Imagine that a person is convicted of theft.

The conviction establishes that the prosecution has proved the offence according to law.

The court must then determine an appropriate sentence.

The judge may consider:

  • The value of the property.
  • Whether the offence was planned.
  • Whether the victim suffered significant loss.
  • Whether the offender has previous convictions.
  • Whether the offender pleaded guilty early.
  • Evidence of remorse or rehabilitation.
  • Relevant sentencing guidelines.
The sentence could potentially involve a fine, community-based punishment, probation, or imprisonment depending on the seriousness of the offence and applicable law.
CARD 54 · CRIMINAL RESPONSIBILITY

Sentencing and Criminal Responsibility

Criminal responsibility asks: Is the accused legally responsible for the offence?

Sentencing asks: What lawful and proportionate consequence should follow from that responsibility?

These are separate but connected questions.

A person may be convicted of the same offence as another person but receive a different sentence because of differences in their circumstances.

CARD 55 · WHY IT MATTERS

Why Sentencing Matters

Sentencing helps:

  • Punish criminal conduct.
  • Protect the public.
  • Deter future offending.
  • Encourage rehabilitation.
  • Recognize harm suffered by victims.
  • Express society's condemnation of serious offences.
  • Promote consistency and proportionality.
  • Provide lawful consequences for criminal responsibility.
Sentencing is therefore an essential part of the criminal justice process.
CARD 56 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

Sentencing is the process by which a court determines the legal consequences of a criminal conviction.

It is guided by principles such as:

  • Retribution — punishment proportionate to the offence.
  • Deterrence — discouraging future offending.
  • Rehabilitation — helping offenders reduce future offending.
  • Incapacitation — protecting the public by restricting criminal conduct.
  • Reparation — addressing harm caused to victims or communities.
  • Denunciation — expressing society's condemnation of criminal conduct.

Possible Sentences

  • Imprisonment.
  • Fines.
  • Probation.
  • Community service.
  • Suspended sentences.
  • Restitution or compensation.
  • Other legally authorized penalties.
Sentencing = Determining the lawful and proportionate consequence of a criminal conviction.

The court must consider the seriousness of the offence, the offender's level of responsibility, aggravating and mitigating factors, applicable sentencing laws, and the legitimate purposes of punishment.

Sentencing is therefore not simply about deciding how harshly to punish an offender. It is a structured legal process designed to produce a lawful, proportionate, and justified response to criminal conduct.
