BREACH OF CONTRACT
● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ●
CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Breach of Contract?

A breach of contract occurs when a party fails to perform a contractual obligation as required by the agreement.

The failure may involve failing to perform a promised obligation, performing incorrectly, performing late, refusing to perform, or making it clear in advance that the party will not perform.

A breach does not automatically mean that every contractual obligation disappears. The legal consequences depend on the seriousness and nature of the breach, the contract, and the applicable law.

CARD 2 · CONTRACTUAL OBLIGATIONS

Contractual Obligations

A contract creates legal obligations for the parties.

For example, A agrees to sell a laptop to B for $800.

A may be required to:

  • Deliver the laptop.
  • Deliver the agreed model.
  • Deliver it by the agreed date.

B may be required to:

  • Pay $800.
  • Pay according to the agreed payment terms.
  • Complete any other agreed obligations.

If either party fails to perform an obligation without lawful justification, a breach may occur.

CARD 3 · PARTIES & TYPES

Who Is in Breach?

The party who fails to perform a contractual obligation is commonly called the:

Breaching party
or
Party in breach.

The other party is often referred to as the:

Innocent party
or
Non-breaching party.

Important Types of Breach

  • Actual breach
  • Anticipatory breach
  • Breach of a condition
  • Breach of a warranty
  • Breach of an intermediate or innominate term

The classification of the breach can affect the remedies available.

CARD 4 · ACTUAL BREACH

Actual Breach

An actual breach occurs when the time for performance arrives and a party fails to perform as required.

For example, A agrees to deliver 100 books to B on 1 September. A does not deliver the books on 1 September.

A may have committed an actual breach.

Actual breach can involve:

  • Total non-performance.
  • Late performance.
  • Defective performance.
  • Incomplete performance.
CARD 5 · FORMS OF ACTUAL BREACH

Late, Defective & Non-Performance

Late Performance

A party may breach a contract by performing too late. Whether the innocent party can terminate depends on the importance of the deadline and the applicable legal rules.

Defective Performance

A party may perform but do so incorrectly. For example, A agrees to supply 500 black chairs but delivers 500 white chairs.

Non-Performance

A party can breach a contract by failing to perform at all. For example, A agrees to build a website for B by 1 October but does not begin the work.

Performance must generally comply with the contractual obligation. Doing something is not necessarily the same as doing what the contract requires.
CARD 6 · ANTICIPATORY BREACH

Anticipatory Breach

An anticipatory breach occurs when, before performance is due, one party indicates that they will not perform their contractual obligations.

For example, A agrees to deliver goods on 1 December. On 1 November, A tells B that A will not deliver the goods.

Depending on the applicable law, the innocent party may have choices such as accepting the repudiation, terminating the contract, seeking damages, or affirming the contract.

The legal consequences depend on the circumstances and the applicable legal system.

CARD 7 · CONTRACTUAL TERMS

Conditions, Warranties & Intermediate Terms

Condition

A condition is generally an important contractual term. Breach of a condition may allow the innocent party to terminate the contract and claim damages.

Warranty

A warranty is generally a less important contractual term. Breach usually gives rise to damages but does not ordinarily allow termination.

Intermediate or Innominate Term

Some terms do not fit neatly into either category. The consequences depend on the seriousness of the actual consequences of the breach.

The classification rules vary between jurisdictions and depend on the wording and nature of the contract.
CARD 8 · SERIOUSNESS OF BREACH

Material, Minor & Fundamental Breach

Material Breach

A material breach is a significant failure to perform an important contractual obligation.

Minor Breach

A minor breach is a less serious failure to perform a contractual obligation. It may give rise to damages without justifying termination.

Fundamental Breach

The term fundamental breach has been used in different ways. Traditionally, it could refer to a breach going to the very root of the contract.

Modern contract law often focuses more closely on the classification of contractual terms and the actual consequences of the breach.

CARD 9 · TERMINATION & ELECTION

Breach and Termination

A breach does not automatically terminate a contract.

Where a breach is sufficiently serious and the law permits termination, the innocent party may choose to end the contract.

Election

Where a serious breach occurs, the innocent party may sometimes choose between:

  • Affirming the contract.
  • Terminating the contract.

Affirmation

Affirmation occurs when the innocent party chooses to continue with the contract despite a breach that could potentially justify termination.

The legal consequences of termination and affirmation depend on the nature of the breach, the contract, and applicable law.
CARD 10 · DAMAGES

Damages for Breach

Damages are one of the principal remedies for breach of contract.

Their purpose is generally to compensate the innocent party for legally recognized loss caused by the breach, rather than to punish the breaching party.

Expectation Damages

Expectation damages aim to protect the benefit that the innocent party expected to receive from the contract.

Reliance Loss

Reliance damages may compensate certain expenses or losses incurred because the party relied on the contract.

Consequential Loss

Additional losses beyond the immediate value of the promised performance may sometimes be recoverable, depending on causation, remoteness, and contractual terms.

CARD 11 · LIMITS ON DAMAGES

Causation, Remoteness & Mitigation

Causation

A claimant generally must establish a sufficient connection between the breach and the loss claimed.

Remoteness

Even where the breach caused a loss, the loss may be too remote to recover. A classic common-law approach considers whether the type of loss arose naturally from the breach or was within the reasonable contemplation of the parties.

Mitigation

The innocent party generally has a duty to take reasonable steps to reduce its loss. The party does not normally have to take unreasonable or disproportionate measures.

Causation asks whether the breach caused the loss. Remoteness asks whether the loss is legally too remote. Mitigation asks whether reasonable steps were taken to reduce the loss.
CARD 12 · EQUITABLE REMEDIES

Specific Performance & Injunctions

Specific Performance

Specific performance is a court order requiring a party to perform their contractual obligation.

It is an equitable remedy and is not automatically available. Courts may consider it where damages would not provide an adequate remedy.

Injunctions

An injunction is a court order requiring a person to do something or refrain from doing something.

In contractual disputes, an injunction may sometimes prevent conduct that would violate contractual obligations.

Rescission

Rescission is primarily associated with setting aside certain contracts affected by factors such as misrepresentation. It should not be treated as automatically interchangeable with contractual termination.

CARD 13 · CONTRACTUAL CLAUSES

Liquidated Damages, Penalties & Liability Clauses

Liquidated Damages

A contract may specify an amount payable if a breach occurs. Such a provision may be called a liquidated damages clause where it satisfies the applicable legal rules.

Penalty Clauses

A contractual clause imposing a financial consequence for breach may be challenged as an unenforceable penalty under some legal systems.

Limitation of Liability

Contracts may contain clauses limiting or excluding liability for certain losses. Such clauses can be subject to statutory restrictions and other legal requirements.

Exclusion Clauses

An exclusion clause attempts to exclude liability for particular types of loss or breach. Courts may examine whether it was incorporated into the contract and whether it is legally effective.

CARD 14 · EVIDENCE & INTERPRETATION

Proving a Breach

Burden of Proof

In a civil contractual dispute, the party alleging breach generally bears the burden of establishing the relevant facts, subject to the applicable legal system.

The claimant may need to establish:

  • A valid contract existed.
  • The defendant had a contractual obligation.
  • The obligation was breached.
  • Legally recognized loss occurred, where damages are claimed.

Important Evidence

  • Written contracts.
  • Emails and messages.
  • Receipts and invoices.
  • Delivery records.
  • Witness testimony.
  • Photographs and digital records.

Courts may also need to interpret the contract to determine what the parties actually agreed to do.

CARD 15 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

Breach of Contract = Failure to perform a contractual obligation as required.

A breach may involve non-performance, late performance, defective performance, or an anticipatory refusal to perform.

Not every breach has the same legal consequences. The seriousness and nature of the breach can determine whether the innocent party may claim damages, terminate the contract, require performance, or seek another appropriate remedy.

Key Questions in a Breach Case

  1. Was there a valid contract?
  2. What obligation did the contract create?
  3. Was that obligation breached?
  4. How serious was the breach?
  5. What remedy is available?
