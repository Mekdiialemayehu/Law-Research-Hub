STATE SOVEREIGNTY
● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ●
CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is State Sovereignty?

State sovereignty is the principle that a state possesses supreme legal authority within its territory and legal independence in its relations with other states.

Sovereignty is one of the foundational principles of international law. It gives states authority to govern themselves while placing them within an international legal system.

A sovereign state generally has authority to govern its territory, make and enforce laws, choose its political and economic systems, conduct foreign relations, and exercise jurisdiction within its legal authority.

Key idea: Sovereignty means legal independence and authority — not unlimited freedom.
CARD 2 · SOVEREIGN EQUALITY

Sovereign Equality

International law is based on a system of legally equal states.

The United Nations Charter recognizes the principle of the sovereign equality of its members.

States may differ enormously in their practical power, but those differences do not automatically create differences in their basic legal status.

  • Population may differ.
  • Economic strength may differ.
  • Military power may differ.
  • Territory may differ.
  • Political influence may differ.
Political power ≠ Legal equality

A small state can possess the same basic sovereign status under international law as a major world power.
CARD 3 · TERRITORIAL SOVEREIGNTY

Territorial Sovereignty

Territorial sovereignty refers to a state's authority over its territory.

Within its territory, a state generally has authority to:

  • Make and enforce laws.
  • Administer government.
  • Regulate public institutions.
  • Exercise jurisdiction.
  • Manage public resources.
  • Maintain internal order.

Other states generally cannot exercise governmental authority within another state's territory without a lawful basis.

Territorial sovereignty is closely connected to territorial integrity and political independence.
CARD 4 · INTERNAL & EXTERNAL SOVEREIGNTY

Internal and External Sovereignty

Internal Sovereignty

Internal sovereignty concerns a state's authority within its own territory.

  • Create and enforce laws.
  • Operate courts.
  • Maintain public institutions.
  • Regulate economic activity.
  • Administer public services.

External Sovereignty

External sovereignty concerns a state's independence from other states.

  • Conduct diplomatic relations.
  • Enter international treaties.
  • Join international organizations.
  • Participate in international disputes.
  • Determine foreign policy.
Internal sovereignty = authority within the state.

External sovereignty = independence from other states.
CARD 5 · POLITICAL INDEPENDENCE

Political Independence

Political independence means that a state is generally free to determine its own political system and governmental structure without unlawful external control.

A state may ordinarily determine its:

  • Government.
  • Constitutional structure.
  • Political institutions.
  • Economic system.
  • Social policies.

The UN Charter protects the political independence of states and prohibits certain threats or uses of force against it.

Political independence protects a state's freedom to govern itself without unlawful external control.
CARD 6 · NON-INTERVENTION

Sovereignty and Non-Intervention

A major consequence of sovereignty is the principle of non-intervention.

Non-intervention means that one state should not unlawfully interfere with another state's sovereign affairs.

Interference can concern:

  • Political systems.
  • Governmental institutions.
  • Economic choices.
  • Social systems.
  • Cultural affairs.
  • Internal political conflicts.

The UN Charter protects matters essentially within domestic jurisdiction while also preserving the UN's authority to take enforcement measures under Chapter VII.

Sovereignty protects independence from unlawful external interference.
CARD 7 · USE OF FORCE

Sovereignty and Use of Force

State sovereignty is closely connected to the prohibition on the unlawful use of force.

Article 2(4) of the UN Charter requires UN members to refrain from the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of states, or in other ways inconsistent with the purposes of the United Nations.

Territorial Integrity

Territorial integrity means that a state's territory should not be unlawfully violated, seized, or divided through external force.

This principle is particularly important in matters involving:

  • Armed conflict.
  • Occupation.
  • Borders.
  • Territorial disputes.
  • Secession.
CARD 8 · SELF-DETERMINATION

Sovereignty and Self-Determination

Sovereignty is closely connected to the principle of self-determination of peoples.

The UN Charter recognizes respect for equal rights and self-determination of peoples as one of the purposes of the United Nations.

Self-determination generally concerns the right of peoples to determine their political status and pursue their economic, social, and cultural development.

The relationship between sovereignty and self-determination can become particularly important in situations involving:

  • Colonial rule.
  • Foreign occupation.
  • Independence movements.
  • Secession disputes.
The legal position depends heavily on the circumstances. Sovereignty and self-determination can sometimes raise competing legal considerations.
CARD 9 · JURISDICTION

Sovereignty and Jurisdiction

Jurisdiction refers to a state's legal authority to regulate and adjudicate matters.

Sovereignty provides an important foundation for jurisdiction.

Territorial Jurisdiction

A state generally has strong jurisdictional authority over conduct occurring within its territory.

Nationality Jurisdiction

A state may also exercise jurisdiction over its nationals in certain circumstances, including certain conduct committed abroad.

Other Bases

International law can recognize other jurisdictional bases, including effects of conduct and protective interests.

The extent of a state's jurisdiction is limited by international law.
CARD 10 · TREATIES

Sovereignty and International Treaties

Sovereignty allows states to decide whether to enter international treaties, subject to relevant international and domestic legal processes.

Once a state validly consents to a treaty, however, it becomes bound by its international obligations according to the treaty and applicable international law.

Sovereignty
State can consent to international obligations.

↓

Treaty obligation
State must perform those obligations in good faith.

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties expresses this through the principle of pacta sunt servanda: treaties in force are binding on their parties and must be performed in good faith.

CARD 11 · LIMITS

Sovereignty Does Not Mean Unlimited Power

Sovereignty is not the same as unlimited authority.

A sovereign state remains subject to applicable international law.

  • International treaties.
  • Customary international law.
  • Rules concerning the use of force.
  • Binding human-rights obligations.
  • Other applicable international obligations.

For example, if a state accepts a binding human-rights treaty, it cannot simply invoke sovereignty as an excuse for failing to comply with that treaty.

Sovereignty = legal independence, not unlimited freedom.
CARD 12 · HUMAN RIGHTS

Sovereignty and Human Rights

Modern international law does not treat sovereignty as an unlimited justification for violating human rights.

States may accept international human-rights obligations through treaties. Once bound, they must comply with those obligations.

The UN Charter also identifies respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms as one of the purposes of the United Nations.

A state can remain sovereign while simultaneously being bound by international human-rights obligations.

Example

Imagine that State A joins a human-rights treaty protecting a particular right. If State A later claims that the treaty cannot apply because the matter is "domestic," sovereignty would not automatically remove the treaty obligation.

CARD 13 · STATE RESPONSIBILITY

Sovereignty and State Responsibility

Sovereignty does not prevent a state from incurring international responsibility.

If a state breaches an international obligation, international law may hold that state responsible.

A state may have sovereign authority over its territory, but if its authorities breach a binding international obligation, sovereignty does not automatically excuse the breach.

The Relationship

The connection can be understood as:

Sovereignty ↓ State possesses international legal personality ↓ State accepts international obligations ↓ State must comply with those obligations ↓ Breach can create state responsibility

Therefore, sovereignty and accountability are not opposites.

CARD 14 · RELATED PRINCIPLES

Related Legal Principles

Concept Connection to Sovereignty
Sovereign Equality States have equal legal status even when their practical power differs.
Territorial Integrity Protects the territorial unity of states from unlawful external force.
Political Independence Protects a state's freedom to determine its political affairs.
Non-Intervention Restricts unlawful interference in another state's sovereign affairs.
Self-Determination Concerns the right of peoples to determine their political status in recognized circumstances.
State Responsibility Allows responsibility to arise when a state breaches an international obligation.
Sovereign Immunity Concerns when a state may be protected from foreign court jurisdiction or enforcement.
CARD 15 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

State sovereignty is the principle that a state possesses legal independence and authority over its territory and internal affairs while having equal legal status with other states.

Sovereignty includes:

  • Internal authority.
  • External independence.
  • Territorial jurisdiction.
  • Political independence.
  • Sovereign equality.

However, sovereignty operates within international law. States remain bound by applicable treaties, customary international law, rules concerning the use of force, and other international obligations.

State Sovereignty = A state's legal independence and authority, exercised within the limits of international law.

Questions to Ask in a Sovereignty Case

  1. Does the state have jurisdiction over the matter?
  2. Is another state interfering with its sovereign affairs?
  3. Is the state's territorial or political independence affected?
  4. Has the state accepted international obligations relevant to the issue?
  5. Does international law permit the conduct in question?
