CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is an Appeal?

An appeal is a legal process through which a party asks a higher court to review a decision made by a lower court.

The purpose of an appeal is generally to determine whether the lower court made a legal, procedural, or other recognised error.

An appeal is not usually a completely new trial. Instead, the higher court examines the decision of the lower court within the limits established by the relevant legal system.

The rules governing appeals differ between countries and types of cases.

CARD 2 · PURPOSE

Purpose of an Appeal

Appeals can serve several important purposes.

They may allow a higher court to:

  • Correct legal errors.
  • Review certain procedural errors.
  • Clarify the interpretation of law.
  • Ensure that courts apply legal principles consistently.
  • Provide a safeguard against unjust or incorrect decisions.
The availability of appeals therefore contributes to fairness and accountability within the legal system.
CARD 3 · PARTIES

Who Can Appeal?

The person or organisation seeking an appeal is generally called the appellant.

The opposing party is often called the respondent.

For example, if a defendant loses a civil case and believes that the court made a legal error, the defendant may become the appellant by bringing an appeal, while the other party becomes the respondent.

The right to appeal is determined by law. Not every decision can necessarily be appealed automatically.
CARD 4 · GROUNDS

Grounds of Appeal

A party cannot usually appeal simply because they are unhappy with the outcome.

An appeal normally requires a recognised legal ground.

Error of Law

An error of law occurs when a court applies the wrong legal rule, incorrectly interprets the law, or otherwise makes a legal mistake.

Procedural Error

An appeal may also arise where the lower court made an important procedural error.

For example, a court may have failed to follow a required legal procedure or denied a party a legally protected opportunity to present their case.

CARD 5 · EVIDENCE

Error in Evaluation of Evidence

In some legal systems and circumstances, an appellate court may review how evidence was evaluated.

However, appellate courts often give some degree of respect to findings made by trial courts because trial judges or juries may have directly heard witnesses and considered evidence.

The exact standard of review varies depending on the jurisdiction and type of case.

Appellate review of evidence can therefore differ from review of a pure question of law.
CARD 6 · APPELLATE COURTS

Appellate Courts

An appeal is generally heard by a higher court, known as an appellate court.

Highest Court
↓
Court of Appeal
↓
Trial Court

The exact structure differs between countries.

Higher courts may hear appeals from lower courts and may establish legal principles that lower courts must follow.

CARD 7 · FIRST AND FURTHER APPEALS

First Appeal and Further Appeals

First Appeal

In some legal systems, a party may have a right to appeal a decision to a higher court as a first appeal.

Depending on the applicable rules, the appellate court may be able to:

  • Confirm the original decision.
  • Reverse the decision.
  • Change part of the decision.
  • Send the case back to the lower court for reconsideration.
  • Order a new hearing in appropriate circumstances.

Further Appeals

Some legal systems allow a case to be appealed beyond the first appellate court.

Access to the highest court is often restricted and may require permission to appeal or involve an important legal question.

CARD 8 · APPEAL VS. RETRIAL

Appeal vs. Retrial

Appeal

Usually involves reviewing a decision that has already been made by a lower court.

The appellate court examines the decision within the limits established by law.

Retrial

Involves hearing the case again, potentially with evidence and arguments reconsidered according to the applicable legal rules.

An appellate court does not automatically conduct a completely new trial merely because an appeal has been filed.
CARD 9 · EXAMPLE

Appeals in Practice

Imagine that a trial court decides that a company breached a contract and orders the company to pay compensation.

The company believes that the judge misunderstood an important rule of contract law.

The company may have a right to appeal.

The appellate court could examine whether the trial court applied the correct legal principle.

The appellate court might:

  • Uphold the original decision.
  • Reverse the decision.
  • Change part of the decision.
  • Send the case back to the lower court for further consideration.
CARD 10 · STANDARD OF REVIEW

Standard of Review

The standard of review describes the approach an appellate court uses when examining a lower court's decision.

Different issues may be reviewed differently.

For example, an appellate court may apply one approach to a question of law and another approach to factual findings.

The level of deference given to a lower court can therefore depend on the nature of the issue being appealed.

Why This Matters

The standard of review helps determine how closely the appellate court will examine the lower court's decision and what level of respect may be given to findings already made.

CARD 11 · CRIMINAL & CIVIL APPEALS

Appeals in Criminal and Civil Cases

Appeals in Criminal Cases

A convicted person may appeal a conviction or sentence where the law permits.

Depending on the legal system, a prosecution authority may also have limited rights to challenge particular decisions.

Appeals in criminal cases can provide an important safeguard against serious legal or procedural errors.

Appeals in Civil Cases

Appeals are also available in many civil disputes.

  • Contracts.
  • Property.
  • Tort claims.
  • Family disputes.
  • Business disputes.

The rules governing civil appeals can differ from those governing criminal appeals.

CARD 12 · PRECEDENT & JUDICIAL REVIEW

Appeals and Other Legal Concepts

Appeal and Precedent

When a higher appellate court decides an important legal question, its reasoning may become a precedent that lower courts must follow in later cases, depending on the legal system.

Appeals can therefore contribute not only to correcting individual decisions but also to the development and clarification of the law.

Appeal and Judicial Review

An appeal generally asks a higher court to review a decision made by a lower court.

Judicial review generally asks a court to examine whether a public authority or other body acted lawfully.

Appeal

"Did the lower court make a legal error?"

Judicial Review

"Did the public authority act within its legal powers?"

CARD 13 · LIMITS & OUTCOMES

Limits and Possible Outcomes

Time Limits

Appeals are generally subject to legal time limits. A party may be required to file an appeal or request permission to appeal within a specified period.

Permission to Appeal

Not every appeal is automatically allowed. In some circumstances, a party must obtain permission to appeal.

Possible Outcomes

  • Uphold the original decision.
  • Reverse the original decision.
  • Vary or modify the decision.
  • Remit the case to a lower court.
  • Order a new hearing, where permitted.
CARD 14 · FINALITY & KEY TAKEAWAY

Why Appeals Matter

Appeals help:

  • Correct certain legal errors.
  • Protect the fairness of judicial proceedings.
  • Promote consistency in the interpretation of law.
  • Provide additional judicial oversight.
  • Develop and clarify legal principles.
  • Strengthen confidence in the justice system.

Finality of Decisions

A legal system must balance the right to challenge decisions with the need for finality.

For this reason, legal systems commonly establish time limits, permission requirements, limits on appealable issues, and rules determining which courts can hear further appeals.

Appeal = Asking a higher court to review a lower court's decision.

Appeals provide an important safeguard within the justice system by allowing certain legal or procedural errors to be examined by a higher court. They also help develop consistent interpretations of the law through decisions made by appellate courts.
