Gender-Based Violence

Understanding gender-based violence against women through international, African, and Ethiopian human-rights law.

Core legal question: What legal duties do states have to prevent, investigate, punish, and provide protection against gender-based violence affecting women and girls?
01 · Definition

What is Gender-Based Violence?

Gender-based violence against women refers to violence that is directed against a woman because she is a woman or that affects women disproportionately. It can impair or prevent women from enjoying their human rights and fundamental freedoms on an equal basis with men.

International human-rights law recognizes that gender-based violence is not limited to one setting. It may occur in private or public life and can take different forms, including physical, sexual, psychological, and economic harm, as well as threats, coercion, harassment, and arbitrary deprivation of liberty.

The key idea is simple: violence against women is a human-rights issue when it interferes with women's equal enjoyment of their rights.
02 · Why It Matters

Why is Gender-Based Violence a Human-Rights Issue?

Gender-based violence can interfere with fundamental rights including the rights to life, equality, liberty and security, freedom from torture or cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment, and equal protection under the law.

Human-rights law therefore looks beyond the individual act of violence and asks whether public authorities took reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable harm, protect people at risk, investigate allegations, prosecute offenders where appropriate, and provide effective remedies.

The legal significance becomes especially clear where repeated violence is known to authorities but the state response is ineffective. In such circumstances, the issue can involve not only the actions of a private perpetrator, but also the state's own human-rights responsibilities.

03 · International Legal Framework

International Protection

CEDAW · Articles 1–2

Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women

CEDAW provides the central international equality framework for women. The CEDAW Committee has clarified that gender-based violence against women is a form of discrimination and that states may be responsible for private acts when they fail to act with due diligence to prevent, investigate, punish, or provide compensation for such violence.

CEDAW · General Recommendation No. 35

Gender-Based Violence as Discrimination

General Recommendation No. 35 explains that the term “gender-based violence against women” emphasizes the gendered causes and effects of the violence and treats the problem as a social and structural issue, requiring comprehensive state responses rather than responses limited to individual incidents.

Read General Recommendation No. 35 →

Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women

United Nations Definition

The UN Declaration defines violence against women as gender-based violence that causes, or is likely to cause, physical, sexual, or psychological harm or suffering, including threats, coercion, or arbitrary deprivation of liberty. It covers violence in the family, the community, and violence perpetrated or condoned by the state.

Read the Declaration →

UN Human Rights Framework

State Responsibility and Due Diligence

A central principle is that human-rights obligations can require states to take positive steps to protect women from violence by private individuals. The question is not simply whether the state itself committed the violence, but whether its authorities responded adequately to known or foreseeable risks and provided effective legal protection.

04 · African Human-Rights Law

The African Legal Framework

Maputo Protocol · Article 4

Elimination of Violence Against Women

Article 4 of the Protocol to the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights on the Rights of Women in Africa requires states to protect women against all forms of violence and to adopt appropriate measures to prevent and punish violence against women, including domestic and sexual violence.

Read the Maputo Protocol →

African Charter · Articles 4–5–18

Life, Dignity, and Protection

The African Charter protects the right to life and integrity, prohibits torture and cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment, and requires the state to ensure the elimination of discrimination against women and to protect the rights of women and children.

Read the African Charter →

Ethiopia · Regional obligations

Ethiopia and the Maputo Protocol

Ethiopia ratified the Maputo Protocol on 18 July 2018. Its obligations under the Protocol therefore form part of the regional legal framework relevant to the protection of women against violence.

05 · Featured Case

Opuz v. Turkey

European Court of Human Rights · 2009

A landmark case on domestic violence and state responsibility

Opuz v. Turkey concerned a woman who had experienced repeated domestic violence and threats, together with violence against her mother. The case reached the European Court of Human Rights after the Turkish authorities had repeatedly failed to provide effective protection.

The Court held that Turkey had violated Article 2 (right to life) in relation to the applicant's mother, Article 3 (prohibition of inhuman or degrading treatment) in relation to the applicant, and Article 14 (prohibition of discrimination) taken together with Articles 2 and 3. The Court considered that the authorities' general lack of response to domestic violence had a discriminatory effect on women.

Why is it relevant?

Opuz is directly relevant because it shows how gender-based violence can become a human-rights violation involving state responsibility. The case demonstrates that a government may have obligations to respond effectively to violence committed by private individuals and that persistent institutional inaction can raise questions of discrimination against women.

This is also an example of the difference between having legal rules on paper and implementing them in practice: protection is meaningful only when institutions respond effectively to known risks.

06 · Key Legal Principles

What Duties Can States Have?

Prevention

States should take reasonable measures to reduce and prevent foreseeable gender-based violence.

Protection

Authorities should provide effective protection where women face serious or known risks of violence.

Investigation

Allegations of serious violence should be investigated through procedures capable of establishing the facts.

Accountability

States should have laws and institutions capable of holding perpetrators accountable and providing remedies to victims and survivors.

Non-discrimination

Women should receive equal protection of the law, without discriminatory attitudes or practices based on sex.

Effective Remedies

People whose rights have been violated should have access to meaningful legal and institutional remedies.

07 · Understanding the Problem

Why Can Violence Persist?

Human-rights analysis often requires examining the systems surrounding individual incidents. Violence may continue where legal protections exist but are not effectively implemented.

Under-reporting
Fear, economic dependence, stigma, social pressure, or lack of trust in institutions can make reporting difficult.
Institutional inaction
Authorities may fail to investigate complaints, enforce protection measures, or treat violence as a serious legal matter.
Discriminatory attitudes
Stereotypes or beliefs about gender roles can influence how complaints are received and how laws are enforced.
Economic dependence
Financial dependence can make it more difficult for women to leave unsafe situations or obtain legal and social support.
Conflict and instability
Conflict and institutional disruption can weaken access to justice and increase risks for women and girls.
Lack of access to justice
Distance, cost, limited information, weak institutions, or inaccessible procedures can prevent effective remedies.
08 · Ethiopia

Gender-Based Violence in Ethiopia

Ethiopia's constitutional framework protects equality, dignity, and personal security and provides a legal foundation for examining gender-based violence through a human-rights lens.

Article 25 · Right to Equality

The Constitution provides that all persons are equal before the law and are entitled to equal protection without discrimination, including discrimination on grounds of sex.

Article 16 · Right to Security of Person

The Constitution protects every person from bodily harm and establishes a right to liberty and security of person. These protections are relevant when considering state duties concerning violence against women.

Article 35 · Rights of Women

The Constitution recognizes women's equal enjoyment of rights and requires measures to address discrimination against women. It also specifically recognizes women's right to protection from harmful customs and practices.

Article 18 · Prohibition of Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment

The Constitution states that everyone has the right not to be subjected to cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment or punishment. This provides an important constitutional protection when examining serious violence.

Why the Ethiopian context matters

A human-rights approach asks not only whether Ethiopia has legal protections, but also whether women can actually use those protections. Access to justice, effective investigation, protection mechanisms, social barriers, and institutional implementation are therefore important parts of the legal question.

09 · Legal Analysis

Law on Paper vs. Protection in Practice

Gender-based violence demonstrates why human-rights law often examines both formal rules and their practical implementation.

Legal promise Practical question
Women are entitled to equal protection of the law. Do institutions treat complaints from women seriously and without discriminatory assumptions?
The state must protect fundamental rights. Does the state respond effectively when authorities know that a woman faces a serious risk?
Violence should be prevented and punished. Are prevention measures, investigations, prosecutions, and protective orders effective in practice?
Victims and survivors should have access to remedies. Can women realistically obtain protection, justice, support, and appropriate remedies?
10 · Research Question

Think Like a Human-Rights Researcher

When a state knows that women are facing gender-based violence, when does a failure to respond become a violation of human rights?

A strong legal analysis should ask three things: What rights are being affected? What duties does the state have? Was the response effective, timely, and non-discriminatory?
