DUTY OF CARE
● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ●
CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Duty of Care?

A duty of care is a legal obligation requiring a person to take reasonable care to avoid causing foreseeable harm to another person.

Duty of care is a fundamental concept in negligence law.

Before a person can generally be held liable for negligence, the claimant must establish that the defendant owed them a duty of care.

The key question is:

Did the defendant have a legal responsibility to take reasonable care toward the claimant?

The existence and scope of a duty depend on the relationship between the parties, the nature of the risk, and the applicable legal system.

CARD 2 · NEGLIGENCE

Duty of Care and Negligence

Duty of care is the first major question in a negligence claim.

A simplified negligence analysis is:

Duty of Care
↓
Breach of Duty
↓
Causation
↓
Damage

If no legally recognized duty of care exists, a negligence claim will generally fail even if the defendant's conduct was careless.

Why Does Duty Matter?

People are not automatically legally responsible for every harm that results from their actions.

Duty of care helps determine when the law considers a person sufficiently connected to another person to owe them a legal responsibility to take reasonable care.

CARD 3 · FORESEEABILITY

Foreseeability

Foreseeability is an important factor in determining whether a duty of care exists.

Could a reasonable person have foreseen that the defendant's conduct might cause harm to the claimant?

If harm was completely unforeseeable, it may be more difficult to establish a duty.

Example

Drivers can reasonably foresee that careless driving may injure pedestrians or other road users.

The law generally focuses on foreseeable risks rather than every imaginable consequence of an action.

CARD 4 · PROXIMITY

Proximity

Proximity refers broadly to the closeness of the relationship between the defendant and claimant.

Proximity can involve:

  • Physical closeness.
  • Direct interaction.
  • Legal relationships.
  • Reliance.
  • Assumption of responsibility.
  • The nature of the parties' relationship.

Foreseeability alone may not always be sufficient. A court may also ask whether the relationship between the parties was sufficiently close to justify imposing a duty.

Proximity helps prevent negligence liability from becoming unlimited.
CARD 5 · POLICY

Fairness and Policy

Some legal systems consider whether it is appropriate, fair, or reasonable to impose a duty of care in the circumstances.

Courts may consider broader policy concerns such as:

  • The potential scope of liability.
  • The social consequences of recognizing a new duty.
  • Whether other legal remedies exist.
  • The nature of the relationship.
  • The responsibilities of the parties.
  • Whether recognizing the duty could create excessive or unlimited claims.

The precise approach depends on the jurisdiction.

CARD 6 · NEIGHBOR PRINCIPLE

The Neighbor Principle

One of the most influential ideas in common-law negligence is the neighbor principle.

It emerged from Donoghue v Stevenson (1932).

People should take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that can reasonably be expected to cause harm to persons who are sufficiently closely and directly affected by their conduct.

The principle was important because it helped establish the modern common-law concept of duty of care.

It demonstrated that a legal duty could exist even where the claimant and defendant did not have a direct contractual relationship.

CARD 7 · LANDMARK CASE

Donoghue v Stevenson

In Donoghue v Stevenson (1932), a consumer became ill after consuming a drink that allegedly contained a decomposed snail.

The consumer had not purchased the drink directly from the manufacturer and therefore did not have a contractual relationship with the manufacturer.

The Legal Question

Could the manufacturer owe a duty of care to the ultimate consumer despite there being no contract between them?

The House of Lords recognized that a duty of care could exist.

The case became a foundational authority in the development of modern negligence law.

CARD 8 · COMMON RELATIONSHIPS

Recognized Duties of Care

Certain relationships commonly involve recognized duties of care, although the precise rules depend on the jurisdiction and circumstances.

Drivers and Road Users

Drivers generally owe duties of care to pedestrians, cyclists, motorcyclists, passengers, and other drivers.

Doctors and Patients

Doctors and healthcare professionals may owe duties concerning diagnosis, treatment, monitoring, advice, and medical procedures.

Employers and Employees

Employers may owe duties concerning workplace safety, equipment, training, working conditions, and supervision.

Schools and Students

Educational institutions may owe duties concerning reasonable measures for student safety and supervision.

CARD 9 · OTHER RELATIONSHIPS

More Relationships and Duties

Manufacturers and Consumers

Manufacturers may owe duties to consumers who could foreseeably be harmed by defective products, even without a direct contractual relationship.

Occupiers and Visitors

People who control or occupy premises may owe duties to people entering the property. This area is often called occupiers' liability.

Professionals and Clients

Professionals may owe duties where people reasonably rely on their expertise.

  • Lawyers
  • Accountants
  • Engineers
  • Architects
  • Financial professionals

The existence and scope of the duty depend on the professional relationship and applicable law.

CARD 10 · OMISSIONS

Positive Acts and Omissions

A major distinction in negligence law is between positive acts and omissions.

Positive Acts

A positive act involves doing something that creates or increases a risk.

For example, a driver who carelessly drives into a pedestrian crossing may create a direct risk of injury.

Omissions

An omission involves failing to act.

The law is often more cautious about imposing liability for omissions.

There is not necessarily a general legal duty to rescue another person merely because someone is in danger.
CARD 11 · EXCEPTIONS

When Can an Omission Create a Duty?

A duty to act may arise in particular circumstances.

  • The defendant created the danger.
  • The defendant has a special relationship with the claimant.
  • The defendant assumed responsibility.
  • The defendant has control over a source of danger.
  • A statute imposes a duty.
  • Another recognized legal relationship exists.

Assumption of Responsibility

A person may owe a duty where they voluntarily assume responsibility for another person's safety or interests.

For example, someone who undertakes to provide professional advice may assume responsibility toward a person who reasonably relies on that advice.

CARD 12 · PUBLIC AUTHORITIES

Public Authorities and Emergency Services

Whether a public authority owes a duty of care can be more complicated.

Public authorities may perform functions involving:

  • Public safety.
  • Healthcare.
  • Education.
  • Emergency services.
  • Law enforcement.

The fact that an authority could have prevented harm does not automatically mean that it owed a private-law duty of care.

Police

Police forces may have statutory responsibilities to protect the public, but this does not automatically mean that every individual member of the public is owed a private-law duty of care in every circumstance.

The rules differ between jurisdictions.

CARD 13 · ECONOMIC LOSS

Negligent Misstatement and Economic Loss

A duty of care concerning pure economic loss can be more difficult to establish than a duty concerning physical injury or property damage.

Courts may consider:

  • Proximity.
  • Reliance.
  • Assumption of responsibility.
  • Foreseeability.
  • Policy considerations.

Negligent Misstatement

Negligent misstatement concerns situations where a person provides information or advice carelessly and another person suffers legally recognized loss after reasonably relying on it.

A sufficiently close relationship, assumption of responsibility, reasonable reliance, and foreseeable harm may be relevant.

CARD 14 · DUTY VS BREACH

Duty, Standard, Breach and Causation

Duty of care and standard of care are different concepts.

Duty of Care: Did the defendant owe a legal responsibility to the claimant?

Standard of Care: What level of care was legally required?

Breach: Did the defendant fall below the required standard?

Causation: Did the breach cause the legally recognized harm?

Damage: What legally recognized harm resulted?

For example, a doctor may owe a patient a duty of care. The next question is whether the doctor acted according to the applicable professional standard.

Duty exists ≠ Breach has occurred.
CARD 15 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

A duty of care is a legal obligation to take reasonable care to avoid causing foreseeable harm to another person.

It is the first major question in a negligence claim.

Duty → Breach → Causation → Damage

Important factors may include:

  • Foreseeability.
  • Proximity.
  • The relationship between the parties.
  • Assumption of responsibility.
  • Applicable legal principles.
  • Policy considerations in some legal systems.

The landmark case Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) played a major role in developing the modern common-law concept of duty of care.

A duty of care does not automatically mean negligence has been established. The claimant must still prove the other required elements of the negligence claim.
