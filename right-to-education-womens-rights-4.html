Women's Rights · Human Rights Law

Right to Education

Understanding the legal protection of education for women and girls through international, African, and Ethiopian law.

Definition Why It Matters International Law African Framework Education Framework Barriers Ethiopia Research Question Sources
Core legal question: What legal protections exist to ensure that women and girls can access education equally and without discrimination?
01 · Definition

What is the Right to Education?

The right to education is a human right that protects every person's ability to access education. International human-rights law does not treat education simply as a service provided by a government; it recognizes education as a right connected to human dignity, equality, participation, and the development of the individual.

For women and girls, the right to education also requires protection against discrimination. A legal system should therefore not merely provide schools in general. It must also address rules, practices, and barriers that prevent girls and women from enjoying education on an equal basis.

The key idea is simple: education must be available, accessible, and enjoyed without discrimination.
02 · Why It Matters

Why is Education a Human-Rights Issue?

Education can affect a person's ability to exercise other rights and participate fully in society. The United Nations describes education as a human right, a public good, and a public responsibility.

When girls are denied education because of discrimination, poverty, early marriage, household responsibilities, conflict, unsafe conditions, or other barriers, the issue is not only educational. It can also raise questions about equality, dignity, participation, and the responsibilities of the state.

This is why the right to education is especially important in women's-rights law: formal equality is not enough when practical barriers continue to prevent girls from attending, staying in, and completing school.

03 · International Legal Framework

International Protection

UDHR · Article 26

Universal Declaration of Human Rights

Article 26 recognizes that everyone has the right to education. It provides that elementary education should be free and compulsory, that technical and professional education should be generally available, and that higher education should be equally accessible on the basis of merit.

Read the UN text →

CEDAW · Article 10

Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women

CEDAW gives the issue a specifically gender-equality framework. Article 10 requires States Parties to take measures to eliminate discrimination against women in education and to ensure equality of men and women in access to studies, examinations, vocational guidance, teaching standards, facilities, curricula, and other aspects of education.

Read more from OHCHR →

ICESCR · Articles 13–14

International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights

Article 13 recognizes the right of everyone to education and explains that education should contribute to the full development of the human personality and respect for human rights. It also sets out duties concerning primary, secondary, and higher education.

Read the Covenant →

CRC · Articles 28–29

Convention on the Rights of the Child

The Convention recognizes every child's right to education. Article 28 addresses access, compulsory primary education, secondary education, higher education, guidance, attendance, and reducing dropout. Article 29 addresses the aims of education, including the development of the child's personality, abilities, dignity, and respect for human rights.

04 · African Human-Rights Law

The African Legal Framework

African Charter · Article 17

Right to Education

Article 17 of the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights states that every individual has the right to education. Article 18 also requires the state to ensure the elimination of discrimination against women and the protection of women's and children's rights.

Read the African Charter →

Maputo Protocol · Article 12

Women and Girls' Education

Article 12 is particularly important for this section. It requires States Parties to eliminate discrimination against women and guarantee equal opportunity and access to education and training. It also addresses stereotypes in educational materials, abuse and sexual harassment in schools, literacy, education and training at all levels, and the enrolment and retention of girls.

Ethiopia · Regional obligations

Ethiopia and the Maputo Protocol

Ethiopia ratified the Maputo Protocol on 18 July 2018. The African Commission has subsequently reviewed Ethiopia's implementation of the African Charter and the Maputo Protocol.

05 · Understanding the Right

Four Dimensions of Education

A useful human-rights framework is to ask not only whether schools exist, but whether education is genuinely reachable and appropriate for everyone.

Availability

Are functioning educational institutions, programmes, teachers, and necessary facilities available?

Accessibility

Can people access education without discrimination or unreasonable legal, economic, physical, or geographic barriers?

Acceptability

Is the education provided of appropriate quality and consistent with human dignity and rights?

Adaptability

Can education respond to the needs of different students and changing social circumstances?

06 · Women and Girls

Barriers to Education

A right can exist in law while remaining difficult to enjoy in practice. For women and girls, barriers may operate at household, community, institutional, economic, geographic, or state levels.

Gender discrimination
When girls receive fewer educational opportunities, face different rules, or are discouraged from studying because of their sex.
Poverty
School may be formally available while transport, materials, uniforms, or other costs make attendance difficult for low-income families.
Child marriage
Marriage during childhood can interrupt schooling and create responsibilities that make continued education harder.
Household work
Domestic responsibilities can reduce the time girls have to attend school, study, or complete homework.
Conflict and displacement
Conflict can damage schools, interrupt teaching, displace families, and prevent children from reaching classrooms safely.
Unsafe school environments
Violence, harassment, discrimination, or inadequate facilities can make continued education difficult or unsafe.
Distance and geography
Long journeys and limited access to schools can create major practical barriers, particularly in rural and remote areas.
07 · Ethiopia

Right to Education in Ethiopia

Ethiopia's constitutional framework connects education with equality, children's rights, and social objectives.

Article 25 · Right to Equality

The Constitution provides that all persons are equal before the law and are entitled to equal protection without discrimination, including discrimination on grounds of sex.

Article 36 · Rights of Children

The Constitution protects children from practices that may be harmful to their education, health, or well-being. It also requires the best interests of the child to be a primary consideration in actions concerning children.

Article 90 · Social Objectives

The Constitution states that, to the extent the country's resources permit, policies should aim to provide all Ethiopians access to public health and education. It also states that education should be provided free from religious influence, political partisanship, or cultural prejudices.

What does this mean for girls?

Read together, these provisions create an important legal picture: education is connected to access, equality, protection from harmful practices, and the state's social responsibilities. The practical question is how effectively these legal commitments are translated into real access for girls across Ethiopia.

Ethiopian context

UNICEF reports that girls in rural and emergency-affected areas can face barriers including poverty, household responsibilities, long distances to school, conflict, climate-related disruption, and social norms. This shows why a legal right must also be examined in its social and practical context.

08 · Legal Analysis

Law on Paper vs. Education in Practice

One of the most important questions in human-rights research is whether a right exists only formally, or whether people can actually exercise it.

Legal promise Practical question
Everyone has a right to education. Are there functioning schools that students can actually reach?
Education must be enjoyed without discrimination. Do girls face different barriers or treatment because they are girls?
Girls should have equal access to education and training. Can girls enroll, remain in school, complete their education, and progress to higher levels?
Children are protected from practices harmful to education. What happens when child marriage, child labour, conflict, or other pressures interfere with schooling?
09 · Research Question

Think Like a Human-Rights Researcher

If education is a fundamental human right, what responsibility does a government have when social, economic, cultural, or geographical barriers prevent girls from accessing it?

A strong legal analysis should ask three things: What does the law promise? What barriers exist? What duties does the state have to close the gap?
