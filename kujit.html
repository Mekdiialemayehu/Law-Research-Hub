When Should a Human-Rights Court Step In? The European Court of Human Rights Rules on National Remedies

Kuijt v. the Netherlands and the difficult boundary between national courts and international human-rights protection

Published: September 2026 By: Mekdes Alemayehu

A recent decision of the European Court of Human Rights has raised an important question about the relationship between national courts and international human-rights protection:

When someone believes that a country's highest court has violated their right to a fair trial, when should the European Court of Human Rights intervene?

On 23 September 2026, the Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights delivered its decision in Kuijt v. the Netherlands, concerning a Dutch woman who challenged a practice used during deliberations at the Supreme Court of the Netherlands.

The case involved Article 6 § 1 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which guarantees the right to a fair hearing by an independent and impartial tribunal established by law.

But the Court did not ultimately decide whether the Dutch practice itself violated Article 6.

Instead, the case became a significant discussion about something more procedural:

Had the applicant properly used the remedies available in her own country before asking an international court to intervene?

What Happened?

The applicant, Johanna Kuijt, had been convicted in the Netherlands after participating in a protest that disrupted a municipal council meeting. She was sentenced to two weeks' imprisonment and later brought an appeal on points of law before the Dutch Supreme Court.

Her case raised concerns about a practice within that court known as the reservisten system.

Under this practice, a Supreme Court case is assigned to a panel of three or five judges. Other judges from the same division who are not members of that panel may also participate in the deliberations, although they do not vote on the outcome.

The purpose of the practice is to promote consistency in the interpretation and application of the law and to help ensure that the Supreme Court's case-law remains coherent.

Kuijt argued that allowing judges outside the assigned panel to participate in deliberations meant that her case was not being heard by the independent and impartial tribunal required by Article 6 of the Convention.

She also argued that the participation of these additional judges was not sufficiently grounded in law.

The Legal Question

Article 6 § 1 of the European Convention protects the right to a fair hearing by an:

  • independent tribunal;
  • impartial tribunal; and
  • tribunal established by law.

Kuijt argued that the reservisten practice raised concerns about these guarantees.

At first glance, the case therefore appeared to ask a major constitutional question:

Can judges who are not formally assigned to a case participate in the confidential deliberations surrounding that case without violating the right to a fair trial?

But before answering that question, the ECHR had to consider another requirement.

Under Article 35 of the Convention, applicants generally must first use the appropriate remedies available within their own country's legal system before bringing a complaint to Strasbourg.

This principle is known as the exhaustion of domestic remedies.

What Did the Court Decide?

The Grand Chamber declared the application inadmissible.

That is important because the Court was not simply saying that Kuijt's allegations were unimportant.

Instead, the Court focused heavily on whether the Dutch courts had been given a proper opportunity to examine the complaint themselves.

The Court noted that the applicant had used a challenge procedure before the Dutch Supreme Court, but that procedure was limited in scope. It dealt specifically with judicial impartiality.

Her broader arguments concerning judicial independence and whether the tribunal was properly "established by law" could have been raised in the main proceedings before the Supreme Court.

The Court therefore concluded that the national legal system had provided a route through which these arguments could have been examined, but Kuijt had not used that route in the required way.

The Court emphasized the principle of subsidiarity.

In simple terms, this means that responsibility for protecting Convention rights is shared between national authorities and the European Court. National courts should normally have the first opportunity to address an alleged violation before Strasbourg becomes involved.

The Court also noted that the Dutch Supreme Court had previously demonstrated that it could examine whether its own practices were compatible with international human-rights standards.

The Court Did Not Decide That the Reservisten System Violates Article 6

This distinction is especially important.

The Grand Chamber did not make a final ruling that the reservisten practice was compatible with Article 6 in every respect.

Instead, it found that the applicant had not properly exhausted the domestic remedies concerning several parts of her complaint.

As for the specific argument that the practice made the tribunal objectively impartial, the Court found that the applicant's fear could not be regarded as objectively justified.

The application was therefore declared inadmissible by a majority.

My Analysis

I think Kuijt v. the Netherlands demonstrates why legal outcomes cannot always be understood simply by asking whether the applicant "won" or "lost."

At first, the central issue appears to be judicial independence.

Could a judge who is not assigned to a particular case influence the judges who are?

That is a serious question because public confidence in courts depends partly on the belief that cases are decided by judges who are properly assigned to them and who can exercise independent judgment.

At the same time, the ECHR's decision demonstrates another principle that is just as important to the Convention system:

International human-rights protection is not intended to replace national courts.

If a national legal system provides a meaningful opportunity to raise a particular human-rights argument, there is a strong reason to require the applicant to use that opportunity first.

Otherwise, the European Court could become the first court asked to decide questions that national courts had never properly considered.

The case therefore shows the tension between two important ideas.

One is access to international human-rights protection.

The other is respect for national courts and domestic legal procedures.

Neither principle completely disappears just because the other is important.

But Is Exhaustion Always Simple?

This is where I find the case especially interesting.

It may sound straightforward to say:

"Use your national remedies first."

But what happens when the complaint is directed against the very institution that is supposed to provide the remedy?

That creates a difficult procedural problem.

In Kuijt, the applicant argued that it was unrealistic to expect her to challenge the Supreme Court's own internal practice through proceedings before that same court.

The Court nevertheless concluded that the Dutch Supreme Court should have been given the opportunity to consider the complaint through the appropriate domestic procedure.

This raises a broader question:

How much should a person be expected to do within a national legal system before an international court becomes accessible?

Too strict a requirement could make international human-rights protection difficult to reach.

Too weak a requirement could undermine the role of national courts and turn the European Court into a first-instance forum for complaints that should initially be handled domestically.

Why This Case Matters

Kuijt v. the Netherlands is important because it illustrates that human-rights law is not only about the rights written in a treaty.

It is also about procedure.

A person may have a serious legal argument, but the way that argument is brought before the courts can determine whether an international court is even able to examine it.

The judgment also highlights the principle of subsidiarity.

The European Court of Human Rights operates within a system where national courts remain primarily responsible for protecting Convention rights. Strasbourg provides international supervision, but it does not normally replace domestic judicial processes.

For me, the most interesting question left by Kuijt v. the Netherlands is therefore not simply whether the Dutch reservisten system is right or wrong.

When a person believes that the highest court in their country has violated their rights, how can the legal system ensure both meaningful access to justice and respect for the role of national courts?

That question extends far beyond the Netherlands.

It goes to the heart of how international human-rights law is supposed to work.
