Article 4 – Prohibition of Slavery and Forced Labour

A structured legal note on the prohibition of slavery, servitude, forced labour, and human trafficking under the European Convention on Human Rights.

Definition

Article 4 of the European Convention on Human Rights protects individuals from slavery, servitude, and forced or compulsory labour. This right is absolute in relation to slavery and servitude, meaning that they can never be justified. Article 4 also requires States to prevent exploitation and protect individuals from serious forms of abuse, including human trafficking and forced labour.

Legal Provision

Article 4 states:

1. No one shall be held in slavery or servitude.

2. No one shall be required to perform forced or compulsory labour.

3. The term “forced or compulsory labour” shall not include:

(a) work required during lawful detention imposed according to Article 5 of the Convention;
(b) military service or alternative service for conscientious objectors;
(c) service required during emergencies threatening the community;
(d) work forming part of normal civic obligations.

Purpose of Article 4

The purpose of Article 4 is to protect human dignity and personal freedom by preventing individuals from being controlled, exploited, or forced to work against their will. It requires States to create effective laws, investigate abuses, and take reasonable measures to prevent slavery, forced labour, and human trafficking.

The aim of Article 4 is to ensure that no person is treated as property or subjected to exploitation.

Key Principles

  • Absolute Prohibition – Slavery and servitude are completely prohibited.
  • Protection from Forced Labour – Individuals cannot be forced to work through threats or coercion.
  • Positive Obligation – States must take measures to prevent, investigate, and punish exploitation.
  • Human Dignity – Article 4 protects individuals from treatment that removes their freedom and autonomy.
  • Modern Slavery Protection – The Article covers human trafficking and other forms of exploitation.
  • Limited Exceptions – Certain forms of compulsory work are permitted under Article 4(3).
  • Servitude as a Serious Dependency – Servitude exists where a person is placed in a situation of dependency and cannot realistically change their circumstances.

Leading Cases

  • Siliadin v. France – Established that States must provide effective protection against domestic servitude and exploitation.
  • Rantsev v. Cyprus and Russia – Confirmed that human trafficking falls within Article 4 and requires State action to prevent and investigate trafficking.
  • C.N. v. the United Kingdom – Recognised that domestic servitude can amount to a violation of Article 4.
  • Chowdury and Others v. Greece – Confirmed that severe exploitation of migrant workers can constitute forced labour.
  • Van der Mussele v. Belgium – Explained the meaning of “forced labour” and the limits of Article 4(3).

Key Legal Terms

  • Slavery – Treating a person as property and denying their freedom.
  • Servitude – A condition where a person is forced to live and work under another’s control.
  • Forced Labour – Work demanded through threats, pressure, or coercion.
  • Positive Obligation – The State’s duty to protect individuals from exploitation.
  • Human Trafficking – The exploitation of people through recruitment, transport, or control.
  • Exploitation – The unfair use of a person’s vulnerability or labour.

Exam Tips

  • Remember that Article 4 protects against slavery, servitude, forced labour, and trafficking.
  • Explain that slavery and servitude are absolute rights with no exceptions.
  • Mention that States have positive obligations to prevent and investigate exploitation.
  • Use cases such as Siliadin v. France and Rantsev v. Cyprus and Russia to support answers.

Summary

Article 4 protects one of the most fundamental aspects of human dignity: freedom from exploitation and forced control. It prohibits slavery, servitude, and forced labour while requiring States to actively prevent and punish modern forms of exploitation. Through its case law, the European Court of Human Rights has expanded Article 4 to protect individuals from trafficking and other serious abuses.
