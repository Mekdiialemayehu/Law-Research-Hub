S. and Marper v. the United Kingdom (2008)

European Court of Human Rights | Human Rights Law

Case Overview

Case Name: S. and Marper v. the United Kingdom (2008)

Court: European Court of Human Rights (Grand Chamber)

Area of Law: Human Rights Law / Privacy / Data Protection

Key Article: Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights (Right to Respect for Private and Family Life)

Additional Article Raised: Article 14 of the European Convention on Human Rights (Prohibition of Discrimination)

Key Principle: The indefinite retention of fingerprints, DNA samples and DNA profiles of persons who have been arrested but not convicted can amount to a violation of the right to respect for private life under Article 8 of the Convention.

Facts of the Case

The case concerned two applicants, referred to as S. and Marper, who had both been arrested in England and had their fingerprints and DNA taken by the police.

S. was a child at the time of his arrest and was later acquitted. The criminal proceedings against Marper were discontinued. Neither applicant was convicted of a criminal offence.

Despite the absence of convictions, the police refused to destroy their biometric information, relying on domestic law which allowed the continued retention of such material.

At the time, the law in England, Wales and Northern Ireland permitted the systematic and indefinite retention of DNA profiles and fingerprints taken after arrest, even where no conviction followed.

The applicants argued that retaining their biometric information created an unjustified interference with their private life, particularly because DNA material contains highly personal information and can reveal information relevant not only to the individual but potentially to biological relatives.

Key Legal Issue

The central question before the Court was whether the indefinite retention of the applicants' fingerprints, cellular samples and DNA profiles, despite the absence of any conviction, violated their right to respect for private life under Article 8 of the Convention.

The applicants also complained that the retention regime treated convicted and unconvicted persons alike in a way that engaged Article 14.

Legal Arguments

Applicants (S. and Marper)

  • DNA samples and profiles contain deeply personal biological information and therefore fall within the protection of private life.
  • The indefinite retention of biometric information allowed the State to continue storing and comparing their personal data even after their cases had ended without conviction.
  • The retention made them feel stigmatised and treated as though they remained under suspicion despite one applicant being acquitted and the other not being convicted.
  • The applicants argued that there was no adequate justification for treating unconvicted persons in substantially the same way as convicted offenders.
  • The blanket and indefinite nature of the retention policy was disproportionate to the legitimate aim of preventing and detecting crime.

Government (United Kingdom)

  • The Government argued that retaining biometric material was justified as an important tool for preventing and detecting crime.
  • It maintained that DNA profiles were essentially identification codes rather than a complete record of a person's genetic characteristics.
  • The Government argued that the interference with private life was limited and proportionate to the legitimate aim of public safety.
  • It maintained that retaining the material could assist the authorities in solving future crimes and preventing wrongful convictions.

Judgment and Reasoning

Decision

The Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights unanimously held that there had been a violation of Article 8 of the Convention.

The Court found that the retention of the applicants' cellular samples, fingerprints and DNA profiles constituted an interference with their right to respect for private life.

  • Article 8 (Right to Respect for Private and Family Life): The Court held that the blanket and indefinite retention of biometric data from persons who had not been convicted was too broad and failed to strike a fair balance between the competing public and private interests.
  • Article 14 (Prohibition of Discrimination): The Court did not consider it necessary to examine the Article 14 complaint separately because it had already found a violation of Article 8.

Key Judicial Reasoning

  • The Court recognised that fingerprints, cellular samples and DNA profiles contain personal information and therefore fall within the scope of private life protected by Article 8.
  • DNA material was regarded as particularly sensitive because it contains significant information about an individual's identity and biological characteristics.
  • The Court accepted that the State had a legitimate interest in preventing and detecting crime and that biometric databases could serve important public safety purposes.
  • However, the Court found that the retention regime was exceptionally broad because it applied to virtually all persons who had been arrested, regardless of the seriousness of the allegation or the outcome of the proceedings.
  • The lack of a meaningful distinction between convicted and unconvicted persons was a major concern because persons who had not been convicted were effectively subjected to a continuing form of suspicion.
  • The Court emphasised that the presumption of innocence has important consequences for how personal information about unconvicted individuals should be treated.
  • The indefinite nature of the retention increased the seriousness of the interference with private life because the information could remain stored regardless of the person's circumstances or the passage of time.
  • The Court noted that other European States generally used more limited retention periods or different rules based on factors such as the seriousness of the offence and the outcome of proceedings.
  • The United Kingdom had therefore failed to strike a fair balance between the public interest in crime prevention and the applicants' right to respect for private life.

Legal Impact

  • Became a leading European human rights authority on privacy and the retention of biometric information.
  • Confirmed that DNA profiles, cellular samples and fingerprints fall within the scope of Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights.
  • Established that the indefinite retention of biometric data from persons who have not been convicted can be disproportionate.
  • Strengthened the importance of distinguishing between convicted and unconvicted persons when developing data-retention policies.
  • Influenced subsequent debates and reforms concerning police databases, DNA retention and personal data protection in the United Kingdom.
  • Had wider European significance by establishing principles concerning proportionality, privacy and the use of biometric databases.
  • Demonstrated that technological tools used for crime prevention must remain subject to human rights safeguards.

Critical Evaluation

The judgment in S. and Marper v. the United Kingdom is widely regarded as an important protection for personal privacy because it recognised that the collection and retention of biometric information is not simply a neutral administrative procedure.

A major strength of the judgment is its emphasis on the difference between people who have been convicted and those who have not. The Court recognised that retaining the biometric information of unconvicted individuals indefinitely can create a continuing association with criminal suspicion even where the person has been acquitted or the proceedings have been discontinued.

The judgment also demonstrated that Article 8 can adapt to developments in technology. DNA databases and biometric identification systems offer significant benefits for crime detection, but they also give governments powerful tools for collecting and storing highly personal information.

Another strength is that the Court did not reject DNA databases or police use of biometric information altogether. Instead, it focused on the lack of safeguards, the blanket nature of the retention regime and the absence of sufficient distinctions between different categories of individuals.

However, the judgment also presents a difficult policy question. Governments need effective tools to investigate and prevent serious crime, and biometric databases can be valuable in identifying offenders and solving cases. More restrictive retention rules may therefore create practical challenges for law enforcement.

Nevertheless, the Court's approach is persuasive because it demonstrates that public safety cannot automatically justify unlimited government retention of personal information. The State must show that its data-retention policies are proportionate and contain meaningful safeguards.

Overall, S. and Marper illustrates the importance of maintaining a balance between public security, technological development and individual privacy.

Final Conclusion

S. and Marper v. the United Kingdom is a landmark privacy case under Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

The European Court of Human Rights made clear that the indefinite retention of fingerprints, cellular samples and DNA profiles belonging to people who have not been convicted constitutes a serious interference with private life and requires strong justification.

The judgment demonstrated that the State cannot treat all arrested persons in the same way as convicted offenders and must take account of the presumption of innocence when designing biometric data-retention policies.

The case remains highly important in modern discussions about privacy, data protection, digital records and the limits of State surveillance. It shows that technological developments in law enforcement must operate within a framework that protects fundamental human rights.

The case deepened my understanding of Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights and showed me how human rights principles can be applied to modern challenges such as DNA databases and digital surveillance.
