CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Negligence?

Negligence is a tort involving a failure to exercise the level of care that the law reasonably expects in the circumstances, resulting in legally recognized harm to another person.

Unlike contract law, negligence does not generally depend on a voluntary agreement between the parties.

A negligence claim usually requires the claimant to establish:

  1. A duty of care.
  2. A breach of that duty.
  3. Causation of legally recognized harm.
  4. Harm that is not too remote.

The exact requirements vary between legal systems.

CARD 2 · NEGLIGENCE AS A TORT

Negligence as a Tort

Negligence is part of tort law.

Tort law generally concerns duties imposed by law rather than obligations voluntarily created by contract.

Examples

  • A driver owes duties of care to other road users.
  • A doctor may owe duties of care to a patient.
  • A property occupier may owe duties of care to lawful visitors.
  • A manufacturer may owe duties to consumers in appropriate circumstances.
These duties can exist even when the parties never entered into a contract.

A negligence claim commonly involves four main elements:

A claimant generally must establish each required element.

If one essential element is not established, the negligence claim may fail.

Key Question

Did the defendant fail to take reasonable care, causing legally recognized harm to the claimant?

CARD 4 · DUTY OF CARE

Duty of Care

A duty of care is a legal obligation requiring a person to take reasonable care to avoid causing foreseeable harm to others.

The existence of a duty depends on the relationship between the parties and the circumstances.

Examples

  • Drivers to other road users.
  • Doctors to patients.
  • Employers to employees.
  • Manufacturers to consumers.
  • Occupiers to certain visitors.
  • Professionals to people who reasonably rely on their services.

The precise test for duty varies between jurisdictions.

CARD 5 · FORESEEABILITY & PROXIMITY

Foreseeability and Proximity

Foreseeability

Foreseeability asks whether a reasonable person could reasonably have anticipated the possibility of harm.

For example, a driver should reasonably foresee that driving at an excessive speed may endanger pedestrians or other road users.

Proximity

Some legal systems consider proximity when determining whether a duty of care exists.

Proximity broadly refers to the closeness or relationship between the parties.

  • Physical proximity.
  • Legal relationships.
  • Direct interactions.
  • Reliance.
  • Assumption of responsibility.
CARD 6 · BREACH

Breach of Duty

Once a duty of care is established, the claimant generally must show that the defendant breached that duty.

A breach occurs when the defendant's conduct falls below the legally required standard of reasonable care.

The law does not normally require people to eliminate every possible risk.

Instead, it generally requires reasonable care in the circumstances.

Factors Considered

  • Likelihood of harm.
  • Seriousness of potential harm.
  • Cost or difficulty of precautions.
  • Social value of the defendant's conduct.
  • The defendant's role or expertise.
CARD 7 · STANDARD OF CARE

Standard of Care

The standard of care is the level of care expected from a reasonable person in the relevant circumstances.

The Reasonable Person

The reasonable-person standard is an objective legal standard.

The court generally asks:

"What would a reasonably careful person have done in the same circumstances?"

The defendant's personal belief that they acted carefully is not necessarily decisive.

The law focuses on the objective standard required by the circumstances.

CARD 8 · SPECIAL STANDARDS

Special Standards of Care

Professionals

Professionals may be judged according to the standard expected of a reasonably competent member of their profession.

Examples include doctors, lawyers, engineers, accountants, and architects.

Children

Children may be judged according to a standard appropriate to their age and circumstances in legal systems that recognize a special standard for minors.

Physical Disability

Some legal systems take a defendant's physical disability into account when determining the appropriate standard.

The precise approach depends on the jurisdiction.

CARD 9 · CAUSATION

Causation

After establishing breach, the claimant generally must show that the defendant's breach caused the harm.

"Did the defendant's breach actually cause the claimant's loss or injury?"

Factual Causation

A common test for factual causation is the "but for" test.

"Would the harm have occurred but for the defendant's breach?"

For example, if a driver negligently runs a red light and hits a pedestrian, the court may ask whether the injury would have occurred without the driver's negligent conduct.

CARD 10 · MULTIPLE CAUSES & REMOTENESS

Multiple Causes and Remoteness

Multiple Causes

Sometimes harm results from several contributing factors.

  • Two people may independently contribute to an accident.
  • A pre-existing condition may contribute to an injury.
  • Several negligent acts may combine to cause harm.

Intervening Acts

An intervening event can sometimes break the chain of causation.

It may involve another person's action, the claimant's conduct, or an independent natural event.

Remoteness

Even when a defendant's breach factually caused harm, the defendant may not be liable for every consequence.

The law may limit liability to losses that are sufficiently connected to the breach.
CARD 11 · DAMAGE

Damage in Negligence

A negligence claim generally requires legally recognized harm.

Possible Types of Damage

  • Physical injury.
  • Property damage.
  • Financial loss in appropriate circumstances.
  • Psychological harm where recognized by law.

Pure Economic Loss

Pure economic loss refers to financial loss that does not result from physical injury or property damage.

Many legal systems treat pure economic loss cautiously and apply special rules to determine when it is recoverable.

Personal Injury

Personal injury is one of the most common forms of damage in negligence claims.

CARD 12 · DEFENCES

Defences to Negligence

A defendant may have legal defences to a negligence claim.

Contributory Negligence

This occurs when the claimant's own lack of reasonable care contributed to their harm.

In many modern systems, contributory negligence reduces compensation rather than completely preventing recovery.

Voluntary Assumption of Risk

Voluntary assumption of risk, sometimes called volenti, may apply where the claimant knowingly and voluntarily accepted a particular risk.

Simply knowing that an activity involves some risk does not automatically establish legal consent to all possible harm.

CARD 13 · SPECIAL AREAS

Special Areas of Negligence

Medical Negligence

Medical negligence occurs when a healthcare professional fails to meet the applicable standard of care and causes legally recognized harm.

Occupiers' Liability

An occupier of land or premises may owe duties to certain people who enter the property.

Product Liability

Manufacturers and suppliers may potentially be liable when defective products cause harm.

Professional Negligence

Professional negligence occurs when a professional fails to meet the required professional standard of care.

CARD 14 · RELATED DOCTRINES

Related Negligence Doctrines

Negligence Per Se

Some legal systems recognize negligence per se, where violation of a statute designed to protect a particular class from a particular type of harm may help establish negligence.

Res Ipsa Loquitur

Res ipsa loquitur means roughly "the thing speaks for itself."

In certain circumstances, it can allow an inference of negligence from the nature of an accident.

Vicarious Liability

Employers may sometimes be legally responsible for negligent acts of employees performed in the course of employment.

These doctrines do not automatically establish negligence. Their availability and effect vary between jurisdictions.
CARD 15 · KEY TAKEAWAY

The Negligence Test

1. Duty of Care
Did the defendant owe a duty to the claimant?

↓

2. Breach
Did the defendant fail to meet the required standard of care?

↓

3. Causation
Did the breach cause the harm?

↓

4. Remoteness
Was the harm sufficiently connected to the breach?

↓

5. Damage
Did the claimant suffer legally recognized loss or injury?

A simple formula is:

Negligence = Failure to take reasonable care + Legally recognized harm

Negligence is therefore different from intentional wrongdoing and from breach of contract. It focuses on whether a person failed to take reasonable care in circumstances where the law required them to do so.
