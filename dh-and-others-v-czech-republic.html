D.H. and Others v. the Czech Republic (2007)

European Court of Human Rights | Human Rights Law

Case Overview

Case Name: D.H. and Others v. the Czech Republic (2007)

Court: European Court of Human Rights (Grand Chamber)

Area of Law: Human Rights Law / Equality and Non-Discrimination

Key Articles: Article 14 of the European Convention on Human Rights (Prohibition of Discrimination), taken together with Article 2 of Protocol No. 1 (Right to Education)

Key Principle: States must ensure that education systems do not discriminate against individuals on the basis of race or ethnic origin. Even if a policy appears neutral, it may violate the Convention if it disproportionately disadvantages a particular group without objective and reasonable justification.

Facts of the Case

The applicants were eighteen Czech nationals of Roma origin who had been placed in special schools in Ostrava between 1996 and 1999. These schools were intended for children with mental disabilities and followed a simplified curriculum that significantly limited students' future educational and employment opportunities.

The applicants argued that they had been placed in these schools because of their ethnic origin rather than any genuine intellectual disability.

Statistical evidence showed that Roma children were far more likely than non-Roma children to be assigned to special schools. Although Roma children represented only a small percentage of the overall student population, they made up more than half of the pupils in special schools.

After their complaints were rejected by the Czech authorities and the Constitutional Court, the applicants brought their case before the European Court of Human Rights, alleging discrimination in their right to education.

Key Legal Issue

The central question before the Court was whether the placement of Roma children in special schools amounted to discrimination based on ethnic origin, in violation of Article 14 of the European Convention on Human Rights, taken together with Article 2 of Protocol No. 1.

Legal Arguments

Applicants (D.H. and Others)

  • The system of placing children in special schools had a discriminatory effect on Roma children.
  • Psychological tests used to assess children were culturally biased and did not adequately take into account linguistic and social differences.
  • Roma children were disproportionately placed in special schools and consequently received an inferior education.
  • Placement in special schools significantly restricted their future educational and employment opportunities.
  • The applicants argued that the discriminatory effect of the system violated Article 14 in conjunction with Article 2 of Protocol No. 1.

Government (Czech Republic)

  • The Government argued that school placement decisions were based on psychological assessments rather than ethnic origin.
  • It maintained that the education system was designed to meet the needs of children requiring special educational support.
  • The Government argued that parental consent was obtained before children were placed in special schools.
  • It denied that the system was intended to discriminate against Roma children.

Judgment and Reasoning

Decision

The Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights held that the Czech Republic had violated Article 14 of the Convention in conjunction with Article 2 of Protocol No. 1.

The Court accepted the statistical evidence showing that Roma children were significantly overrepresented in special schools. It recognised that discrimination can occur indirectly where a seemingly neutral policy has a disproportionately negative effect on a particular ethnic group.

  • Article 14 (Prohibition of Discrimination): The Court found that the education system had a disproportionately harmful effect on Roma children and that the difference in treatment was not objectively and reasonably justified.
  • Article 2 of Protocol No. 1 (Right to Education): The Court found that the unequal educational opportunities resulting from the placement system affected the applicants' right to education.

Key Judicial Reasoning

  • The Court recognised that discrimination does not require proof of an intention to discriminate. A policy that appears neutral may still violate the Convention if it produces a disproportionately harmful effect on a protected group.
  • Reliable statistical evidence can be important in establishing a case of indirect discrimination where it demonstrates a significant disparity between different groups.
  • Once the applicants established a prima facie case of discriminatory effect, the Government was required to provide an objective and reasonable justification for the difference in treatment.
  • The Court found that the Government had failed to provide a sufficient justification for the disproportionate placement of Roma children in special schools.
  • The psychological tests used to determine placement did not adequately take into account the cultural and linguistic background of Roma children, increasing the risk of incorrect classification.
  • The Court held that parental consent could not, by itself, remove the discriminatory nature of the system, particularly where parents were not fully informed of the long-term consequences of placement in special schools.
  • The judgment emphasised the importance of equal access to education and the need for educational systems to provide fair and effective opportunities to children from minority groups.

Legal Impact

  • Established an important principle that indirect discrimination can be established through reliable statistical evidence.
  • Confirmed that a neutral policy may violate Article 14 when it disproportionately disadvantages a protected group without objective and reasonable justification.
  • Strengthened the protection of Roma children and other minority groups against discrimination in education.
  • Emphasised that educational assessments and placement procedures should be fair, objective, and free from ethnic bias.
  • Influenced later European human rights cases concerning equality, education, and racial discrimination.
  • Encouraged efforts to reduce the segregation of Roma children in schools across Europe and to improve equal access to education.

Critical Evaluation

The judgment in D.H. and Others v. the Czech Republic (2007) is widely regarded as a landmark decision in European human rights law because it strengthened protection against indirect racial discrimination in education. Before this case, proving discrimination often required evidence of intentional unequal treatment. The Court recognised that discrimination may also occur where a seemingly neutral policy has a disproportionately harmful effect on a particular ethnic group.

An important aspect of the judgment was the Court's approach to statistical evidence and the burden of proof. Once the applicants presented convincing evidence demonstrating that Roma children were disproportionately placed in special schools, the Government was required to provide an objective and reasonable justification for this unequal impact. The Government failed to demonstrate that the difference in treatment was justified, reinforcing the principle that States must actively ensure equality in practice rather than merely in law.

The judgment also reinforced the importance of equal access to education. The Court recognised that placing Roma children in special schools based on assessments that did not adequately account for cultural and linguistic differences could deny them equal educational opportunities and limit their future prospects.

Another significant feature of the judgment was its recognition that discriminatory systems do not need to be intentionally designed to discriminate. This expanded the understanding of equality under the Convention and made it possible to challenge systemic forms of discrimination by examining their real-world effects.

However, some commentators have argued that the judgment did not immediately eliminate the segregation of Roma children in education. Despite the Court's ruling, educational segregation continued in some areas, demonstrating that legal judgments alone cannot resolve deeply rooted social inequalities.

Meaningful equality requires governments to implement practical reforms, improve assessment methods, monitor educational practices, and maintain long-term political commitment to preventing discrimination.

Nevertheless, the decision remains highly influential because it established important principles on indirect discrimination and equality that continue to guide the European Court of Human Rights and domestic courts throughout Europe.

Final Conclusion

D.H. and Others v. the Czech Republic is one of the most significant decisions on equality and the right to education under the European Convention on Human Rights.

The European Court of Human Rights held that the disproportionate placement of Roma children in special schools amounted to indirect racial discrimination, violating Article 14 in conjunction with Article 2 of Protocol No. 1.

By recognising that discriminatory effects, even without discriminatory intent, can breach the Convention, the judgment strengthened the protection of minority groups and expanded the understanding of equality under European human rights law.

The case remains a cornerstone of European anti-discrimination jurisprudence and continues to influence how courts assess equality, education, and the protection of vulnerable groups across Europe.

Personal Reflection

I found D.H. and Others v. the Czech Republic particularly important because it shows that discrimination is not always obvious or intentional. A policy may appear fair on its face, but if it disproportionately disadvantages a particular group, it can still violate human rights.

This case also demonstrates the vital role of education in ensuring equality and protecting human dignity. It has increased my understanding of indirect discrimination and the responsibility of States to provide equal opportunities for all children regardless of their ethnic background.

It also showed me that achieving equality requires examining the real effects of laws and policies rather than simply their wording.

Studying this case has helped me appreciate how the European Court of Human Rights uses the Convention to address systemic inequality and promote social justice.
