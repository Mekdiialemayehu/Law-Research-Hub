Forced Marriage

Understanding the legal protection of free and full consent to marriage through international, African, and Ethiopian law.

Definition Why It Matters International Law African Framework Key Principles Barriers Ethiopia Research Question Sources
Core legal question: What legal protections ensure that a person can choose whether and whom to marry and can enter marriage only with free and full consent?
01 · Definition

What is Forced Marriage?

Forced marriage is a marriage in which one or both people do not give free and full consent to the marriage. Pressure or coercion may take different forms, including threats, intimidation, deception, or other circumstances that prevent a person from making a genuinely voluntary choice.

The legal principle is not simply that a marriage should be formally registered. The central question is whether each person had the freedom to decide whether to marry and whom to marry. International human-rights law protects this freedom as part of equality and personal autonomy in marriage and family relations.

The key idea is simple: marriage requires free and full consent, not merely the absence of an objection.
02 · Why It Matters

Why is Forced Marriage a Human-Rights Issue?

Forced marriage can interfere with fundamental rights including equality, dignity, personal autonomy, freedom from violence and coercion, and the right to choose whether and whom to marry.

The issue is especially important where pressure comes from family members, communities, or other private individuals. Human-rights law can require states to create legal protections and institutions capable of preventing forced marriage, protecting people at risk, and providing effective remedies.

Child marriage is closely related but should not be treated as exactly the same legal concept. A child marriage may involve circumstances in which meaningful consent cannot legally be given, while a forced marriage can also involve an adult whose free and full consent has been overcome by coercion.

03 · International Legal Framework

International Protection

CEDAW · Article 16

Free and Full Consent to Marriage

Article 16 of CEDAW requires States Parties to eliminate discrimination against women in marriage and family relations. It specifically requires equality between men and women, including the same right freely to choose a spouse and to enter into marriage only with their free and full consent.

UDHR · Article 16

Right to Marry by Free Consent

Article 16 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights recognizes the right of men and women of full age to marry and found a family without limitation due to race, nationality, or religion. It also states that marriage should be entered into only with the free and full consent of the intending spouses.

Read Article 16 →

ICCPR · Article 23

Marriage and Consent

The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights recognizes the right of men and women of marriageable age to marry and found a family and requires that no marriage be entered into without the free and full consent of the intending spouses.

Read the ICCPR →

CEDAW · General Recommendation No. 21

Equality in Marriage and Family Relations

The CEDAW Committee has explained that a woman's right to choose a spouse and enter marriage freely is central to her dignity and equality. General Recommendation No. 21 examines the consequences of inequality in marriage and emphasizes the importance of free consent.

04 · African Human-Rights Law

The African Legal Framework

Maputo Protocol · Article 6

Marriage and Free Consent

Article 6 requires states to ensure equality between women and men in marriage and specifically requires national measures providing that no marriage shall take place without the free and full consent of both parties. It also establishes a minimum marriage age of 18 years for women and requires marriages to be recorded and registered.

Read the Maputo Protocol →

African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child · Article 21

Child Marriage

The African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child requires states to eliminate harmful social and cultural practices affecting children. It expressly prohibits child marriage and betrothal and requires legislation establishing 18 as the minimum age of marriage.

Read the Children's Charter →

African Charter · Article 18

Family Protection and Equality

Article 18 recognizes the family as a natural unit and basis of society and requires states to ensure equality of rights and responsibilities of spouses with regard to children during marriage and when marriage ends.

Read the African Charter →

Ethiopia · Regional obligations

Ethiopia and the Maputo Protocol

Ethiopia ratified the Maputo Protocol on 18 July 2018. Its protections concerning marriage, consent, harmful practices, and women's rights therefore form part of the regional human-rights framework relevant to Ethiopia.

05 · Key Legal Principles

What Does Free Consent Require?

Freedom of Choice

A person must be free to decide whether to marry and whom to marry.

Free Consent

Consent must be voluntary rather than produced through threats, coercion, intimidation, or other improper pressure.

Full Consent

Consent must come from the person entering the marriage and must be genuine rather than assumed from silence or family approval.

Equality

Women and men should have equal rights in choosing a spouse and entering marriage.

Legal Protection

States should have laws and institutions capable of preventing forced marriage and protecting people at risk.

Effective Remedies

People affected by forced marriage should be able to access appropriate legal protection and remedies.

06 · Understanding the Problem

Why Can Forced Marriage Persist?

Human-rights analysis examines not only the marriage itself but also the circumstances that can make free choice difficult or impossible.

Family pressure
Family expectations may create intense pressure on a person to marry someone they do not freely choose.
Social norms
Community expectations about gender, family honour, or marriage can limit genuine freedom of choice.
Economic dependence
Dependence on relatives or a future spouse can make refusing a proposed marriage more difficult.
Gender inequality
Unequal social or legal power can reduce a woman's ability to exercise her right to choose freely.
Child marriage
Children require special protection because marriage during childhood can undermine education, autonomy, development, and other rights.
Limited access to justice
Distance, cost, lack of information, fear, or weak protection mechanisms can make legal safeguards difficult to use.
07 · Ethiopia

Forced Marriage in Ethiopia

Ethiopia's constitutional and family-law framework recognizes marriage as a matter requiring the free and full consent of the parties and protects women and girls against discrimination and harmful practices.

Article 34 · Marital, Personal and Family Rights

The Constitution provides that men and women who have attained marriageable age as defined by law have the right to marry and found a family. It expressly states that marriage may be entered into only with the free and full consent of the intending spouses.

Article 35 · Rights of Women

The Constitution guarantees women's equal enjoyment of rights and requires measures to prevent and eliminate discrimination against women. It also recognizes women's right to protection from harmful customs and practices.

Article 25 · Right to Equality

The Constitution provides equal protection of the law and prohibits discrimination on grounds including sex. This equality principle is relevant when examining whether women and girls receive effective protection from forced marriage.

Marriage under Ethiopian law

Ethiopia's family-law framework reinforces the constitutional principle that consent is essential to a valid marriage. The Revised Family Code, applicable in Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa, identifies the free and full consent of the spouses as an essential condition of marriage.

Why the Ethiopian context matters

The existence of a legal rule against forced marriage does not by itself answer whether everyone can safely exercise the right to choose. A complete human-rights analysis therefore asks how effectively laws are enforced, whether people can obtain protection, and whether social and economic barriers prevent genuine freedom of choice.

08 · Legal Analysis

Law on Paper vs. Freedom in Practice

Forced marriage illustrates the difference between formal legal protection and the practical ability to exercise a right.

Legal promise Practical question
Marriage requires free and full consent. Can a person safely refuse a marriage without threats, coercion, or serious consequences?
Women and men have equal rights in marriage. Do women and girls have the same real ability to choose whether and whom to marry?
Child marriage is prohibited under the African children's rights framework. Are laws protecting children from marriage effectively implemented in practice?
People should have legal protection and remedies. Can a person at risk obtain effective protection from authorities before the forced marriage occurs?
09 · Research Question

Think Like a Human-Rights Researcher

If marriage is protected only when it is based on free and full consent, what responsibility does a state have when family, social, or economic pressure prevents a person from making a genuinely voluntary choice?

A strong legal analysis should ask three things: Was consent genuinely free? What legal protections were available? Could the person actually access those protections in practice?
