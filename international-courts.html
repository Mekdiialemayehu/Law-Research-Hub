CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Are International Courts?

International courts are judicial institutions established under international law to decide legal disputes, interpret international rules, determine responsibility, and, in some cases, protect individual rights.

Different international courts have different jurisdictions, legal mandates, parties, subject matters, procedures, and remedies.

Some primarily hear disputes between states. Others can hear cases involving individuals, international organizations, or other entities depending on their legal framework.

Which court has authority? → What can it decide? → What remedy is available?

This principle is fundamental to international litigation because there is no single international court with authority over every international dispute.

CARD 2 · IMPORTANCE

Why International Courts Matter

International courts help ensure that international law can be interpreted and applied through legal procedures rather than relying entirely on political negotiation.

They can:

  • Resolve international disputes.
  • Interpret treaties.
  • Determine whether international obligations have been breached.
  • Develop international legal principles through judgments.
  • Protect human rights in certain regional systems.
  • Hold individuals criminally responsible under international criminal law.
  • Order or award certain remedies.
International courts therefore contribute to the international rule of law by providing judicial mechanisms for resolving legal disputes.

Courts vs. Domestic Courts

A domestic court normally operates within the legal system of one state. An international court operates under an international legal instrument and exercises the jurisdiction provided by that instrument.

CARD 3 · JURISDICTION

International Jurisdiction

Jurisdiction means the legal authority of a court to hear and decide a case.

Before considering the merits of a dispute, an international court generally needs to determine whether it has jurisdiction.

Jurisdiction can concern:

  • The parties.
  • The subject matter.
  • The relevant time period.
  • The territorial scope.
  • The legal basis for bringing the case.
Who? + What? + When? + Where? + Why does this court have authority?

Jurisdiction is therefore one of the most important preliminary issues in international litigation.

CARD 4 · CONSENT

Consent and International Jurisdiction

Unlike domestic courts, international courts often depend significantly on state consent.

A state may consent to international jurisdiction through:

  • A treaty.
  • A special agreement.
  • A declaration.
  • An optional clause.
  • A compromissory clause.
  • Membership in an international legal system, depending on its rules.
A state cannot generally be assumed to have accepted the jurisdiction of every international court.

The exact basis of jurisdiction depends on the legal instrument establishing the particular court.

Why Consent Matters

Once jurisdiction has been validly accepted, the state may be bound by the court's governing legal framework.

CARD 5 · ICJ

International Court of Justice

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) is the principal judicial organ of the United Nations.

It was established by the UN Charter and operates under the Statute of the International Court of Justice.

The ICJ primarily deals with:

  • Disputes between states.
  • Advisory opinions on legal questions referred to it by authorized United Nations organs and agencies.

Parties Before the ICJ

Only states can be parties in contentious cases before the ICJ.

State A v. State B → Possible ICJ contentious case

Individual A v. State B → Not an ordinary ICJ contentious case

Individuals and private companies cannot directly bring ordinary contentious cases before the ICJ.

CARD 6 · ICJ PROCEDURE

ICJ Cases and Advisory Opinions

Contentious Cases

In a contentious case, the ICJ determines a legal dispute between states.

Cases may concern:

  • Treaty interpretation.
  • State responsibility.
  • Territorial disputes.
  • Diplomatic relations.
  • Use of force.
  • Genocide.
  • Maritime boundaries.
  • Other questions of international law.

Advisory Opinions

The ICJ can also issue advisory opinions on legal questions requested by the UN General Assembly, Security Council, and certain authorized UN agencies and organizations.

An advisory opinion is different from a contentious judgment between opposing states, although advisory opinions can have significant legal and interpretive importance.
CARD 7 · ICJ LAW

ICJ Sources, Judgments & State Responsibility

Under Article 38 of the ICJ Statute, the Court may consider:

  • International conventions.
  • International custom.
  • General principles of law.
  • Judicial decisions and scholarly writings as subsidiary means for determining rules of law.

Judgments

An ICJ judgment is legally binding on the parties to that particular case under the conditions established by the Court's Statute.

The ICJ does not operate under a strict doctrine of stare decisis. Previous decisions can nevertheless be highly persuasive when interpreting international law.

Article 59 of the ICJ Statute provides that the decision of the Court has binding force between the parties and in respect of that particular case.

State Responsibility

Where the Court has jurisdiction, it can determine whether a state has breached an international obligation and establish the appropriate legal consequences.

CARD 8 · ICC

International Criminal Court

The International Criminal Court (ICC) is different from the ICJ because it focuses on individual criminal responsibility, not primarily state responsibility.

Its jurisdiction operates within the framework of the Rome Statute and includes specified international crimes.

  • Genocide.
  • Crimes against humanity.
  • War crimes.
  • The crime of aggression, subject to applicable jurisdictional rules.
ICJ ICC
Mainly disputes between states. Individual criminal responsibility.
Principal judicial organ of the UN. Independent international criminal court created by the Rome Statute.

CARD 9 · HUMAN RIGHTS

Regional Human-Rights Courts

Some international courts operate at the regional level and focus primarily on human-rights protection.

Important examples include:

  • European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR).
  • Inter-American Court of Human Rights.
  • African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights.

European Court of Human Rights

The ECtHR interprets and applies the European Convention on Human Rights.

It can hear:

  • Individual applications.
  • Inter-State cases.
Unlike the ICJ, the ECtHR can receive individual applications within the Convention system when the applicable procedural and admissibility requirements are satisfied.

Individual Applications

An individual who claims to be a victim of a Convention violation may apply if requirements such as victim status, exhaustion of domestic remedies, applicable time limits, and jurisdictional requirements are satisfied.

CARD 10 · REGIONAL COURTS

Inter-American & African Courts

Inter-American Court of Human Rights

The Inter-American Court operates within the Inter-American human-rights system and interprets and applies the American Convention on Human Rights and other applicable instruments within its jurisdiction.

Its work can concern:

  • Right to life.
  • Freedom from torture.
  • Fair trial.
  • Freedom of expression.
  • Indigenous rights.
  • Disappearances.
  • State responsibility for human-rights violations.

African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights

The African Court operates under the African human-rights system and interprets relevant African human-rights instruments.

It can address:

  • Human rights.
  • Peoples' rights.
  • State obligations.
  • Remedies for violations.
Access to regional courts depends on the specific treaty, jurisdictional rules, and procedural requirements of each system.
CARD 11 · ITLOS

International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea

The International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (ITLOS) is a judicial body established under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS).

It deals with disputes concerning the law of the sea.

Its work can involve:

  • Maritime boundaries.
  • Navigation.
  • Fisheries.
  • Marine environmental protection.
  • Seabed activities.
  • Detention of vessels in certain circumstances.

Maritime Disputes

A disagreement between states may concern territorial seas, exclusive economic zones, or continental shelves.

Depending on the relevant agreements and jurisdictional requirements, a maritime dispute may potentially be considered by ITLOS or another appropriate international court or tribunal.

UNCLOS → Law of the Sea → Maritime Disputes → Appropriate Tribunal
CARD 12 · ARBITRATION

International Arbitration

Not every international dispute is decided by a permanent court. States may agree to submit disputes to arbitration.

International investment disputes can also be heard through arbitral mechanisms.

Investment Disputes

For example, ICSID provides a framework for resolving certain investment disputes between states and foreign investors.

Investor ↔ State

State ↔ State

CARD 13 · ADMISSIBILITY

Jurisdiction, Standing & Admissibility

Jurisdiction Ratione Personae

Ratione personae concerns the persons or entities that may appear before a court.

  • ICJ → states in contentious cases.
  • ICC → individuals.
  • ECtHR → individuals and states within its Convention system.
  • Investment arbitration → may involve investors and states.

Jurisdiction Ratione Materiae

Ratione materiae concerns the subject matter over which a court has jurisdiction.

Temporal & Territorial Jurisdiction

A court may need to determine whether the alleged events occurred during the relevant period and within the territorial or jurisdictional scope of its legal instrument.

Admissibility

Even when a court has jurisdiction, a case may still be inadmissible because of procedural requirements such as failure to exhaust domestic remedies, late filing, lack of victim status, duplicate proceedings, or insufficient legal basis.

Jurisdiction asks: Does the court have legal authority?

Admissibility asks: Does this particular claim satisfy the requirements for consideration?
CARD 14 · PROCEDURE & REMEDIES

International Court Procedure

Evidence

International courts rely on evidence to establish facts. Evidence may include documents, official records, witness testimony, expert reports, photographs, maps, scientific evidence, digital material, and diplomatic communications.

Written Proceedings

International litigation often includes applications, memorials, counter-memorials, written observations, preliminary objections, and written responses.

Oral Proceedings

During hearings, lawyers present arguments, judges ask questions, parties respond to legal issues, and evidence or submissions may be clarified.

Preliminary Objections

A party may challenge jurisdiction or admissibility before the court considers the merits.

Provisional Measures

Some courts can order provisional measures to protect rights or preserve the situation while a case is pending and prevent serious or irreparable harm.

Remedies

Depending on the court, remedies may include:

  • Compensation.
  • Restitution.
  • Declaration of a violation.
  • Orders to cease unlawful conduct.
  • Guarantees of non-repetition.
  • Other appropriate relief.
CARD 15 · KEY TAKEAWAY

International Courts: Key Principles

International courts form a diverse system rather than a single hierarchy. Each institution has its own jurisdiction, parties, subject matter, procedures, remedies, and legal framework.

Court Main Focus Typical Parties
ICJ Interstate disputes and advisory opinions States
ICC International crimes Individuals accused of crimes
ECtHR European human rights Individuals and states within its system
Inter-American Court Inter-American human rights States and eligible applicants within its system
African Court African human and peoples' rights States and eligible applicants under its system
ITLOS Law of the sea States and other entities where permitted
Which court? → Who can bring the case? → What law applies? → Is it admissible? → What remedy is available?

Key Takeaway

  • ICJ: Interstate disputes and international law.
  • ICC: Individual responsibility for specified international crimes.
  • Regional human-rights courts: Protection and interpretation of regional human-rights treaties.
  • ITLOS: Law of the sea.
  • International arbitration: Disputes submitted to arbitrators according to an applicable agreement.

International courts can determine state responsibility, individual criminal responsibility in appropriate cases, treaty interpretation, human-rights violations, and other legal questions within their jurisdiction.

Before asking whether a claim is legally justified, an international lawyer must first ask: "Which court has jurisdiction?"
