Absolute vs. Qualified Rights

Understanding the different levels of protection given to human rights and the legal principles governing restrictions on qualified rights.

Human Rights and Different Levels of Protection

Human rights are fundamental rights and freedoms that belong to individuals because they are human.

Human-rights law does not treat every right in exactly the same way. Some rights are absolute, while others are qualified.

Key Question: Can the government lawfully limit this right, and if so, under what conditions?

The exact classification of rights can vary between legal systems, constitutions, and international human-rights instruments.

What Are Absolute and Qualified Rights?

An absolute right is a human right that cannot ordinarily be restricted or balanced against competing interests.

A qualified right may be restricted in certain circumstances, provided that the government satisfies the legal requirements established by the relevant human-rights framework.

Simple distinction:
Absolute right = the state cannot ordinarily justify violating it.
Qualified right = the state may restrict it when the law permits the restriction and the required conditions are satisfied.

This distinction is important because it determines how much freedom governments have when responding to competing public interests.

Absolute Rights

Absolute rights receive particularly strong protection. A government cannot normally justify violating them by arguing that the violation would benefit society.

Common examples include:

  • The prohibition of torture.
  • The prohibition of slavery and servitude.
  • The protection against retroactive criminal punishment in its protected form.

The exact scope of an absolute right depends on the legal instrument being applied.

A government's claim that a violation is necessary for national security, public safety, or political necessity does not normally make an otherwise prohibited violation lawful.

Qualified Rights

Qualified rights are protected rights that may be restricted in defined circumstances.

Common examples include:

  • Freedom of expression.
  • Freedom of assembly.
  • Freedom of association.
  • Respect for private and family life.
  • Freedom of movement in relevant legal frameworks.

A qualified right does not mean that the government has unlimited power.

Restrictions normally need to satisfy requirements such as legality, legitimate aim, necessity, and proportionality.

Legal Basis and Legitimate Aim

Legal Basis

A restriction should have a legal basis rather than being imposed arbitrarily by government officials.

This promotes predictability, transparency, accountability, and protection against arbitrary state action.

Legitimate Aim

The restriction must normally pursue an objective recognized by the applicable human-rights framework.

  • National security.
  • Public safety.
  • Prevention of crime.
  • Protection of public health.
  • Protection of the rights of others.
Not every government objective automatically qualifies as a legitimate aim. The applicable legal instrument determines which objectives are permitted.
Card 5 of 15

Necessity

A restriction must generally be necessary for achieving the legitimate aim.

The government should demonstrate a genuine need for the interference rather than relying only on convenience or preference.

A court may consider:

  • Whether there was a genuine need for the restriction.
  • Whether the restriction was connected to the objective.
  • Whether a less restrictive measure could have achieved the same purpose.
Necessity asks whether the government really needed to interfere with the right in the way that it did.
Card 6 of 15

Proportionality

Proportionality is one of the most important principles governing restrictions on qualified rights.

It requires consideration of the relationship between the individual's right and the government's legitimate objective.

A proportionate restriction should:

  • Serve a legitimate objective.
  • Be connected to that objective.
  • Be necessary.
  • Not impose an excessive burden on the individual.
Example: If public safety can be protected through a less restrictive measure, imposing a much more severe restriction may be disproportionate.
Card 7 of 15

The Proportionality Test

When reviewing a restriction on a qualified right, a court may ask:

  1. Is there a legal basis for the restriction?
  2. Is there a legitimate aim?
  3. Is the restriction connected to that aim?
  4. Is the restriction necessary?
  5. Does it place an excessive burden on the individual?
The precise formulation of the proportionality test differs between legal systems and courts.

The central idea is that government interference should not go further than the law legitimately permits.

Examples of Qualified Rights

Freedom of Expression

People generally have the right to express opinions, political views, information, criticism of government, and artistic or academic ideas.

However, expression may sometimes be restricted to protect interests such as national security, public safety, or the rights and reputation of others.

Freedom of Assembly

Individuals may gather for lawful purposes, including demonstrations, peaceful protests, meetings, and community activities.

Freedom of Association

Individuals may form or join organizations such as civil-society groups, professional organizations, political organizations, trade unions, and community groups.

Card 9 of 15

Privacy, Religion, and Freedom of Movement

Private and Family Life

The right to respect for private and family life can cover privacy, family relationships, home life, correspondence, and aspects of personal autonomy.

Religion and Belief

Human-rights law can distinguish between the internal freedom to hold a belief and the external manifestation of that belief.

The internal freedom of thought and conscience receives particularly strong protection, while manifestation may be subject to lawful restrictions under certain legal frameworks.

Freedom of Movement

Freedom of movement may protect a person's ability to move within a country or leave a country, subject to limitations recognized by the relevant legal system.

Absolute Rights: Important Examples

Prohibition of Torture

The prohibition of torture is one of the clearest examples of an absolute human-rights protection.

Torture cannot ordinarily be justified by arguing that it is necessary to obtain information or protect national security.

Prohibition of Slavery

Human-rights law protects individuals from being treated as property and prohibits slavery and the slave trade.

Freedom from Retroactive Criminal Punishment

The principle of legality protects individuals from being punished for conduct that was not criminal when it occurred and generally protects against a heavier criminal penalty than the one applicable at the relevant time.

Emergencies and Derogation

Emergencies can create difficult questions concerning human rights. Governments may have special powers during war or serious threats to the life of a nation.

Derogation

Derogation refers to a temporary suspension or modification of certain treaty obligations during a qualifying emergency.

Derogation is different from an ordinary restriction on a qualified right.

Not every right can be derogated from. International human-rights treaties protect certain fundamental rights even during emergencies.

For example, the ICCPR identifies certain rights that are non-derogable during a qualifying public emergency.

Card 12 of 15

State Power and Human-Rights Obligations

Human-rights law imposes both negative and positive obligations on states.

Negative Obligations

The state must refrain from unjustifiably interfering with protected rights.

Positive Obligations

The state may also be required to take reasonable measures to protect individuals and make rights effective.

Protecting individuals from torture may require more than simply banning torture. States may also need effective laws, investigations, accountability, and safeguards against abuse.
Card 13 of 15

Human Rights, National Security, and Public Interests

Governments sometimes rely on national security, public safety, public health, or the rights of others to justify restrictions on qualified rights.

These objectives can sometimes constitute legitimate aims, but they do not automatically make every restriction lawful.

The court may ask:

  • Was the restriction authorized by law?
  • Was the objective legitimate?
  • Was the restriction necessary?
  • Was it proportionate?
  • Were procedural safeguards followed?
Public interest arguments must still operate inside the human-rights framework.
Card 14 of 15

Judicial Review and Margin of Appreciation

Judicial Review

Courts can review government restrictions on qualified rights. They may determine whether the restriction satisfies the relevant legal standards.

Margin of Appreciation

In European human-rights jurisprudence, the margin of appreciation recognizes that national authorities may have some discretion when balancing rights and legitimate public interests.

The extent of that discretion can depend on the nature of the right, the seriousness of the interference, and the circumstances of the case.

A margin of appreciation does not give governments unlimited freedom to restrict human rights.

Absolute vs. Qualified Rights

Absolute Rights Qualified Rights
Cannot ordinarily be restricted. May be restricted in certain circumstances.
Receive especially strong protection. Restrictions are subject to defined legal requirements.
Cannot normally be balanced away by public interest arguments. May involve balancing competing interests.
Example: prohibition of torture. Example: freedom of expression.
Key Takeaway: Absolute rights generally cannot be justified away through ordinary proportionality balancing, while qualified rights may be restricted where the applicable legal requirements are satisfied.

Always examine the exact wording of the relevant constitution, statute, or human-rights instrument because the classification and scope of rights can vary between legal systems.

Remember:

Absolute Right → The state cannot ordinarily justify violating it through ordinary balancing.

Qualified Right → The state may restrict it when the restriction is lawful, pursues a legitimate aim, is necessary, and is proportionate.
