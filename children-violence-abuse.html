Violence and Abuse Against Children

Understanding violence and abuse against children through international, African, and Ethiopian human-rights law.

Core legal question: What legal duties do states have to prevent, identify, investigate, and respond effectively to violence, abuse, neglect, and exploitation against children?
01 · Definition

What is Violence and Abuse Against Children?

Violence against children includes physical or mental violence, abuse, neglect, negligent treatment, maltreatment, exploitation, and sexual abuse. It can occur in the home, at school, in institutions, in communities, online, or anywhere else children live and interact.

The Convention on the Rights of the Child takes a broad approach to protection. It does not limit violence to one type of injury or one particular setting. Instead, it requires states to protect children from violence and abuse while they are in the care of parents, guardians, or any other person responsible for them.

Violence against children can therefore involve both direct acts and failures to protect. A human-rights analysis may ask not only who caused the harm, but also whether adults and public authorities took reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable abuse and respond effectively when concerns became known.

The key idea is simple: children have an independent right to protection from violence, abuse, neglect, and exploitation.
02 · Why It Matters

Why is Violence Against Children a Human-Rights Issue?

Violence against children can interfere with fundamental rights including the rights to life, dignity, health, education, development, physical and mental integrity, protection from exploitation, and freedom from cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment.

Children are also legally recognized as rights-holders whose age and developmental needs require particular protection. The best-interests principle means that decisions concerning children should place their welfare and rights at the centre of legal and administrative action.

Human-rights law therefore looks beyond individual incidents and asks whether systems of protection are functioning. This can include reporting mechanisms, investigations, social services, child-friendly justice procedures, medical and psychological support, and measures designed to prevent violence before it occurs.

The problem is global. UNICEF describes violence against children as occurring in every country and across homes, schools, communities, and online environments. 2

03 · International Legal Framework

International Protection

Convention on the Rights of the Child · Article 19

Freedom from Violence and Abuse

Article 19 of the Convention on the Rights of the Child requires states to take appropriate legislative, administrative, social, and educational measures to protect children from physical or mental violence, injury or abuse, neglect, negligent treatment, maltreatment, or exploitation, including sexual abuse.

The protection is therefore broader than criminal punishment alone. States are expected to establish systems for prevention, identification, reporting, referral, investigation, treatment, follow-up, and, where appropriate, judicial involvement.

CRC · General Comment No. 13

The Right to Freedom from All Forms of Violence

General Comment No. 13 provides a detailed interpretation of Article 19. It emphasizes that states must develop comprehensive national systems capable of preventing violence and responding to it when it occurs.

The Committee on the Rights of the Child explains that effective protection requires more than isolated interventions. States need coordinated systems that address prevention, identification, reporting, referral, investigation, treatment, and follow-up.

Read General Comment No. 13 →

CRC · Article 34

Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse

Article 34 requires states to protect children from all forms of sexual exploitation and sexual abuse. This includes taking appropriate national, bilateral, and multilateral measures to prevent children from being unlawfully subjected to sexual activity or exploitation.

The provision demonstrates that child protection extends beyond violence occurring in traditional settings and includes forms of exploitation that may involve organized, commercial, or technology-related abuse.

Read Article 34 in the Convention →

CRC · Articles 37 and 39

Dignity, Recovery, and Reintegration

The Convention protects children from torture and cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment and recognizes the right of children affected by abuse, neglect, exploitation, or other serious harm to receive measures that promote physical and psychological recovery and social reintegration.

Protection therefore includes what happens after violence as well as what authorities do to prevent it. Effective systems should help children recover safely and restore their dignity.

04 · African Human-Rights Law

The African Legal Framework

African Children's Charter · Article 16

Protection Against Child Abuse and Torture

Article 16 of the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child requires states to take specific legislative, administrative, social, and educational measures to protect children from all forms of torture, inhuman or degrading treatment, physical or mental injury or abuse, neglect, maltreatment, and sexual abuse while in their care.

The Charter also requires effective procedures for prevention, identification, reporting, referral, investigation, treatment, and follow-up of child abuse and neglect.

Read the African Children's Charter →

African Children's Charter · Article 4

Best Interests of the Child

Article 4 establishes the best interests of the child as a primary consideration in all actions concerning children undertaken by public or private welfare institutions, courts, administrative authorities, or legislative bodies.

This principle is important in abuse cases because decisions about investigation, protection, placement, services, and justice should be guided by the child's rights and welfare rather than solely by the interests of adults or institutions.

African Human-Rights Approach

Protection Requires Systems

The African framework treats child protection as a system-wide responsibility. Effective protection can require laws, institutions, trained professionals, reporting mechanisms, social services, justice systems, and preventive measures working together.

This is particularly important where abuse occurs in environments that children depend on for care, education, housing, or other essential services.

05 · Featured Case

A. v. the United Kingdom

European Court of Human Rights · 1998

A landmark case on the protection of children from ill-treatment

A. v. the United Kingdom concerned a nine-year-old child who had been subjected to repeated and severe physical punishment by his stepfather. The domestic legal system at the time permitted a defence of "reasonable punishment," and the stepfather was acquitted.

The case was subsequently brought before the European Court of Human Rights. The Court examined whether the legal framework had provided the child with adequate protection against treatment prohibited by Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

The Court held that Article 3 had been violated. It emphasized that children and other vulnerable people are entitled to protection through effective measures against serious forms of ill-treatment and found that the law had not adequately protected the child in this case. 3

Why is it relevant?

A. v. the United Kingdom is directly relevant to child-protection law because it demonstrates that the existence of a legal system does not automatically mean that children are effectively protected.

The case shows how a state's legal framework can itself become part of the human-rights question: if domestic law permits serious ill-treatment without adequate protection or deterrence, the state may fail to meet its obligation to protect the child.

It therefore fits the central legal question of this page — the difference between formal legal rules and meaningful protection in practice.

06 · Key Legal Principles

What Duties Can States Have?

Prevention

States should take reasonable legislative, administrative, social, and educational measures to prevent violence against children before it occurs.

Protection

Authorities should provide effective protection when children are known or reasonably suspected to be at risk of abuse, neglect, exploitation, or other violence.

Identification and Reporting

Child-protection systems should make it possible for abuse to be identified, reported, referred, and assessed through safe and accessible procedures.

Investigation

Allegations of serious abuse should be investigated promptly, effectively, and in a manner that respects the child's dignity and rights.

Best Interests

The child's best interests should be a primary consideration when authorities make decisions about protection, care, investigation, justice, and support.

Recovery and Remedies

Children affected by violence should have access to appropriate health, psychological, social, legal, and other support needed for recovery and reintegration.

07 · Understanding the Problem

Why Can Violence Against Children Persist?

Violence against children can continue even where laws and child-protection systems exist. Human-rights analysis therefore requires examination of the conditions that make abuse difficult to identify, report, investigate, or prevent.

Under-reporting
Children may depend on the adults around them for food, housing, education, or care, which can make reporting abuse difficult. Fear, shame, stigma, and lack of trust can also discourage disclosure.
Dependence on caregivers
Abuse can be especially difficult to address when the person responsible for the harm is also responsible for the child's daily care or has significant authority over the child.
Institutional inaction
Child-protection systems may fail when reports are ignored, referrals are delayed, investigations are incomplete, or institutions do not communicate effectively with one another.
Social acceptance
Some harmful disciplinary practices or forms of violence may be treated as normal within families, communities, or institutions, making it harder to recognize them as rights violations.
Conflict and displacement
Conflict, displacement, and humanitarian emergencies can weaken family and community support systems, disrupt education and social services, and increase children's vulnerability.
Limited access to justice
Distance, cost, lack of information, inaccessible procedures, and limited child-friendly services can prevent children from obtaining effective protection and remedies.
08 · Ethiopia

Violence and Abuse Against Children in Ethiopia

Ethiopia's constitutional framework gives children specific rights to protection from exploitation, harmful work, and cruel or inhuman treatment, while also requiring the best interests of the child to guide decisions affecting children.

UNICEF Ethiopia identifies violence, exploitation, abuse, and harmful practices as continuing child-protection concerns. It reports that children remain vulnerable to violence in different settings and that Ethiopia's child-protection system continues to focus on prevention, referral, social welfare, justice, and protection in emergencies. 4

Article 36 · Rights of Children

Article 36 of the FDRE Constitution recognizes a number of specific rights of children. It protects children from exploitative practices and from work that may be hazardous or harmful to their education, health, or well-being.

Most directly for this topic, Article 36(1)(e) gives children the right to be free from corporal punishment or cruel and inhuman treatment in schools and other institutions responsible for their care. 5

Best Interests of the Child

Article 36(2) provides that in all actions concerning children undertaken by public and private welfare institutions, courts, administrative authorities, or legislative bodies, the primary consideration shall be the best interests of the child.

Article 18 · Protection from Cruel Treatment

Article 18 of the Constitution protects everyone from cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment or punishment and prohibits slavery and trafficking in human beings. These protections are relevant when examining serious forms of violence, exploitation, and abuse involving children.

Child Protection Systems

Ethiopia's child-protection approach includes prevention of violence, referral pathways, child-friendly justice, social welfare services, and protection in emergencies. UNICEF Ethiopia's 2025 monitoring guidance identifies weak data systems, limited resources, fragmented frameworks, and institutional capacity as continuing challenges for monitoring and responding to violence against children. 6

Online Child Sexual Exploitation and Abuse

Child protection increasingly includes digital environments. UNICEF Ethiopia reported in 2025 that the Ethiopian government and partners had launched national guidance and training materials concerning online child sexual exploitation and abuse, reflecting the need for child-protection systems to respond to risks created by digital technologies as well as physical settings. 7

Why the Ethiopian context matters

Ethiopia illustrates the central challenge of child-protection law: strong rights on paper must be supported by institutions that can identify risks, respond to reports, investigate abuse, provide services, and protect children in practice.

A human-rights analysis must therefore ask not only whether Ethiopian law recognizes children's rights, but whether children can actually obtain protection when those rights are threatened or violated.

09 · Legal Analysis

Law on Paper vs. Protection in Practice

Violence against children demonstrates why human-rights law examines both formal legal rules and the systems that make those rules effective.

Legal promise Practical question
Children have a right to protection from violence and abuse. Can children and trusted adults report suspected abuse safely and have those reports taken seriously?
Authorities should take preventive measures. Are families, schools, communities, and institutions equipped to identify and reduce risks before abuse occurs?
Serious allegations should be investigated. Are investigations timely, effective, child-sensitive, and capable of establishing what happened?
Children have a right to recovery and support. Do children affected by abuse receive appropriate medical, psychological, social, and legal assistance?
The child's best interests must be a primary consideration. Do institutions place the child's safety, dignity, welfare, and rights at the centre of their decisions?
Children are entitled to effective legal protection. Are laws, institutions, and remedies strong enough to protect children even when abuse occurs in places they depend on for care?
A legal prohibition is only meaningful when a child can actually rely on it. Human-rights law asks whether protection is accessible, enforceable, child-sensitive, and effective in practice.
10 · Research Question

Think Like a Human-Rights Researcher

When a state knows that children are at risk of violence or abuse, when does a failure to protect them become a violation of human rights?

A strong legal analysis should ask three things: What rights are being affected? What duties does the state have? Was the protection effective, accessible, and focused on the child's best interests?
