CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Are Criminal Defences?

Criminal defences are legal arguments or grounds that may prevent a person from being held criminally responsible, reduce their criminal responsibility, or justify or excuse conduct that would otherwise constitute an offence.

They operate within the criminal justice system by addressing whether the accused should be found legally responsible for the alleged offence.

Why should the accused not be held criminally liable for the conduct alleged?

A defence may challenge an element of the prosecution's case, provide a legal justification for the conduct, excuse the accused because of particular circumstances, or otherwise prevent criminal liability.

The availability and requirements of criminal defences depend on the offence and the applicable legal system.

CARD 2 · PROSECUTION

Criminal Defences and the Prosecution's Case

The prosecution generally has the burden of proving the elements of the offence.

An accused person does not necessarily have to prove their innocence simply because a defence is raised.

A defence may operate by showing that:

  • An essential element of the offence was not established.
  • The accused possessed a legally recognized justification or excuse.
  • A particular circumstance prevents criminal responsibility.
  • The prosecution has failed to satisfy the required burden of proof.
The effect of a defence depends on its legal classification and the applicable rules of evidence and criminal procedure.
CARD 3 · ELEMENTS

Elements of an Offence and Defences

A criminal offence generally contains legal elements that the prosecution must prove.

  • Actus reus.
  • Mens rea.
  • Causation.
  • Particular circumstances specified by legislation.

A defence may challenge one of these elements rather than operating as an independent justification.

Not every reason for avoiding conviction is technically an affirmative defence. Sometimes the prosecution simply fails to prove an essential element.
CARD 4 · FAILURE OF THE CASE

Failure of the Prosecution's Case

One of the most important ways an accused may avoid conviction is by showing that the prosecution has failed to prove an essential element of the offence.

For example, if an offence requires proof that the accused acted intentionally and the evidence does not establish the required intention, the prosecution may fail to prove the offence.

The prosecution must prove every element required by the offence according to the applicable standard of proof.

In such circumstances, the accused may not need to rely on a separate affirmative defence.

CARD 5 · CLASSIFICATION

Classification of Criminal Defences

Criminal defences may be divided into different categories.

Justifications

The conduct is treated as legally acceptable or justified in the circumstances.

Excuses

The conduct may have been wrongful, but the accused is not held criminally responsible because of particular circumstances affecting responsibility.

Common Examples

  • Self-defence.
  • Duress.
  • Necessity.
  • Insanity or mental disorder defences.
  • Automatism or involuntary conduct.
  • Mistake of fact.
  • Infancy or age-based responsibility rules.
  • Consent in limited circumstances.
  • Entrapment in jurisdictions where it is recognized.
CARD 6 · JUSTIFICATION & EXCUSE

Justification and Excuse

Justification Excuse
The conduct is treated as legally justified. The conduct remains wrongful but the accused is excused.
Focuses on the circumstances surrounding the conduct. Focuses more on the accused's responsibility or ability to choose.
Self-defence is a common example. Duress and certain mental incapacity defences may be examples.
The law treats the conduct as permissible in the circumstances. The law recognizes a reason not to hold the accused responsible.
CARD 7 · SELF-DEFENCE

Self-Defence

Self-defence is one of the most widely recognized criminal defences.

It generally applies where a person uses force to protect themselves or another person from unlawful violence or an imminent threat.

The law may require the accused to show that:

  • They genuinely believed force was necessary.
  • The belief was legally sufficient under the relevant test.
  • The amount of force used was reasonable or proportionate in the circumstances.
The exact requirements of self-defence differ significantly between jurisdictions.
CARD 8 · DEFENCE OF ANOTHER

Defence of Another Person

A person may sometimes use force to protect another individual from unlawful violence.

This is generally treated as an extension of the principles of self-defence.

Whether the defence applies may depend on:

  • The nature of the threat.
  • Whether force was necessary.
  • Whether the response was reasonable.
  • The applicable legal rules.
CARD 9 · PROPORTIONALITY

Proportionality and Self-Defence

A major issue in self-defence cases is whether the force used was proportionate or reasonable in the circumstances.

A person may not generally use unlimited force simply because they were threatened.

Courts May Consider

  • The seriousness of the threat.
  • Whether the danger was immediate.
  • The amount of force used.
  • The circumstances known to the accused at the time.
The precise legal test varies between jurisdictions.
CARD 10 · NECESSITY

Necessity

Necessity is a defence based on the idea that the accused committed an otherwise unlawful act in order to avoid a greater harm.

For example, a person may argue that they broke a legal rule because doing so was necessary to prevent a more serious and immediate danger.

Depending on the jurisdiction, the defence may require:

  • A serious and imminent danger.
  • A genuine belief that the unlawful conduct was necessary.
  • No reasonable alternative.
  • A proportionate response.
Necessity is not available in every situation, and courts generally apply strict requirements.
CARD 11 · DURESS

Duress

Duress generally applies where a person commits an offence because they are subjected to threats of serious harm.

The accused may argue that the threats were so serious that they should not be held criminally responsible for the conduct.

Common requirements may involve:

  • A threat of serious harm.
  • Pressure directed at the accused.
  • A sufficiently immediate or compelling threat.
  • A causal connection between the threat and the criminal conduct.
Some offences may be excluded from the defence.
CARD 12 · DURESS BY THREATS

Duress by Threats

Duress by threats occurs where another person threatens the accused with serious harm unless the accused commits an offence.

The accused may argue that they acted because of the threat rather than from free choice.

The legal requirements concerning the seriousness, immediacy, and reasonableness of the response depend on the jurisdiction.

CARD 13 · NECESSITY & DURESS

Necessity and Duress Compared

Necessity Duress
Conduct is carried out to avoid a serious danger. Conduct is carried out because of threats or coercion.
The danger may arise from circumstances. The pressure commonly comes from another person.
The accused may argue that the unlawful act prevented a greater harm. The accused may argue that threats deprived them of genuine freedom of choice.
Although the concepts can overlap, they are legally distinct in many systems.
CARD 14 · MISTAKE

Mistake of Fact and Mistake of Law

Mistake of Fact

A mistake of fact occurs where the accused honestly misunderstands a factual circumstance.

A mistake may affect criminal liability where the offence requires the accused to possess a particular mental state.

For example, if an offence requires knowledge that an object belongs to another person, a genuine mistake about ownership may prevent the prosecution from proving the required knowledge.

Mistake of Law

A mistake of law occurs when a person is mistaken about what the law prohibits or requires.

The general principle in many legal systems is:

Ignorance of the law is not normally a defence.
CARD 15 · INSANITY

Insanity and Mental Disorder Defences

Some legal systems recognize a defence based on severe mental disorder or legal insanity.

The defence does not simply ask whether the accused had a mental illness.

Instead, the law generally asks whether the accused's mental condition affected their ability to understand the nature of their conduct, understand its wrongfulness, or control their conduct according to a legally defined test.

The requirements vary greatly between jurisdictions.
CARD 16 · AUTOMATISM

Automatism and Involuntary Conduct

Automatism concerns situations where the accused's physical actions occurred without the required conscious control.

Criminal law generally requires voluntary conduct.

If the relevant conduct was truly involuntary, the prosecution may fail to establish the necessary actus reus.

Examples

  • Unconscious physical activity.
  • Certain medical conditions.
  • Sleep-related conduct.
  • External physical influences.
The exact legal treatment depends on the jurisdiction and the cause of the involuntary state.
CARD 17 · INTOXICATION

Intoxication

Intoxication can sometimes affect criminal responsibility, particularly where a specific mental state is required.

The legal treatment may depend upon:

  • Whether the intoxication was voluntary or involuntary.
  • The type of offence.
  • Whether the offence requires intention or another specific mental state.
  • Whether intoxication prevented formation of the required mens rea.
Voluntary intoxication does not automatically provide a complete defence.
CARD 18 · AGE & CONSENT

Infancy, Consent and Entrapment

Infancy

The law may recognize that children below a specified age should not be held criminally responsible in the same way as adults.

Legal systems establish minimum ages of criminal responsibility and may have special rules for young offenders.

Consent

Consent may operate as a defence in some offences where the law recognizes that the consent of the relevant person affects criminal responsibility.

Entrapment

Entrapment concerns situations in which law enforcement officers or their agents induce a person to commit an offence that they would not otherwise have committed.

The legal treatment of consent and entrapment differs substantially between jurisdictions.
CARD 19 · STRICT LIABILITY

Defences and Strict Liability

Strict liability can affect the availability of certain defences.

Where an offence does not require mens rea for a particular element, a person may not be able to rely on a mistake simply by showing that they lacked knowledge of that element.

However, a statutory defence may still be available.

For example, legislation may provide a defence based on reasonable precautions or due diligence.

The precise effect depends on whether the offence is strict liability, absolute liability, or an ordinary mens rea offence.

CARD 20 · PROPERTY & AUTHORITY

Defence of Property and Public Authority

Defence of Property

Some legal systems permit limited force to protect property from unlawful interference.

Courts may consider:

  • Whether the property was under immediate threat.
  • Whether the accused reasonably believed intervention was necessary.
  • The amount of force used.
  • Whether less harmful alternatives were available.

Public Authority

Conduct that would otherwise constitute an offence may be lawful because it was carried out under lawful authority.

  • Lawful arrest.
  • Execution of a court order.
  • Use of force by authorized officials within legal limits.
  • Conduct specifically authorized by legislation.
CARD 21 · ALIBI & IDENTITY

Alibi and Mistaken Identity

Alibi

An alibi is evidence that the accused was somewhere else when the offence occurred.

It directly challenges the prosecution's identification of the accused as the person who committed the offence.

Mistaken Identity

The accused may argue that someone else committed the offence.

Evidence may include:

  • Eyewitness testimony.
  • Identification procedures.
  • CCTV evidence.
  • Digital records.
  • Physical evidence.
  • Timing and location evidence.
An alibi and mistaken identity are generally arguments that the prosecution has failed to prove who committed the offence, rather than justifications or excuses.
CARD 22 · BURDEN OF PROOF

Defences and Burden of Proof

The burden of proof concerning a defence depends upon the legal system and the type of defence.

In many criminal proceedings, the prosecution initially bears the burden of proving the elements of the offence beyond reasonable doubt.

Some defences require only that the accused raise sufficient evidence to put the issue before the court.

In other situations, legislation may place a particular evidential or persuasive burden on the accused.

The exact allocation of burdens must be determined according to the applicable law.
CARD 23 · STANDARD OF PROOF

Defences and Standard of Proof

The prosecution generally must prove the elements of the offence according to the applicable criminal standard of proof.

In many common law systems this is beyond reasonable doubt.

The standard applicable to a defence may be different depending on the legal system.

Some jurisdictions require the prosecution to disprove a defence once it has been properly raised, while others may place a defined burden on the accused.
CARD 24 · PRESUMPTION OF INNOCENCE

Defences and Presumption of Innocence

Criminal defences are closely connected with the presumption of innocence.

The accused begins the criminal process presumed innocent unless and until the prosecution proves the offence according to law.

A defence does not automatically shift the entire burden of proving guilt onto the accused.

The prosecution must still prove the elements of the offence that are legally required.

CARD 25 · MENS REA & ACTUS REUS

Defences and Mens Rea / Actus Reus

Mens Rea

  • Mistake of fact may prevent proof of knowledge.
  • Intoxication may affect the ability to form a specific intention in some circumstances.
  • Involuntary conduct may undermine the requirement of voluntary action.

Actus Reus

If the accused's conduct was involuntary, the prosecution may fail to establish the required physical element.

Some situations described as "defences" actually operate by preventing the prosecution from proving an essential element of the offence.
CARD 26 · EVIDENCE

Defence and Evidence

The effectiveness of a defence often depends on the available evidence.

  • Witness testimony.
  • Documents.
  • Video recordings.
  • Medical evidence.
  • Digital communications.
  • Physical evidence.
  • Expert evidence.
  • The accused's own statements.

The court assesses evidence according to the rules of evidence and the applicable burden and standard of proof.

Expert Evidence

Some defences may require specialized evidence, such as medical evidence in mental disorder cases or scientific evidence concerning intoxication.

Expert evidence does not automatically determine the outcome. The court remains responsible for deciding the legal issues.
CARD 27 · JUSTIFICATION & RESPONSIBILITY

Defences and Criminal Responsibility

The existence of a defence does not necessarily mean that the accused's conduct was desirable or harmless.

A defence determines whether the accused should be held legally responsible under circumstances recognized by law.

Self-defence, for example, does not mean that violence is generally lawful. It means the law may recognize an exception where force was necessary and legally justified in response to an unlawful threat.
CARD 28 · COMPLETE & PARTIAL

Complete and Partial Defences

Complete Defence

A complete defence may result in an acquittal where all legal requirements are satisfied.

Partial Defence

A partial defence does not necessarily result in complete acquittal but may reduce the seriousness of criminal liability.

One important example in some legal systems is a partial defence to murder that may reduce liability from murder to manslaughter.

The availability and effect of partial defences vary by jurisdiction.

CARD 29 · MURDER

Defences to Murder

Murder is among the most serious criminal offences, and legal systems often recognize specific defences or partial defences.

Potential Issues

  • Self-defence.
  • Insanity or mental disorder.
  • Diminished responsibility in jurisdictions that recognize it.
  • Loss of control in certain legal systems.
  • Duress in jurisdictions where it can apply.
  • Mistake where relevant to the required mental element.
The exact defences and their requirements depend on the jurisdiction.
CARD 30 · PROCEDURE

Defences and Criminal Procedure

Defences must be raised and determined according to procedural rules.

This may involve:

  • Notice requirements.
  • Disclosure of evidence.
  • Admissibility of evidence.
  • Expert reports.
  • Jury directions.
  • Judicial rulings.
  • Burden and standard of proof.
A legally recognized defence may still raise procedural questions about how and when the issue should be presented.
CARD 31 · TRIAL

Defences at Trial

At trial, the defence may seek to:

  • Challenge the prosecution's evidence.
  • Establish that an element of the offence has not been proved.
  • Raise a recognized affirmative defence.
  • Introduce evidence supporting the defence.
  • Challenge the reliability of witnesses.
  • Argue that the prosecution has not met the required standard of proof.
The judge determines questions of law, while the fact-finding function may belong to a jury or judge depending on the legal system.
CARD 32 · APPEAL

Defences on Appeal

An appellate court may consider whether a trial court correctly applied the law relating to a defence.

An appeal may concern:

  • Whether the correct legal test was used.
  • Whether the judge correctly directed the jury.
  • Whether evidence relevant to the defence was improperly excluded.
  • Whether the burden of proof was correctly allocated.
  • Whether the defence was legally available.
  • Whether the evidence was sufficient to support the defence.
CARD 33 · STATUTORY INTERPRETATION

Defences and Statutory Interpretation

Many criminal defences are defined by legislation.

Courts may therefore need to interpret statutory wording to determine:

  • The elements of the defence.
  • The required mental state.
  • Whether the defence is complete or partial.
  • Who bears the burden of proof.
  • What evidence is sufficient to raise the defence.
The wording of the statute and relevant case law are central to understanding a particular defence.
CARD 34 · DIFFERENT SYSTEMS

Defences in Different Legal Systems

Criminal defences vary significantly across jurisdictions.

A defence recognized in one legal system may be limited, modified, or unavailable in another.

Differences may concern:

  • The name of the defence.
  • The elements that must be established.
  • Whether it is a justification or excuse.
  • The burden of proof.
  • The standard of proof.
  • Whether the defence is complete or partial.
Criminal defences must always be studied within the applicable legal system.
CARD 35 · WHY IT MATTERS

Why Criminal Defences Matter

Criminal defences help:

  • Protect individuals from unjust criminal punishment.
  • Distinguish wrongful conduct from legally justified conduct.
  • Recognize circumstances that reduce or eliminate criminal responsibility.
  • Ensure that the prosecution proves the elements of an offence.
  • Protect the presumption of innocence.
  • Recognize limits on state punishment.
  • Promote fairness in criminal proceedings.
Defences are an essential part of the balance between individual responsibility and the power of the criminal justice system.
CARD 36 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

Criminal defences are legal rules that may prevent, reduce, or otherwise limit criminal responsibility.

They may operate in different ways.

  • Justification — the conduct is treated as legally justified, such as lawful self-defence.
  • Excuse — the conduct remains wrongful but the accused is excused, such as certain forms of duress or mental incapacity.
  • Failure of the prosecution's case — the prosecution has failed to prove an essential element of the offence.

Common Criminal Defences

  • Self-defence — reasonable force used to protect against unlawful harm.
  • Necessity — unlawful conduct carried out to prevent a greater or serious danger.
  • Duress — criminal conduct carried out under legally sufficient coercion or threats.
  • Mistake of fact — a factual mistake that may prevent the required mental element from being established.
  • Insanity or mental disorder — a legally recognized mental condition that may prevent criminal responsibility under the applicable test.
  • Automatism — involuntary conduct occurring without the required conscious control.
  • Alibi — evidence showing that the accused was elsewhere when the offence occurred.
Criminal Defences = Legal reasons why the accused should not be held criminally responsible for the alleged offence.

Understanding criminal defences is essential because criminal law is not concerned only with whether prohibited conduct occurred. It also considers whether the law recognizes circumstances that justify, excuse, or otherwise prevent criminal responsibility.
