CARD 1 · DEFINITION

What Is Attempt?

Attempt is a criminal law concept concerning conduct directed toward committing an offence that is not completed.

It answers the question:

What happens when a person intends to commit a crime and takes steps toward committing it, but the crime is not completed?

A person may sometimes be criminally responsible for an attempt even though the intended offence was unsuccessful.

Attempt is therefore an important part of the law of inchoate offences.

An inchoate offence is an offence that is incomplete because the intended criminal result or full offence has not been achieved.

CARD 2 · PURPOSE

Purpose of the Law on Attempt

The law of attempt allows criminal justice systems to respond to sufficiently serious conduct directed toward committing an offence even when the final offence does not occur.

It helps:

  • Prevent serious crimes from being completed.
  • Hold individuals accountable for substantial steps toward criminal conduct.
  • Distinguish preparation from criminal attempts.
  • Recognize the significance of criminal intent combined with conduct.
  • Protect society from conduct that presents a serious risk of an offence being completed.
The law generally does not treat every thought, plan, or preparatory act as an attempt.
CARD 3 · INCHOATE OFFENCE

Attempt as an Inchoate Offence

Attempt is classified as an inchoate offence because the intended crime is incomplete.

Other Inchoate Offences

  • Conspiracy.
  • Encouraging or assisting another person to commit an offence.
  • Other offences established by particular legislation.

The precise categories differ between legal systems.

An attempt focuses on the accused's own conduct toward completing the intended offence.
CARD 4 · ELEMENTS

Elements of Attempt

Although the precise legal test varies, an attempt commonly requires two main components:

  1. The required mental element — the accused intended to commit the relevant offence.
  2. An act beyond mere preparation — the accused took conduct sufficiently close to committing the offence.

Both elements are important.

A person may have a strong intention to commit a crime but still not have committed an attempt if they have not taken the legally required step.
CARD 5 · MENS REA

Attempt and Mens Rea

Mens rea is particularly important in attempt cases.

The prosecution generally needs to establish the mental state required for the attempted offence.

For example, a person who accidentally performs an act that appears suspicious does not necessarily commit an attempt.

The law generally distinguishes between intentional conduct directed toward committing a crime and accidental or merely careless conduct.

The precise mental requirements depend on the offence and jurisdiction.

CARD 6 · ACTUS REUS

The Actus Reus of Attempt

Attempt also requires an appropriate physical element.

The accused must generally have moved beyond merely thinking about or preparing for the offence.

The law therefore asks whether the conduct was sufficiently close to the commission of the intended crime.

Different Legal Approaches

  • An act beyond mere preparation.
  • A substantial step.
  • An overt act toward completion of the offence.

The exact test depends on the applicable law.

CARD 7 · PREPARATION

Preparation vs Attempt

One of the most important issues in attempt cases is distinguishing preparation from attempt.

Preparation Attempt
Getting ready to commit an offence. Conduct that has progressed further toward committing the offence.
May involve obtaining equipment or making plans. Generally involves conduct sufficiently beyond preparation.
Usually does not by itself establish attempt. May satisfy the physical element of attempt where the legal test is met.
CARD 8 · WHY PREPARATION IS NOT ENOUGH

Why Preparation Is Generally Not Enough

Criminal law generally avoids punishing people simply because they have criminal thoughts or have begun making plans.

A person may change their mind before carrying out the plan.

For this reason, the law generally requires conduct demonstrating a sufficiently serious commitment to the intended offence before criminal attempt liability arises.

Thinking about a crime → Preparing for a crime → Attempting to commit a crime

The precise boundary between these stages varies between jurisdictions.

CARD 9 · PROXIMITY

Proximity to the Completed Offence

Some legal systems consider how close the accused's conduct was to completing the offence.

The closer the conduct is to completion, the more likely it may satisfy the legal test for attempt.

For example, entering the final stage of carrying out an offence may clearly go beyond preparation.

Courts do not necessarily require the accused to have reached the final possible moment before the law recognizes an attempt.
CARD 10 · FAILURE

Attempt and Failure

A person may commit an attempt even if the intended offence ultimately fails.

The intended offence may fail because:

  • The intended victim leaves.
  • The police intervene.
  • The equipment does not work.
  • Another person stops the conduct.
  • The intended result does not occur.
Failure does not automatically eliminate criminal responsibility for attempt.

The question is whether the legal requirements for attempt were satisfied.

CARD 11 · IMPOSSIBILITY

Impossible Attempts

An impossible attempt occurs when a person intends to commit an offence but cannot actually complete it because of some circumstance unknown to them.

Depending on the legal system, an impossible attempt may still result in criminal liability where the accused has demonstrated the required criminal intent and taken sufficient steps toward committing the offence.

Factual Impossibility

Factual impossibility occurs when external circumstances make completion of the offence impossible.

Legal Impossibility

Legal impossibility concerns circumstances in which the intended conduct, if completed as the accused planned it, would not actually amount to the intended crime under the law.

The treatment of impossibility varies between jurisdictions.
CARD 12 · ABANDONMENT

Abandonment

Abandonment occurs when a person voluntarily decides not to continue with an attempted offence.

The effect of abandonment varies between legal systems.

In some systems, voluntarily abandoning the offence may be relevant to criminal liability in particular circumstances.

Once the legal requirements for attempt have already been satisfied, later abandonment may not automatically erase liability for the attempt.

The precise rule depends on the applicable law.

CARD 13 · ATTEMPT & COMPLETION

Attempt and Completion

Attempt Completed Offence
The offence is not fully completed. All required elements of the offence are established.
Requires conduct directed toward the offence. Requires completion of the prohibited conduct and any required result.
Generally requires intent to commit the offence. Mental element depends on the completed offence.
Often carries a specific statutory penalty. Penalty is normally based on the completed offence.

The exact relationship between attempt and the completed offence varies between jurisdictions.

CARD 14 · TYPES OF OFFENCES

Attempt and Different Types of Offences

Result Crimes

Attempt can apply to offences that require a particular result.

A person may intend to cause a prohibited result but fail to cause it.

Conduct Crimes

Attempt may also apply to offences focused on prohibited conduct.

Where the underlying offence does not require a separate result, the court may focus more directly on whether the accused intended to carry out the prohibited conduct and took sufficient steps toward doing so.

The legal analysis depends heavily on the wording of the particular offence.
CARD 15 · CAUSATION

Attempt and Causation

Attempt can differ from completed result offences because the intended result may never occur.

In a completed result offence, the prosecution may need to establish causation between the accused's conduct and the prohibited result.

In an attempt case, the intended result may be absent. The prosecution instead focuses on whether the accused intended to commit the offence and performed the legally required conduct toward its completion.

Attempt and Actus Reus

The physical element of attempt concerns conduct that goes beyond mere preparation and is sufficiently connected to the intended offence.

Actus reus of attempt = conduct sufficiently beyond preparation toward commission of the offence.

CARD 16 · BURDEN & EVIDENCE

Attempt and Evidence

The prosecution generally carries the burden of proving the elements of an attempted offence.

This may include proving:

  • The accused's required intention.
  • The relevant conduct.
  • That the conduct went beyond mere preparation.
  • Any other elements required by law.

Evidence

Evidence may include:

  • Witness testimony.
  • Physical evidence.
  • Documents.
  • Digital communications.
  • Recordings.
  • Statements made by the accused.
  • Evidence concerning actions taken before the alleged attempt.

Because intention is a mental state, courts may infer it from conduct and surrounding circumstances.

CARD 17 · EXAMPLES

Examples of Attempt

Example: Attempt

Imagine that a person intends to commit theft. They identify the property they want to take and begin carrying out their plan by physically attempting to take it.

Before they can leave with the property, another person intervenes and prevents the offence from being completed.

Depending on the applicable law, the person may be guilty of attempted theft because:

  1. They intended to commit the offence.
  2. They took conduct toward committing it.
  3. Their conduct went beyond mere preparation.
  4. The completed theft did not occur.

Example: Preparation

If a person merely thinks about committing a crime and buys equipment that could potentially be used to carry it out, but has not begun carrying out the planned offence, the conduct may remain preparation rather than attempt.

CARD 18 · WITHDRAWAL

Attempt and Withdrawal

A person may decide to stop before completing the intended offence.

Whether withdrawal prevents criminal liability depends on when the person stops and the rules of the relevant legal system.

If the conduct has already crossed the legal threshold for attempt, simply stopping later may not automatically eliminate liability.

The circumstances of the withdrawal may nevertheless be relevant under some legal systems.

CARD 19 · PUNISHMENT

Punishment for Attempt

The punishment for attempt varies between legal systems.

Some jurisdictions establish a specific statutory penalty for attempted offences.

In some systems, an attempt may receive a lower maximum penalty than the completed offence.

Other systems may provide different sentencing rules depending on the offence.

The seriousness of the intended offence is often relevant to determining the potential punishment.
CARD 20 · DIFFERENT SYSTEMS

Attempt in Different Legal Systems

The law of attempt differs between jurisdictions.

Common law systems commonly require:

  • An intention to commit the relevant offence.
  • Conduct going beyond mere preparation.

Other legal systems may use different concepts or statutory definitions.

Some jurisdictions establish detailed rules concerning:

  • Impossibility.
  • Abandonment.
  • Punishment for attempts.
  • Attempted offences involving different mental states.
The precise requirements and consequences of attempt must be determined according to the relevant legal system.
CARD 21 · APPEAL & PUBLIC PROTECTION

Attempt on Appeal and Public Protection

Attempt on Appeal

An appellate court may consider whether the trial court correctly applied the legal test for attempt.

An appeal may concern:

  • Whether the accused intended to commit the offence.
  • Whether the conduct went beyond preparation.
  • Whether the correct legal test was applied.
  • Whether impossibility was treated correctly.
  • Whether the evidence was sufficient to establish attempt.

Public Protection

The law of attempt can serve a preventive function by allowing the legal system to intervene when a person has progressed significantly toward committing a serious offence.

The law must also avoid criminalizing mere thoughts or ordinary preparation.
CARD 22 · WHY IT MATTERS

Why Attempt Matters

The law of attempt helps:

  • Distinguish criminal thoughts from criminal conduct.
  • Prevent serious offences before completion.
  • Hold people responsible for substantial steps toward criminal conduct.
  • Establish liability when the intended crime fails.
  • Provide a legal boundary between preparation and criminal action.
  • Protect society while maintaining limits on criminal liability.
Attempt is important because a person does not always need to complete a crime before the law recognizes serious criminal conduct.
CARD 23 · KEY TAKEAWAY

Key Takeaway

Attempt is an inchoate criminal offence involving conduct directed toward committing an offence that is not completed.

A simple way to remember the concept is:

Attempt = Criminal intention + conduct beyond mere preparation

The law generally requires more than thoughts or plans. The accused must usually have taken a sufficiently significant step toward committing the intended offence.

An attempt may still be criminal even when the intended offence fails because the victim escapes, another person intervenes, the circumstances prevent completion, or another factor stops the crime from being completed.

Understanding attempt is essential because criminal law can impose responsibility not only for completed offences but, in appropriate circumstances, for sufficiently serious conduct directed toward committing them.
